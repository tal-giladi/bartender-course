# 07.4 · Opening and serving wine and sparkling wine

Opening a bottle at the bar or at a table is a small performance: the guest watches your hands. Done well, it is quiet, quick and clean. Done badly, a cork crumbles into the wine, a sparkling cork flies across the room, or the first glass foams over the guest's hand. This lesson teaches the standard method for still wine with a waiter's friend corkscrew, the safe method for sparkling wine that lesson 06.3 promised, serving temperatures, how to chill a bottle fast, and how to store wine before and after opening. The hands-on part trains the hand movements with a cheap bottle and a timer.

## Why it matters

A table of four orders a bottle of Israeli Cabernet and a bottle of cava to start. If you open the cava with a pop, pointed toward the next table, you can injure someone's eye. If you pour it fast into warm flutes, it foams over. If the Cabernet's cork breaks and you push it into the bottle, the table waits while you strain the wine. A calm, standard routine avoids all of this and makes the guests trust your recommendations.

## Core ideas

### Tools

- **Waiter's friend** (פותחן מלצרים, *potchan meltzarim*): a folding corkscrew with a small blade for the foil, a spiral called the **worm**, and a hinged lever that rests on the bottle's lip. Two-step (double-hinged) models lift the cork in two stages and are easier for beginners (professional judgment).
- **Service cloth**: a clean napkin for wiping the bottle neck and catching drips.
- **Ice bucket**: ice and water together, not ice alone. The Court of Master Sommeliers Europe asks for enough iced water to cover the bottle up to the neck; water touches the whole bottle and chills it far faster than ice cubes alone (professional judgment, consistent with [lesson 03.5](../module-03/lesson-05.md)).
- **Sparkling-wine stopper**: a clip-on stopper made to hold the pressure of an opened sparkling bottle.

### Serving temperatures

The Court of Master Sommeliers Europe (CMS) gives these ranges in its Service Standards; the WSET groups the same wines as "well chilled", "chilled", "lightly chilled" and "room temperature" and warns that below about 6 °C white, rosé and sparkling wines lose flavour, and above about 18 °C full reds taste dull.

| Wine | CMS range | WSET group |
|---|---|---|
| Sparkling (non-vintage cooler, vintage warmer) | 6–12 °C | well chilled |
| Sweet white | 6–8 °C | well chilled |
| Light white and rosé | 7–10 °C | chilled |
| Full, oaked white | 10–13 °C | lightly chilled |
| Light red (e.g. Pinot Noir) | 10–13 °C | room temperature or lightly chilled |
| Medium and full red | 15–18 °C | room temperature |

"Room temperature" means a cool room. In an Israeli summer a bar can be 25 °C or more, so reds often need 15–20 minutes in a fridge or a few minutes in an ice bucket before serving (professional judgment). The Comité Champagne (the trade body of Champagne) suggests 20–30 minutes in an ice bucket to chill a bottle of Champagne to about 8–10 °C.

### Still wine: the standard sequence

From the CMS Europe Service Standards, simplified for a bar:

1. Present the bottle to the host, label towards them, and say the producer, wine and vintage.
2. Cut the foil with the blade **below the second lip** of the bottle neck, so wine never runs over the foil. Remove the foil and put it in your pocket, not on the table.
3. Wipe the top of the cork.
4. Insert the worm just off centre and turn it in, stopping before it goes through the bottom of the cork.
5. Lever the cork out gently, **without a pop**.
6. Wipe the bottle mouth; offer the cork to the host on a small plate or coaster (house style).
7. Pour about 50 ml for the host to taste. Wait for approval.
8. Serve the other guests first and the host last. Fill a glass about one third to one half, depending on its size.
9. At the end of each pour, twist the bottle slightly as you lift it and wipe the neck to stop drips.

A **screwcap** bottle is presented the same way; the cap is removed and the neck wiped, and the cap is not presented.

### Sparkling wine: open it safely

> [!WARNING]
> A sparkling-wine bottle is under high pressure. The Union des Maisons de Champagne (UMC) says a cork can leave the bottle at about 13 m/s, fast enough to hit an eye one metre away in under a tenth of a second. Keep your thumb on the cork from the moment the cage is loosened, point the bottle away from every person (towards the ceiling or a wall), never shake the bottle and never open it against your body. No sabrage (opening with a sword or knife) in this course.

Combined from CMS Europe, the Comité Champagne and the UMC:

1. Make sure the bottle is cold enough and has not been shaken. Wipe it dry.
2. Present it to the host.
3. Remove the foil (cut below the cage, or use the tear strip).
4. **Thumb on the cork.** Holding the neck with one hand and pressing the cork with that thumb, untwist the wire loop with the other hand (CMS: about six turns). Loosen the cage but leave it on the cork as extra grip, or remove cage and foil together if the cork is tight (UMC).
5. Hold the bottle at **30–45°**, pointing away from people.
6. **Turn the bottle, not the cork.** Grip the cork firmly and slowly turn the bottle by its base. Let the pressure push the cork out gently into your hand with a quiet sigh, not a pop.
7. Keep the bottle at 45° for a few seconds while the gas settles. Wipe the neck.
8. Pour in two stages: a little first, let the foam settle, then fill slowly to about two thirds of the glass (Comité Champagne). Pour down the side of a tilted glass if it foams a lot (lesson 06.3).

The Comité Champagne recommends a tall tulip-shaped glass; flutes and coupes are acceptable. A Prosecco or cava is opened the same way.

### Storing wine

From CMS Europe and the WSET blog:

- **Unopened**: cool (about 10–15 °C), steady temperature, dark, no vibration. **Corked bottles lie on their side** so the cork stays moist; screwcap bottles can stand. Never store wine on a shelf above the coffee machine or in a sunny window (professional judgment, following the heat and light rules).
- **Opened still wine**: recork or use a vacuum stopper or an inert-gas system and keep it in the fridge, reds too. Most bars use an opened bottle of still wine within about 2–3 days (professional judgment; quality falls day by day).
- **Opened sparkling**: a proper sparkling-wine stopper and a cool place; the Comité Champagne says it will not keep long and calls the spoon-in-the-neck trick a myth. Use it the same or next day (professional judgment).
- Label the opening date on every bottle opened for by-the-glass service (professional judgment; Module 8 covers labelling and shelf life).

### Faulty bottles

The WSET Level 2 course names three common faults (descriptions are professional judgment):

- **Cork taint** ("corked"): smells musty, like wet cardboard or a damp cellar; fruit is muted.
- **Heat damage**: tastes cooked or jammy; the cork may have pushed up or leaked.
- **Failure of the closure** (air got in): tastes flat, brownish, sherry-like.

If a guest says a wine is faulty, smell it, apologise and replace it; do not argue (professional judgment).

> [!NOTE]
> Steps and temperatures from the Court of Master Sommeliers Europe Service Standards (version 24.1, 2024), the Comité Champagne and UMC pages, and WSET (blog 2022; Level 2 specification April 2026), all checked 2026-10-17. Opened-bottle life in days is professional judgment. House style (cork presentation, pour size, glass) varies by bar and comes first.

## Step by step

### Chilling a bottle fast

1. Fill an ice bucket (or a large pot) half with ice, then top with cold water to the bottle's neck.
2. Optional: add a handful of salt to the water (professional judgment: salt lowers the melting point, so the water gets colder).
3. Put the bottle in up to the neck. Turn it now and then.
4. Check with a thermometer in the water or by feel after 15–20 minutes; sparkling wine usually needs 20–30.

### Recovering from a broken cork

1. If the cork breaks halfway, insert the worm again at an angle into the part still in the neck and lever gently.
2. If it crumbles into the wine, pour the wine through a clean fine strainer or a coffee filter into a clean decanter or carafe, out of the guest's view if possible (professional judgment).
3. Never push the cork into the bottle at the table.

## Hands-on

Three short drills: the waiter's friend sequence on a cheap corked bottle, the sparkling-wine hand positions on a bottle that cannot launch a cork, and a chilling race between a fridge and an ice bucket.

> [!CAUTION]
> The foil blade on a waiter's friend is sharp: cut away from your other hand. Work over a counter with a cloth under the bottle; glass bottles break if dropped. If you use a real sparkling bottle, follow every step of the warning above and point it at a wall, away from people and windows.

### What you need

- A waiter's friend corkscrew (sold in most Israeli kitchen and wine shops), a service cloth, a small plate.
- One cheap bottle with a natural or plastic cork: wine, or a non-alcoholic wine or grape juice with a cork if you prefer. The contents can be used for cooking or for lesson 07.5.
- For the sparkling drill: a 1.5 litre plastic soda bottle (no cork, so no risk), and optionally a bottle of sparkling grape juice or non-alcoholic sparkling wine with a mushroom cork and wire cage.
- An ice bucket or large pot, ice, water, a thermometer, and two identical bottles of water at room temperature.
- The checklist [`labs/module-07/wine-service-checklist.md`](../../labs/module-07/wine-service-checklist.md) and [`templates/practice-log.md`](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: no corked bottle? Practise steps 1–3 (present, cut foil, wipe) on any wine bottle with a foil capsule, and the worm-and-lever movement on a wine cork pushed half into an empty bottle neck.

### Time

50 minutes. Start the 20-minute chilling race first; do the still-wine drill (20 minutes) and the sparkling drill (10 minutes) while it runs; then 20 minutes to review your film and record results.

### Steps

1. **Chilling race (start first).** Put one water bottle in the fridge and one in the ice bucket with ice and water up to the neck. Note the time.
2. **Still wine.** Film yourself doing the nine-step sequence on the corked bottle, talking to an imaginary host: present, cut foil below the second lip, wipe, insert the worm off centre, lever without a pop, wipe, present the cork, pour 50 ml "for the host" into a glass, then pour for the "table" in the right order. Time it. Repeat three times if you can re-use the bottle (push the cork back in by hand for practice runs).
3. Watch the film with the checklist. Mark each step done well, done badly or missed.
4. **Sparkling hand positions.** Using the plastic soda bottle, practise 10 times: hold at 45° pointing at a wall, thumb on the cap as if it were a cork, untwist an imaginary cage with the other hand (six turns), then turn the bottle (not the cap) to open it slowly so it only hisses.
5. Optional: open the sparkling grape juice or NA sparkling bottle for real with the full method. Pour into two glasses: one quickly in one go, one in two stages. Compare the foam.
6. **Chilling race result.** After 20 minutes, measure the temperature of the water in both bottles (pour a little into a glass and measure) and record it.

### What you should see

A first still-wine opening usually takes 90–120 seconds and has one slip (foil cut too high or a small pop); by the third run most learners are under about 60 seconds without a sound (professional judgment). In the chilling race the ice-bucket bottle is usually around 6–10 °C after 20 minutes while the fridge bottle is still well above that, often around 15–18 °C. The one-go sparkling pour foams to the rim; the two-stage pour does not.

### What this practice cannot teach

A plastic soda bottle has no cork and far less pressure than sparkling wine, and water cannot reproduce how a real sparkling wine foams; only careful, supervised openings of real bottles teach the feel of the cork and the sigh of the gas.

### Completion checklist

- [ ] I completed the still-wine sequence on film and scored it with the checklist.
- [ ] My third run was quiet, under about 60 seconds, with no foil drips.
- [ ] I practised the sparkling hand positions 10 times with the bottle always pointing away from people.
- [ ] I recorded the chilling race and can explain why ice and water beat a fridge.

## Common mistakes

- **Popping sparkling wine.** It wastes wine and bubbles and can injure. Fix: thumb on the cork, turn the bottle, release slowly.
- **Turning the cork instead of the bottle.** The cork can snap or shoot. Fix: hold the cork still, turn the bottle by its base.
- **Ice without water in the bucket.** The bottle chills slowly. Fix: ice and water to the neck.
- **Serving reds at a hot bar's "room temperature".** They taste soupy and alcoholic. Fix: 15–18 °C; a few minutes in the fridge or bucket.
- **Cutting the foil above the lip.** Wine runs over the foil and drips. Fix: cut below the second lip.
- **Arguing with a guest about a corked wine.** Fix: smell it, apologise, replace.

## Key takeaways

- Still wine: present, cut foil below the lip, worm in off centre, lever out quietly, host tastes about 50 ml, host served last.
- Sparkling: thumb on the cork, bottle at 30–45° away from people, turn the bottle, release with a sigh, pour in two stages.
- Serve sparkling and sweet wines well chilled, light whites chilled, full whites and light reds lightly chilled, full reds at 15–18 °C.
- Ice plus water chills a bottle in 20–30 minutes; a fridge takes much longer.
- Store corked bottles on their side, cool and dark; stopper, refrigerate and date opened bottles.
