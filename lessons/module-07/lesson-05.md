# 07.5 · Alcohol-free beer, wine and drinks

Guests who do not drink alcohol, or not tonight, are part of every shift: drivers, pregnant guests, people on medication, people who do not drink for religious or health reasons, and people who just want a lighter evening. They deserve a real choice, served with the same care as a cocktail. This lesson explains what the numbers on alcohol-free labels mean, how alcohol-free beer and wine are made and why they taste different, and how to build a short zero-proof (alcohol-free) list from drinks you already know. You finish by comparing alcohol-free products and designing four zero-proof serves.

## Why it matters

Three guests sit together. One is pregnant and asks for "an alcohol-free beer". One is driving and wants "something that feels like a drink". One wants "a beer, but light". If you hand the pregnant guest a beer labelled "alcohol-free 0.5%" without saying the number, you have made her decision for her. If you hand the driver a glass of cola in a plastic cup, he feels like an afterthought and will not come back. Getting this right is accuracy and hospitality at once.

## Core ideas

### What the label words mean

There is no single worldwide rule. Lesson 02.8 showed the US rule; the table adds the UK guidance and the common international pattern.

| Label | Typical meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "0.0%" | no measurable alcohol; residual alcohol under about 0.05% | Foods review 2026 (industry practice) |
| "Alcohol-free" (UK) | alcohol removed, no more than 0.05% ABV | UK low-alcohol descriptors guidance (2018) |
| "De-alcoholised" (UK) | alcohol removed, no more than 0.5% ABV | UK guidance (2018) |
| "Non-alcoholic" (US) | less than 0.5% ABV, with that statement on the label | TTB |
| "Low alcohol" (UK) | 1.2% ABV or less | UK guidance (2018) |
| "Alcohol-free" in many other markets | below 0.5% ABV | Foods review 2026 |

In Israel, labels say נטול אלכוהול (*natul alkohol*, "alcohol-free") or ללא אלכוהול (*lelo alkohol*, "without alcohol") and usually show a number; always read the number, not the word ([lesson 02.8](../module-02/lesson-08.md)).

### Who needs what

- **Pregnant guest**: the Israeli Ministry of Health says to avoid alcohol completely during pregnancy because no amount is known to be safe. Offer a 0.0% product, and if you only have a product with a small number such as 0.3% or 0.5%, say the exact number and let the guest decide.
- **Driver**: same rule as pregnancy for the offer (0.0% first, exact number stated); responsible service is covered in Module 9.
- **Guest avoiding alcohol for religious, health or personal reasons**: 0.0% first; state the number if it is not 0.0.
- **Guest who wants "something light"**: a low-alcohol beer, a spritz, a highball, or an alcohol-free option, offered without judgment.
- **Possible minor**: no alcohol of any kind, and alcohol-free "beer" is a separate question that depends on the law and house policy; Module 9 covers minors and ID.

Never secretly swap an alcoholic drink for an alcohol-free one, or the other way round (professional judgment). The guest must know what is in the glass.

### How alcohol-free beer is made

A 2026 review in the journal *Foods* describes two routes:

- **Biological**: stop alcohol forming. Brewers mash so that less fermentable sugar is made, stop fermentation early, ferment cold, or use special yeasts that cannot ferment maltose. These beers often taste **"worty"** (like the sweet grain liquid before fermentation) and sweeter, with fewer fruity notes.
- **Physical**: brew a normal beer, then remove the alcohol by gentle heat under vacuum (vacuum distillation) or by filtering through membranes. These beers often lose aroma, can feel thinner and taste more acidic.

Many producers blend or add back aroma to improve the taste (same review). For you this means: alcohol-free beers differ a lot, so taste the ones on your list (they are perfect for staff tastings, since they have no alcohol), and describe them honestly: "light and crisp", "a bit sweeter than a lager".

### How alcohol-free wine is made

Almost always, a normal wine is made first and the alcohol is removed by membrane filtering or by vacuum or spinning-cone distillation (Foods review of wine dealcoholisation, 2021). The process can change colour and lose some aroma, and alcohol itself gives wine body and warmth, so alcohol-free wines often taste lighter and sometimes sweeter, because some producers add grape juice or sugar to restore body (professional judgment; check the label). Alcohol-free sparkling wine is usually the most convincing, because bubbles and acidity carry it (professional judgment).

### Alcohol-free spirits and zero-proof drinks

**Alcohol-free spirits** are botanical distillates or flavoured waters sold as alternatives to gin, rum, aperitifs and so on. Read the label: some contain traces of alcohol, sugar, or allergens such as nuts (professional judgment). A **zero-proof drink** (or "mocktail") is a mixed drink with no alcohol. You already have many: every recipe card in Modules 5 and 6 has an alcohol-free analogue, and [lesson 06.6](../module-06/lesson-06.md) showed how to adapt a drink without alcohol. Three rules from those lessons make a zero-proof drink feel complete (professional judgment):

1. **Replace what alcohol gave**: body (a little more syrup, tea, aquafaba foam), warmth or bite (ginger, pepper, chilli), bitterness (tonic, tea, grapefruit, alcohol-free bitter aperitif).
2. **Keep balance**: sweet and sour in the same ratio logic as the original sour ([lesson 03.3](../module-03/lesson-03.md)).
3. **Serve it properly**: the right glass, ice and garnish, named on the menu like any other drink.

Hebrew you may hear: alcohol-free נטול אלכוהול (*natul alkohol*); "zero" זירו (*ziro*), as in "a zero beer" (professional judgment). In Israel "בירה שחורה" (*bira shchora*, "black beer") on a supermarket shelf is usually a sweet malt soft drink, not a dark beer; read the label for alcohol content (professional judgment).

> [!NOTE]
> Label definitions from GOV.UK (guidance published 2018; the UK consulted later on changing the alcohol-free threshold, so check for updates) and TTB; production methods from reviews in *Foods* (2026 on beer, 2021 on wine); pregnancy advice from the Israeli Ministry of Health. All checked 2026-10-17. Products, brands and laws in this area change fast.

## Step by step

### Answering "do you have something without alcohol?"

1. Smile and say yes; never make the guest explain why.
2. Ask one question: "Beer-like, wine-like, or a mixed drink?"
3. Name two options with their exact ABV: "We have a 0.0 lager and an alcohol-free sparkling wine at 0.0; our NA IPA is 0.4%."
4. If the guest is pregnant, driving or avoiding alcohol completely, recommend the 0.0% option first and say the number of any other.
5. Serve it in the proper glass with the same garnish and care as an alcoholic drink, and ring it up correctly.

### Turning a cocktail analogue into a menu drink

1. Pick a recipe card from Module 5 or 6 and read its alcohol-free analogue.
2. Add one element that replaces what alcohol gave (body, bite or bitterness).
3. Taste and balance with the sour-sweet ratio.
4. Give it a name, a glass, ice and a garnish, and write one honest menu line.

## Hands-on

Compare three alcohol-free products by label and taste, then design four zero-proof serves for a menu.

> [!WARNING]
> Some "alcohol-free" products contain up to about 0.5% alcohol. If you are pregnant, driving or avoiding alcohol completely, choose only 0.0% products for the tasting.

### What you need

- Three alcohol-free products from a supermarket, for example a 0.0% beer, an alcohol-free beer with a small number (0.3–0.5%) if you can find one, and an alcohol-free wine or sparkling wine. About 30–60 ILS for all three (professional judgment; prices vary).
- Two glasses per product, a notebook, and [`templates/tasting-notes.md`](../../templates/tasting-notes.md).
- The worksheet [`labs/module-07/zero-proof-worksheet.md`](../../labs/module-07/zero-proof-worksheet.md) and your recipe cards from Modules 5 and 6.
- Ingredients for one of your four zero-proof serves: citrus, sugar syrup, soda or tonic, tea, ginger, ice.
- Low-cost alternative: if you cannot buy the products, do the label part from an Israeli online supermarket and skip the tasting.

### Time

50 minutes: 20 for the comparison, 30 to design four serves and build one.

### Steps

1. For each product, record: name, type, exact ABV, Hebrew wording (נטול אלכוהול or ללא אלכוהול), ingredients, sugar per 100 ml, allergen notes, and whether the production method is stated.
2. Pour each into the glass it would get on a bar (lesson 07.1 for beer, 07.4 for wine) at the right temperature.
3. Taste and note: body (thin to full), sweetness, acidity, bitterness, aroma, and anything "missing" compared with the alcoholic version.
4. Write one honest guest description for each, including the ABV.
5. On the worksheet, design four zero-proof serves from four different families (lesson 06.1): for each, the base analogue, the element that replaces alcohol, the glass, ice and garnish, and a one-line menu description.
6. Build one of the four and taste it. Adjust once and note what you changed.

### What you should see

Products made by stopping fermentation usually taste sweeter and more "grainy"; those with alcohol removed usually smell less and feel thinner; alcohol-free sparkling wine usually feels closest to the original. Sugar per 100 ml often differs a lot between products, which matters for guests watching sugar. Your zero-proof serves feel more like "a drink" when they have bitterness or bite, not only sweetness.

### What this practice cannot teach

Three products cannot show the whole range on a real bar's list, and how guests actually react to alcohol-free options, including the quiet relief of not being questioned, is learned only in real service.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying "zero" when the label says 0.5%.** The guest cannot make an informed choice. Fix: always say the exact number.
- **Serving alcohol-free drinks in a "lesser" glass.** It singles the guest out. Fix: same glassware, ice and garnish as the alcoholic version.
- **Making zero-proof drinks too sweet.** Without alcohol, sugar dominates. Fix: add bitterness, acidity or spice.
- **Asking the guest why they are not drinking.** It is personal. Fix: just offer the options.
- **Treating alcohol-free beer as safe for minors without checking the law and house policy.** Fix: follow Module 9 and your manager.

## Key takeaways

- Read the number, not the word: 0.0%, under 0.05%, under 0.5% and 1.2% are different promises.
- For a pregnant guest, a driver or anyone avoiding alcohol completely, recommend 0.0% first and state the ABV of anything else.
- Alcohol-free beer is made by stopping fermentation (sweeter, "worty") or removing alcohol (less aroma, thinner).
- Alcohol-free wine is made by removing alcohol from wine; it is lighter, and sparkling versions usually work best.
- Zero-proof drinks need something to replace alcohol's body, bite or bitterness, and deserve full presentation.
