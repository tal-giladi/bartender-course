# 07.1 · Beer styles, temperatures and glassware

In lesson 02.8 you learned to tell an ale from a lager and to name five common beer styles. This lesson turns that knowledge into service. You will learn how cold each style should be, which glass suits it, and why a glass that looks clean can still kill the foam on a beer. These three things decide whether a beer tastes the way the brewer meant it to, and they are the first things a bar manager notices when you serve a beer. You finish by auditing the glasses and the fridge in your own kitchen.

## Why it matters

A guest orders a dark, strong Belgian-style ale. You pull it from the coldest corner of the fridge and pour it into the same tall glass you use for the house lager. The guest says it tastes of nothing. Ten minutes later the same thing happens with an IPA in a glass that still has a film of dishwasher rinse aid: the foam collapses in seconds and big bubbles cling to the sides. Nothing was wrong with either beer. Temperature, glass and cleanliness are the bartender's part of the job.

## Core ideas

### Temperature: colder is not always better

Cold numbs the tongue and keeps carbon dioxide (CO2) in the beer. That suits a light, crisp lager, where refreshment is the point. It hides the flavour of a dark or strong beer, where the malt, fruit and roast are the point. The American Homebrewers Association publishes a temperature table (based on the beer writer Randy Mosher's book *Tasting Beer*) with these rules of thumb:

- Serve all beers between about 3 °C and 13 °C (38–55 °F).
- Lagers colder than ales.
- Stronger beers warmer than weaker ones.
- Darker beers warmer than lighter ones.
- Chill a few degrees below the target, because beer warms in the glass and in the hand.

| Style (lesson 02.8) | Serving temperature | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mainstream light lager | 1–4 °C | refreshment first; little flavour to hide |
| Pale lager, pilsner | 3–7 °C | crisp and clean |
| Wheat beer | 4–10 °C | fruit and spice aromas need a little warmth |
| Pale ale, IPA | 7–10 °C | hop aroma opens up |
| Nitro stout | 4–7 °C | smooth, light body |
| Stout, porter | 7–13 °C | roast and chocolate need warmth |
| Belgian dubbel, strong lager | 10–13 °C | strong and malty |
| Sour beer (lambic) | 4–10 °C | sharp, fruity; served fairly cold |

Temperatures are converted from the °F table and rounded. The Brewers Association, which writes for draught systems, gives a narrower working range: beer stored at about 1–3 °C and served at about 3–7 °C. Most bars keep one cold room or one fridge, so in practice you serve most beers at fridge temperature and let darker or stronger ones stand a few minutes, or keep them in the warmer part of the fridge (professional judgment).

### Glassware: what a beer glass does

A glass does four jobs, according to the Brewers Association's glassware chart:

- **Aroma**: a glass that narrows at the top traps aroma; an open top lets it go.
- **Foam support**: a curve inward near the top holds the foam (head) dense and tall.
- **Temperature**: a stem or handle keeps the warm hand off the beer; a big bowl (a snifter) invites the hand to warm it.
- **Size**: stronger beers go in smaller glasses. It helps the drinker drink responsibly and it matches the price.

| Glass | Shape | Typical styles (BA chart and Cicerone) |
|---|---|---|
| Nonic pint | straight glass with a bulge near the top (the bulge stops chips and helps stacking) | British ales, pale ales, stouts, IPAs |
| Shaker pint | plain straight-sided tumbler | American lagers and ales; the all-purpose bar glass |
| Pilsner glass (pilsner flute) | tall, slim, narrow at the base | pilsner, pale lager, wheat beers |
| Weizen (vase) | tall, curved, wide at the top | German wheat beer with a big head |
| Willi Becher | straight, slightly narrowing at the top | lagers and German styles generally |
| Tulip | stem, round bowl, flared lip | Belgian styles, strong ales, sours, IPAs |
| Snifter | stem, wide bowl, narrow top, small size | very strong ales, imperial stouts |

You do not need every glass. A bar with a pint, a tall slim glass and one stemmed tulip-type glass can serve almost any beer well (professional judgment). In Israel, beer is often sold as a שליש (*shlish*, a third of a litre, 330 ml) or a חצי (*chatzi*, half a litre, 500 ml), as you saw in [lesson 01.5](../module-01/lesson-05.md) (professional judgment).

### "Beer-clean" glass

A glass can be spotless to the eye and still ruin a beer. Fat, lipstick, detergent residue and rinse aid all break foam. The Brewers Association calls a glass that is free of these residues **beer-clean** and gives three tests:

1. **Sheeting test**: dip the glass in water and lift it. On a beer-clean glass the water covers the inside in an even sheet. On a dirty glass it breaks into droplets.
2. **Salt test**: sprinkle salt inside a wet glass. It sticks evenly to a clean surface and leaves bare patches where there is a greasy film.
3. **Lacing test**: as a beer is drunk, foam sticks to a clean glass in rings ("lacing"). On a dirty glass it sticks randomly or not at all.

Signs of a dirty glass during service: bubbles clinging to the inside walls, a head that collapses fast, no lacing (professional judgment, consistent with the Brewers Association's tests).

The same manual says to air-dry beer glasses (towels leave lint and odours), store them upside down in a wire basket, never on a rubber mat or towel, and never freeze them: a frozen glass forms a thin sheet of ice that makes beer foam over. Chilled glasses should go into the chiller dry.

## Step by step

### Choosing temperature and glass for an order

1. Read the style and ABV (label, tap list or menu).
2. Light lager or pilsner: straight from the fridge, tall slim glass or pint.
3. Wheat beer: from the fridge, tall weizen-type glass with room for a big head.
4. Pale ale or IPA: from the fridge, pint or tulip.
5. Stout, porter, strong or Belgian-style ale: from the warmer part of the fridge or let it stand a few minutes; smaller glass for strong beer (tulip or snifter).
6. Check the glass: no chips, no film, no smell. Never use a glass straight from a freezer.

### Checking a glass for beer-clean (sheeting test)

1. Fill a clean sink or bowl with cold water.
2. Dip the glass and lift it out.
3. Look at the inside: an even sheet is clean; droplets or dry patches mean a film.
4. If it fails, wash again with a non-oily detergent and a brush, rinse well in cold water, and let it air-dry upside down.

## Hands-on

A glass and fridge audit: test four glasses at home for beer-clean, match each to beer styles, and check what temperature your fridge actually holds.

> [!CAUTION]
> Handle glasses over a towel or the sink, and stop if a glass is chipped or cracked. Do not put glasses in the freezer for this exercise.

### What you need

- Four different glasses (for example a tall tumbler, a short tumbler, a wine glass, a mug or any beer glass you own).
- Table salt, a bowl of water, washing-up liquid and a soft brush or sponge.
- A kitchen or cooking thermometer (a cheap probe thermometer is enough), and a glass of water left in the fridge for at least 2 hours.
- The worksheet [`labs/module-07/glass-audit.md`](../../labs/module-07/glass-audit.md) and [`templates/practice-log.md`](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: no thermometer? Skip step 5 and note the fridge's dial setting; the glass tests need only water and salt.

### Time

40 minutes.

### Steps

1. Take the four glasses as they are in your cupboard (do not rewash them yet). Do the sheeting test on each and note the result on the worksheet.
2. Do the salt test on each wet glass. Photograph the inside if you can.
3. Wash any glass that failed with washing-up liquid and a brush, rinse thoroughly in cold running water and let it air-dry upside down for 15 minutes. Repeat both tests.
4. On the worksheet, match each glass to the beer styles it could serve best, using the glassware table. Write one reason (aroma, foam, size, temperature) for each.
5. Measure the temperature of the glass of water that has been in the fridge: one reading from the top shelf, one from the door or bottom drawer. Compare with the temperature table: which shelf would you use for a pilsner and which for a stout?
6. Optional, small and only if you are 18+ and not driving: pour the same beer into two of your glasses, one that passed and one that failed before washing, and compare the head after one minute. Water and soda will not show this, because they do not hold a beer-style foam.

### What you should see

Glasses straight from a dishwasher that uses rinse aid often fail the sheeting test: water beads up. A hand-washed and well-rinsed glass usually passes. Salt sticks in an even coat on a clean glass and leaves clear patches on a filmed one. Most home fridges read about 3–7 °C on the shelves and warmer in the door, which is fine for lagers; the door is the better spot for a stout.

### What this practice cannot teach

Water and soda do not foam like beer, so you cannot see how a dirty glass kills a real head without real beer; a working bar also cleans glasses in a glasswasher with bar detergent, which you will meet in Module 8.

## Common mistakes

- **Serving every beer ice-cold.** Strong and dark beers taste flat and thin. Fix: keep them in the warmer part of the fridge or let them stand a few minutes.
- **Using frozen glasses.** Ice forms inside, the beer foams over and is watered down. Fix: chill glasses dry in a fridge or chiller, never in the freezer.
- **Drying beer glasses with a towel.** Lint and odours stay on the glass. Fix: air-dry upside down on a wire rack.
- **Pouring a 10% beer into a 500 ml glass.** The guest gets far more alcohol than they expect. Fix: smaller glass, and say the strength when serving.
- **Ignoring clinging bubbles.** Bubbles stuck to the inside wall mean a film. Fix: replace the glass and rewash the batch.

## Key takeaways

- Serve beer between about 3 and 13 °C: lagers colder, strong and dark beers warmer.
- A glass traps aroma, holds foam, keeps the hand off the beer and sets the portion size.
- Stronger beers go in smaller glasses.
- Beer-clean means free of grease, detergent and rinse aid; check with the sheeting and salt tests.
- Air-dry beer glasses and never freeze them.
