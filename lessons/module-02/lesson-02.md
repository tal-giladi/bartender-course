# 02.2 · Vodka and gin

Vodka and gin are the two clear spirits you will pour most often in mixed drinks. They start the same way, as a very pure, high-strength spirit, and then go in opposite directions: vodka aims to taste of as little as possible, while gin is flavoured, above all with juniper. This lesson explains what the law says each one is, the gin styles you will see on a back bar, how to store and substitute them, and trains your nose on the botanicals that make gin taste like gin.

## Why it matters

A guest orders "a gin and tonic, but not the floral one, the classic one." Another asks for a Martini with vodka "because gin tastes like a Christmas tree." A third asks if the pink gin on the shelf is "the same thing with colour." Your answers decide whether the guest gets what they expect. Knowing the styles also matters when stock runs out mid-shift: replacing one London dry gin with another is fine; replacing it with a sweet, fruity gin changes the drink.

## Core ideas

### Vodka: a neutral spirit

Vodka (וודקה, *vodka*) is made from ethyl alcohol of agricultural origin (grain, potato, grape, sugar beet or other crops) that is distilled and sometimes filtered through charcoal to strip out the taste of the raw material. EU rules set a minimum of 37.5% ABV. US rules describe vodka as neutral spirits distilled at 95% ABV or more and bottled at no less than 40%; they allow a tiny amount of sugar (up to 2 g per litre) and citric acid (up to 1 g per litre).

"Neutral" does not mean all vodkas are identical. A wheat vodka tends to feel soft and slightly sweet, a rye vodka a little spicy, and a potato vodka rounder and oilier (professional judgment). In a highball with juice or soda the difference is small; in a Martini or served chilled and straight it shows.

**Flavoured vodka** has added flavourings (citrus, vanilla, berries). It is a different product: a lemon vodka in a Cosmopolitan changes the recipe.

### Gin: juniper first

Gin (ג'ין, *jin*) is a spirit whose main flavour must be juniper (ערער, *ar'ar*), the small blue-black berry of a conifer. Other plant flavourings are called **botanicals**. EU law defines three levels, from loosest to strictest, all with a minimum of 37.5% ABV:

| EU category | How it is made | What the label tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Gin | Neutral spirit flavoured with juniper and other flavourings; may be made by simply mixing in flavours | Juniper must dominate; quality varies widely |
| Distilled gin | Neutral spirit re-distilled with juniper and other botanicals; more flavour may be added after distillation | The core flavour came from a still |
| London gin (London dry gin) | Re-distilled with natural botanicals only; nothing added after distillation except water and at most 0.1 g sugar per litre; no colouring | Dry, clean, consistent; a style, not a place — it can be made anywhere |

### Gin styles on a back bar

- **London dry**: dry, juniper-led, with citrus and spice. The default "classic" gin and tonic and Martini gin.
- **Contemporary or "new western" gins**: juniper still present but other botanicals (cucumber, flowers, local herbs) lead. Many craft and local gins fit here (professional judgment).
- **Old Tom**: a slightly sweetened historical style used in some old recipes.
- **Genever (jenever)**: the Dutch and Belgian ancestor of gin, made with a malted-grain spirit, so it tastes bready and fuller. Not a direct swap for London dry.
- **Navy strength**: gin bottled at about 57% ABV (industry convention, professional judgment). Same style, much stronger: reduce the pour or warn the guest.
- **Flavoured and pink gins**: gin with fruit flavours, sugar and often colour. Many are sweet. Treat them as their own product.

### The main botanicals

| Botanical | Hebrew (transliteration) | Smell |

| Juniper berry | ערער (*ar'ar*) | Pine, resin, a little pepper |
| Coriander seed | זרעי כוסברה (*zar'ei kusbara*) | Citrus, warm spice (not like the leaves) |
| Citrus peel (lemon, orange) | קליפת לימון / תפוז (*klipat limon / tapuz*) | Fresh, bright oil |

### Israeli gins and vodkas

Israel has its own distilleries; for example, the Milk & Honey (M&H) distillery in Jaffa makes a gin as well as whisky. Imported international brands dominate most back bars you will see (professional judgment, not a market statistic). Whatever the origin, read the label for the category ("London dry gin", "distilled gin", "gin") and the ABV.

### Storage and serving

Store vodka and gin upright, closed, at room temperature and away from sunlight. They do not spoil once opened, though flavoured versions slowly lose freshness (professional judgment). Many bars keep one vodka in the freezer for chilled shots and Martinis: at 37.5–40% it will not freeze in a normal freezer.

## Step by step

### How to handle a gin or vodka order with no brand named

1. Use the house (well) product unless the guest names a brand.
2. If the guest describes a taste ("the classic one", "something lighter"), translate it into a style: classic = London dry; lighter or floral = contemporary.
3. If a requested brand is out, offer the closest style and say so: "We're out of that one; I can do it with another London dry. Is that okay?"
4. Never swap a flavoured or sweet gin into a recipe without telling the guest.

### How to pick a substitute

1. Same category and style first (London dry → another London dry).
2. Then the same style family (London dry → distilled gin with a juniper-forward profile).
3. Watch the ABV. A navy-strength gin at about 57% in place of a 40% gin makes the drink much stronger; reduce the pour or warn the guest.
4. Vodka for gin (or the reverse) is a different drink. Only do it when the guest asks.

## Hands-on

You will train your nose on gin botanicals and build an alcohol-free "gin tea" that shows how a distiller balances them.

> [!CAUTION]
> You use a paring knife or peeler for the citrus peel and hot water for the infusions. Peel away from your hand on a cutting board, and let the kettle water cool for a minute before pouring.

### What you need

- Juniper berries (about 10; sold at spice shops as ערער), coriander seed (1 tsp), cardamom pods (3), a cinnamon stick, black peppercorns, a lemon and an orange.
- A peeler or paring knife, a cutting board, a mortar or a spoon and a cup for crushing, six small cups or glasses, a kettle, a tea strainer.
- The [tasting notes template](../../templates/tasting-notes.md).
- Low-cost alternative: no juniper? Use a sprig of rosemary for the resinous note and write "rosemary instead of juniper" in your notes. Any three spices from your kitchen will still train the method.

### Time

40 minutes: 10 to prepare, 15 for the dry smelling round, 15 for the infusions and blend.

### Steps

1. Peel a strip of lemon and a strip of orange, without the white pith.
2. Lightly crush the juniper, coriander and cardamom so they release their oils. Put each botanical in its own cup.
3. Dry round: smell each cup for 3–5 seconds, with a short break between cups. Write one or two words per botanical (for example "pine", "citrus spice").
4. Blind round: ask someone to swap the cups around (or close your eyes and shuffle them). Identify each one by smell only. Record your score out of six.
5. Infusions: put each botanical in 100 ml of hot (not boiling) water for 5 minutes, then strain. Smell again; note what changed.
6. Blend: make your "London dry" in a glass: 50 ml juniper tea, 20 ml coriander tea, 15 ml citrus tea, 10 ml cardamom tea, 5 ml cinnamon tea. Smell it. Then make a "contemporary" blend with only 15 ml juniper and more citrus and cardamom. Compare.
7. Fill in one tasting-notes sheet for each blend. Optional: taste the cooled blends; they are alcohol-free.

### What you should see

Juniper and citrus are the easiest to spot; coriander is often confused with citrus because it has citrus notes. Warm water makes the aromas stronger and changes them: cardamom becomes more medicinal, juniper more piney. In the blends, the juniper-heavy glass smells "like gin" even without alcohol, while the contemporary blend smells fresher and less like a pine tree. A blind score of 4 or more out of 6 is a good start; repeat in a week.

Juniper: pine, resin, slight pepper. Coriander seed: lemony, warm, slightly floral. Lemon peel: sharp, zesty. Orange peel: sweeter, rounder. Cardamom: eucalyptus, sweet spice. Cinnamon: warm, sweet, woody.

### What this practice cannot teach

Water infusions show aroma, but not how alcohol carries and sharpens it or how a real gin feels in the mouth; comparing two real gins (optional, tiny sips, spit) is the only way to learn that.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking "London dry" means made in London.** It is a production method. Fix: read it as "re-distilled, nothing added after, unsweetened".
- **Treating all gins as interchangeable.** A sweet pink gin in a Negroni changes it completely. Fix: substitute within the same style and tell the guest about any change.
- **Calling vodka "tasteless" to a guest.** It is neutral, not identical. Fix: describe it as clean or smooth and offer the house or a named brand.
- **Forgetting the ABV on navy-strength gin.** It can be about 57%. Fix: check the label before using it as a substitute.
- **Smelling too long.** After a few seconds your nose tires. Fix: short sniffs, breaks between samples, smell your own sleeve or plain water to reset.

## Key takeaways

- Vodka is a neutral spirit, at least 37.5% ABV in the EU and 40% in the US; flavoured vodka is a different product.
- Gin must taste mainly of juniper; EU law separates gin, distilled gin and London gin.
- London dry gin is a method: re-distilled botanicals, nothing added afterwards except water and a trace of sugar.
- Substitute within the same style, watch the ABV, and tell the guest when the drink will change.
- Juniper, coriander seed and citrus peel are the core botanicals; you can learn them with your nose and a kettle.
