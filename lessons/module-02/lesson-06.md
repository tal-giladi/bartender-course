# 02.6 · Liqueurs and bitters

Liqueurs are sweetened, flavoured spirits: orange, coffee, cherry, herbs, nuts, cream. Bitters are the opposite: intensely flavoured, unsweetened, used a few drops at a time. Between them they provide much of the sweetness, colour and complexity in classic cocktails. This lesson explains what makes a liqueur a liqueur, the families you will find on a back bar, what bitters are and why their alcohol matters, and shows by weighing that sugar makes liquids heavier, which is the secret of layered drinks.

## Why it matters

A recipe calls for "orange liqueur" and the shelf has three different bottles. A guest asks for a "virgin" Old Fashioned and you reach for the aromatic bitters. Another guest with a nut allergy asks what is in the almond-flavoured liqueur. Each of these is a real decision: the wrong orange liqueur changes the balance, the bitters add a little alcohol to an "alcohol-free" drink, and a guessed answer about allergens can hurt someone.

## Core ideas

### What a liqueur is

A liqueur (ליקר, *liker*) is a spirit that has been sweetened and flavoured with fruit, herbs, spices, nuts, coffee, cream or other ingredients. EU law sets two minimums: at least 15% ABV and at least 100 g of sugar per litre (lower for a few types, such as gentian-flavoured at 80 g and cherry-spirit-based at 70 g). Products labelled "crème de" (for example crème de cassis, blackcurrant) are a sweeter subgroup. Most liqueurs sit between about 15% and 40% ABV; read the label.

### Liqueur families

| Family | Examples you will meet | Typical use | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Orange | Triple sec, orange curaçao, cognac-based orange liqueur | Margarita, Cosmopolitan, Sidecar | Sweetness and ABV differ between brands |
| Cherry and berry | Maraschino (cherry), crème de cassis, raspberry liqueurs | Classic sours, Kir | Maraschino is dry-ish and funky, not "cherry syrup" |
| Herbal | Green and yellow herbal liqueurs, elderflower | Last Word-style drinks, spritzes | Often secret recipes; strong flavour, use small amounts |
| Anise | Sambuca | Neat, with coffee | Sweet, unlike arak (lesson 02.5) |
| Coffee | Coffee liqueurs | Espresso Martini, White Russian | Some are very sweet; check before swapping |
| Nut | Almond-flavoured (amaretto), hazelnut | Amaretto Sour, desserts | Allergen question: see below |
| Cream | Irish cream liqueurs | Shots, coffee drinks | Contain dairy; storage on the label |
| Bitter (amaro, aperitivo) | Red Italian aperitivo bitters, amari | Negroni, Spritz | Covered in lesson 02.7 |

### Allergens in liqueurs

Cream liqueurs contain milk. Nut liqueurs may be flavoured with real nuts or with apricot kernels or flavourings; the label does not always say. Never guess: if a guest declares an allergy, check the producer's information or the importer's label, and if you cannot confirm, say so and offer a drink you can confirm (Module 9 covers allergens in detail).

### Bitters

Cocktail bitters (ביטרס, *biters*) are concentrated infusions of bitter roots, barks, herbs and spices in strong alcohol. They are used by the **dash** (a quick flick of the bottle, about 0.8 ml in this course's conversions) to season a drink, like salt in cooking.

| Type | Flavour | Classic use |
|---|---|---|
| Aromatic bitters | Clove, cinnamon, gentian root, dark spice | Old Fashioned, Manhattan |
| Orange bitters | Bitter orange peel, spice | Martini variations |
| Anise-forward red bitters (for example Peychaud's style) | Anise, cherry, floral | Sazerac |

Aromatic bitters are strong: Angostura aromatic bitters are listed at 44.7% ABV. A dash adds very little alcohol, but not zero. Two dashes (about 1.6 ml) at 44.7% add about 0.7 ml of alcohol; in a 200 ml soft drink that is roughly 0.36% ABV. That matters to a guest who avoids alcohol for religious, medical or pregnancy reasons. Tell the guest, or use a bitters labelled alcohol-free.

### Why liqueurs sink: sugar and density

Density is how heavy a liquid is for its volume. Water weighs about 1 g per ml. Sugar dissolved in a liquid makes it heavier per ml; alcohol makes it lighter (ethanol weighs about 0.79 g per ml, lesson 02.1). So a sweet liqueur is usually denser than a dry spirit, and a 40% spirit is lighter than water. Layered drinks (lesson 04.5) stack liquids from the densest at the bottom to the lightest on top.

### Storage

Most liqueurs keep for a long time at room temperature, upright, closed and out of the sun. Cream liqueurs and some fruit liqueurs lose quality faster once opened; follow the storage advice on the label (many cream liqueurs say to refrigerate or use within a set time). Bitters keep almost indefinitely because of their high alcohol (professional judgment).

> [!NOTE]
> Liqueur minimums are from EU Regulation 2019/787, checked 2026-10-17. Brands named as types (triple sec, curaçao, aromatic bitters) are categories, not recommendations; ABVs vary by brand and market.

## Step by step

### How to substitute one orange liqueur for another

1. Compare the ABV on both labels. A 40% orange liqueur is drier and stronger than a 25–30% one.
2. Taste a drop of each (or smell if you do not taste alcohol) for sweetness.
3. If the replacement is sweeter, reduce the syrup in the recipe a little; if drier, add a little.
4. Make one drink, taste with a straw if allowed, and note the adjustment on the station card for the shift.

### How to handle bitters in an alcohol-free order

1. Ask: "Are you avoiding alcohol completely, or just not drinking spirits tonight?"
2. If completely: skip standard bitters, or use an alcohol-free bitters if the bar has one.
3. Say what you changed: "I've left out the bitters because they contain alcohol."

## Hands-on

Weigh how much sugar changes a liquid's density, then use that to build a three-layer alcohol-free drink: the same principle that keeps liqueurs at the bottom of a layered shot.

> [!CAUTION]
> Making the rich (2:1) syrup needs very hot water. Pour slowly, stir with a long spoon and let it cool before handling the glass.

### What you need

- 150 g white sugar, water, a kettle, two jars, a long spoon.
- A kitchen scale (1 g steps), a jigger or a medicine cup that measures 50 ml (or 30 ml), a narrow clear glass (a shot glass or a small tumbler), a bar spoon or teaspoon.
- Red food colouring or a little grenadine/raspberry syrup, a tea bag.
- Optional: a bottle of aromatic bitters, just to smell.
- Your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: measuring spoons instead of a jigger (50 ml ≈ 3 tablespoons + 1 teaspoon), and any coloured juice instead of food colouring.

### Time

35 minutes: 10 to make the syrups and cool them, 10 for weighing, 10 for layering, 5 to log.

### Steps

1. Simple syrup (1:1): stir 100 g sugar into 100 g warm water until clear.
2. Rich syrup (2:1): stir 50 g sugar into 25 g very hot water until clear. Let both cool.
3. Brew a cup of tea and let it cool; this is your "lighter" layer.
4. Weigh 50 ml of each liquid: tare a glass, pour exactly 50 ml with the jigger, read the grams. Do water, tea, simple syrup and rich syrup. Rinse and dry between pours.
5. Work out the density of each: grams ÷ 50 ml.
6. Predict the stacking order from heaviest to lightest. Write it down.
7. Layer: colour the rich syrup red. Pour 15 ml of it into the narrow glass. Hold the bar spoon upside down just above the liquid, touching the glass wall, and pour 15 ml of simple syrup slowly over the back of the spoon. Repeat with 15 ml of tea.
8. If you have bitters, smell them: note clove, cinnamon, citrus or anise. Do not taste them straight.
9. Log your four densities and whether the layers held.

### What you should see

Water and tea weigh about 50 g per 50 ml (density about 1.00). Simple syrup weighs about 61–62 g (about 1.23) and rich syrup about 66–67 g (about 1.33). So: rich syrup at the bottom, simple syrup in the middle, tea on top. If you poured slowly over the spoon, you see three bands; they blur slowly over minutes as the liquids mix at the edges. A very fast pour breaks the layers, however big the density difference.

<details>
<summary>Why this matters for liqueurs</summary>

Liqueurs contain at least 100 g of sugar per litre, and many much more, so most are denser than water; a 40% spirit with no sugar is lighter than water. A layered shot of a heavy coffee liqueur under a cream liqueur under a strong spirit works for the same reason your syrups stacked. You practise real layering in lesson 04.5.

</details>

### What this practice cannot teach

Syrups show the density principle, but real liqueurs vary by brand in sugar and alcohol, so in a bar you still check the order on the spec or test it once before service.

- [ ] I made simple and rich syrup and weighed 50 ml of four liquids.
- [ ] I predicted the order correctly before layering.
- [ ] I built three visible layers at least once.
- [ ] I can explain why a dash of bitters makes a drink "not completely alcohol-free".

## Common mistakes

- **Treating all orange liqueurs as the same.** Fix: compare ABV and sweetness; adjust syrup.
- **Calling a drink with bitters "alcohol-free".** Fix: ask how strictly the guest avoids alcohol and leave the bitters out or use an alcohol-free version.
- **Guessing about allergens.** Nut and cream liqueurs can contain allergens. Fix: check the label or the producer; if unsure, say so.
- **Storing cream liqueur like a spirit.** Fix: follow the label's storage advice, often the fridge after opening.
- **Pouring layers too fast.** Fix: pour slowly over the back of a spoon held against the glass.

## Key takeaways

- A liqueur is a sweetened, flavoured spirit: in the EU at least 15% ABV and at least 100 g of sugar per litre for most types.
- Learn liqueurs by family (orange, cherry/berry, herbal, anise, coffee, nut, cream, bitter) and check sweetness and ABV before swapping.
- Bitters are strong, unsweetened flavourings used by the dash; aromatic bitters are about 44.7% ABV, so they are not alcohol-free.
- Sugar makes liquids denser and alcohol makes them lighter; that is how layered drinks work.
- Never guess allergens in a liqueur; check or say you cannot confirm.
