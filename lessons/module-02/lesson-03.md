# 02.3 · Rum, tequila and mezcal

Rum comes from sugar cane; tequila and mezcal come from agave, a spiky Mexican plant. Together they cover a huge share of the cocktails you will make, from the Daiquiri and Mojito to the Margarita and Paloma. This lesson explains how each is made, how to read their labels, which styles you will meet on a back bar and what you can and cannot swap. You finish by tasting sugar sources and smoke in an alcohol-free comparison and reading eight label descriptions.

## Why it matters

A guest orders a Margarita "with good tequila, the real stuff". Another asks for a Mojito "with the dark rum". A third wants "something smoky" and points at the mezcal. If you know that "100% agave" on a tequila label is the guest's "real stuff", that a dark, heavy rum will turn a Mojito brown and change its taste, and that mezcal's smoke comes from how the agave is cooked, you can give each guest what they meant, not only what they said.

## Core ideas

### Rum: from sugar cane

Rum (רום, *rom*) is distilled from fermented sugar-cane products: molasses (מולסה, *molasa*, the dark syrup left after making sugar), cane syrup or fresh cane juice (from קנה סוכר, *kne sukar*). EU law sets a minimum of 37.5% ABV and distillation below 96%, so that some flavour of the raw material remains. Beyond that, rules vary a lot by country of origin, which is why rum is so diverse.

| Style | What it is | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| White (light) rum | Unaged or briefly aged, often filtered to remove colour; light and clean | Daiquiri, Mojito, highballs |
| Gold rum | Some ageing and/or caramel colour; a little vanilla and spice | Highballs, punches |
| Dark rum | Heavier, often from molasses and pot stills, sometimes coloured | Dark and stormy-type highballs, tiki drinks |
| Aged rum (añejo, "reserva", age statements) | Matured in barrels for years; more wood, dried fruit | Sipping, Old Fashioned-style drinks |
| Rhum agricole | From fresh cane juice, mostly in the French Caribbean; grassy, vegetal | Ti' Punch, some Daiquiri variations |
| Spiced rum | Rum with added spices and usually sugar | Mixed with cola; not a swap for plain rum |
| Overproof rum | Bottled well above 40% ABV, often over 60% | Small amounts in tiki drinks; handle with care |

Rum age statements are not regulated the same way everywhere. A number on the front label is not always the age of the youngest rum in the bottle (professional judgment). Read the back label and the producer's description before telling a guest how old a rum is.

### Tequila: blue agave from Mexico

Tequila (טקילה, *tekila*) is made only in authorised regions of Mexico (mainly the state of Jalisco) from one plant: blue Weber agave (אגבה, *agava*). The heart of the plant is cooked to turn its starches into sugar, crushed, fermented and distilled, usually twice. The Consejo Regulador del Tequila (CRT, the body that certifies tequila) recognises two categories:

- **100% agave tequila** ("100% de agave"): all the sugar comes from blue agave. It must be bottled in the appellation area.
- **Tequila** (often called "mixto"): at least 51% of the sugar from agave; up to 49% from other sugars. If the label does not say "100% agave", it is this category.

And age classes, which apply to both categories:

| Class | Ageing |
|---|---|
| Blanco (silver) | Unaged or rested only briefly; bright, peppery, vegetal |
| Joven (oro, gold) | Blanco blended with aged tequila, or with permitted colouring |
| Reposado ("rested") | At least 2 months in oak |
| Añejo ("aged") | At least 1 year in oak barrels of up to 600 litres |
| Extra añejo | At least 3 years in oak barrels of up to 600 litres |

Israeli bartenders usually say the Spanish class names as they are, for example "blanko" and "reposado" (professional judgment).

"Cristalino" is an aged tequila filtered to remove its colour. It is a marketing term, not an official class (professional judgment based on industry sources).

### Mezcal: many agaves, often smoky

Mezcal (מסקל, *meskal*) is also an agave spirit from Mexico, but it may use many agave species, not only blue agave, and it comes from different regions (Oaxaca is the best known). Traditionally the agave hearts are roasted in pits in the ground with wood and stones, which gives many mezcals their smoky taste. Mexico's mezcal standard (NOM-070) defines three categories by production method:

- **Mezcal**: modern equipment allowed (for example autoclaves for cooking, column stills).
- **Mezcal artesanal**: pit ovens and traditional crushing; pot stills of copper or clay; no column stills.
- **Mezcal ancestral**: the strictest; pit ovens, hand or stone-mill crushing and distillation in clay pots.

Agave spirits made outside these rules may be sold as "destilado de agave". Not every mezcal is very smoky, and not every smoky agave spirit is mezcal.

### Substitutions

- White rum ↔ another white rum: fine. Dark or spiced rum in a white-rum recipe: changes colour and taste; tell the guest.
- Tequila 100% agave ↔ another 100% agave of the same class: fine. Mixto for 100% agave: cheaper and harsher; tell the guest if they asked for "100% agave".
- Blanco in place of reposado (or the reverse): acceptable in most mixed drinks, but the taste changes.
- Mezcal for tequila: a different drink. Many bars offer a "mezcal Margarita" as a named variation.

> Tequila categories and classes are from the CRT website and mezcal categories from NOM-070 as summarised by Difford's Guide, both checked 2026-10-17. Brands and their availability in Israel change; no brand is recommended here.

## Step by step

### How to read a tequila or mezcal label in 20 seconds

1. Find the category word: "Tequila" or "Mezcal" (or "Destilado de agave").
2. For tequila, look for "100% agave" or "100% de agave". No such words means the mixto category.
3. Find the class: blanco/silver, joven/gold, reposado, añejo, extra añejo.
4. For mezcal, look for "artesanal" or "ancestral" and the agave species (for example espadín).
5. Check the ABV and, on an imported bottle in Israel, the Hebrew importer sticker (lesson 02.9).

### How to handle "a rum and cola" when the guest names no rum

1. Use the bar's house rum for that drink (often gold or white; ask your head bartender which).
2. If the guest says "dark", use the dark rum; if "spiced", use the spiced rum.
3. Never use overproof as a house pour.

## Hands-on

Two short exercises: an alcohol-free comparison of the sugar sources and smoke behind these spirits, then a label-reading drill.

> [!CAUTION]
> You use hot water to dissolve the sugars. Let the kettle water cool for a minute and hold cups by the base.

### What you need

- 1 teaspoon each of white sugar, light brown sugar and dark brown sugar; 1 teaspoon of molasses (מולסה, health-food shops) or, if you cannot find it, silan (date syrup) as a dark, rich stand-in.
- 1 teaspoon of agave syrup (סירופ אגבה, sold in most supermarkets).
- One bag of smoky tea (lapsang souchong) or ½ teaspoon of smoked paprika.
- Six small cups, a kettle, a teaspoon for tasting, water to rinse.
- The [tasting notes template](../../templates/tasting-notes.md).
- Low-cost alternative: skip the molasses and agave syrup and compare only white and brown sugar; skip the smoky tea and smell a burnt match after it has gone out (outdoors, briefly).

### Time

40 minutes: 20 for the comparison, 15 for the label drill, 5 to log.

### Steps

1. Dissolve each sweetener in its own cup with 50 ml of warm water. Label the cups.
2. Smell each cup, then taste a little with a clean spoon. Rinse with water between cups. Note: sweetness, colour, aroma (caramel, toffee, grassy, earthy, bitter).
3. Line them up from lightest to darkest. This is roughly the line from light, clean rum to heavy, molasses-rich dark rum.
4. Taste the agave syrup cup. Note how its sweetness differs from cane sugar (many people find it softer, slightly vegetal).
5. Brew the smoky tea (or stir the smoked paprika into 100 ml of hot water). Smell it, then add a teaspoon of it to the agave cup. This is your model of a smoky mezcal next to a clean blanco tequila.
6. Label drill: classify the eight labels below. Write your answers, then open the reference answers.
7. Log the session in your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).

| # | Label description |
|---|---|
| 1 | "Tequila Reposado — 100% de Agave — 38% vol" |
| 2 | "Tequila Gold — 38% vol" (no other claims) |
| 3 | "Mezcal Artesanal — Agave Espadín — 45% vol" |
| 4 | "Rhum Agricole Blanc — pur jus de canne — 50% vol" |
| 5 | "Spiced Rum — with natural spices and vanilla — 35% vol" |
| 6 | "Tequila Blanco 100% Agave — 40% vol" |
| 7 | "Destilado de Agave — 46% vol" |
| 8 | "Overproof Jamaican Rum — 63% vol" |

### What you should see

The darker the sweetener, the stronger the caramel, toffee and slightly bitter notes. Molasses (or silan) tastes much less sweet than its colour suggests and has a burnt-sugar edge: that is the backbone of heavy dark rum. Agave syrup tastes milder and rounder than cane sugar. One teaspoon of smoky tea changes the whole cup: smoke is strong, which is why a little mezcal goes a long way in a drink.

### What this practice cannot teach

Sweeteners in water show the direction of flavour from each raw material, but not what distillation and ageing do to it; only comparing real spirits (optional, tiny sips, spit) teaches that.

- [ ] I tasted and ranked the sweeteners and noted their aromas.
- [ ] I compared agave syrup with and without smoke.
- [ ] I classified all eight labels and checked my answers.
- [ ] I can explain "100% agave" vs "tequila" and the three mezcal categories to a guest.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming gold tequila is aged.** "Gold" or "oro" often means blanco with colouring or a blend. Fix: look for reposado, añejo or extra añejo.
- **Reading "tequila" as "100% agave".** Without those words it is the mixto category. Fix: check for "100% agave" before promising it.
- **Using dark or spiced rum in a white-rum recipe.** The Mojito turns brown and sweet. Fix: use the style the spec names; tell the guest about any change.
- **Calling every mezcal smoky.** Smoke levels vary a lot. Fix: describe the bottle you actually have.
- **Pouring overproof rum like normal rum.** It can be over 60%. Fix: keep it off the speed rail and measure every pour.

## Key takeaways

- Rum is distilled from sugar-cane products; molasses gives heavy, dark flavours and fresh cane juice gives grassy agricole styles.
- Tequila is made from blue Weber agave; "100% agave" means no other sugars, otherwise at least 51% agave.
- Tequila classes: blanco, joven, reposado (2+ months), añejo (1+ year), extra añejo (3+ years).
- Mezcal can use many agave species; NOM-070 divides it into mezcal, artesanal and ancestral by method.
- Substitute within the same style and class, and tell the guest when the drink will taste different.
