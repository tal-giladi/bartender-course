# 02.5 · Arak and other regional spirits

Arak is the spirit of the Levant and one of the drinks most often ordered in Israeli bars and restaurants, usually with water and ice or mixed with juice. It belongs to a family of anise-flavoured spirits around the Mediterranean, including Greek ouzo, Turkish raki and French pastis. This lesson explains how arak is made and served, why it turns white with water, and introduces other regional spirits you will meet in cocktails: pisco from Peru and Chile, cachaça from Brazil and soju from Korea.

## Why it matters

A table of six orders "a bottle of arak, water and ice" with their meze. A guest asks for "arak grapefruit". Someone else wants to know why their arak "went cloudy" and if it has gone bad. In many Israeli bars arak is a daily serve, not a curiosity (professional judgment). Getting the order of water and ice right, pouring a consistent ratio and explaining the cloudiness calmly are basic skills here, and guests notice when they are done well.

## Core ideas

### What arak is

Arak (ערק, *arak*) is a clear, unsweetened spirit flavoured with aniseed (אניס, *anis*), usually 50% ABV or more. It is traditionally made from grapes: wine is distilled, often twice, then redistilled with aniseed and sometimes rested in clay jars. Producers across the region, including in Israel, make it, and methods vary from traditional to industrial (some use neutral spirit as the base). Because it is unsweetened, arak is not a liqueur; that is the main difference from sambuca (lesson 02.6).

### Why it turns milky: the louche

The aroma of anise comes from an oil called anethole. Anethole dissolves in strong alcohol but not in water. When you add water and the alcohol level drops, the oil comes out of solution as tiny droplets that scatter light, so the drink turns milky white. This is called the **louche** (French, pronounced "loosh"). It is normal and expected, not a fault.

Cold has a similar effect: very cold arak, or ice added before water, can make the oil form small white flakes or a film. That is why the traditional order is **arak first, then water, then ice**.

### How arak is served

| Serve | How | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Arak with water and ice | About 1 part arak to 2 parts cold water, then ice | The classic. Ratios from 1:2 to 1:3 are common; guests often adjust themselves |
| Bottle service for a table | Bottle, a jug of cold water, a bucket of ice, small glasses | Guests pour their own; you bring refills of water and ice |
| Arak with grapefruit juice | Arak, grapefruit juice, ice in a highball | A very common Israeli bar serve (professional judgment) |
| Arak with lemonade and mint | Arak, lemonade (לימונענע style, lemon and mint), ice | Popular summer serve (professional judgment) |

These are serves, not fixed recipes; each bar sets its own measures.

### Arak's cousins: other anise spirits

| Spirit | Origin | Key difference from arak |
|---|---|---|
| Ouzo | Greece and Cyprus (protected name) | Anise and other spices, often slightly sweetened; at least 37.5% ABV; may use any agricultural alcohol |
| Raki | Turkey | Grape-based anise spirit, served with water like arak |
| Pastis | France | Anise plus liquorice root, usually lightly sweetened; drunk with lots of water |
| Sambuca | Italy | A sweet anise **liqueur**, not a dry spirit (lesson 02.6) |
| Absinthe | Switzerland and France | Anise, fennel and wormwood, very strong; louches with water |

> [!WARNING]
> Some bars set absinthe or sambuca on fire for show. This course never teaches flaming drinks: they burn people, glasses crack and the alcohol vapour can catch fire. Serve them without flame.

### Other regional spirits for cocktails

- **Pisco** (פיסקו, *pisko*) is grape brandy from Peru and Chile, central to the Pisco Sour (lesson 06.2). Peruvian pisco is distilled once to bottling strength, 38–48% ABV, with nothing added, not even water, and is unaged. Chilean pisco may be diluted with water and aged in wood. Peru and Chile both claim the name, so labels show the country.
- **Cachaça** (קשסה, *kashasa*) is Brazil's sugar-cane spirit, made from fresh cane juice rather than molasses, typically bottled around 38–48% ABV. It is the base of the Caipirinha (lesson 06.5). It tastes grassier than most rums.
- **Soju** (סוג'ו, *soju*) is Korea's most popular spirit. Modern green-bottle soju is usually diluted neutral spirit, often much lower in alcohol than vodka; check the label, as strengths vary a lot by brand.

> [!NOTE]
> Arak serving practice and Israeli serves are professional judgment; production facts are from Difford's Guide and the other sources below, checked 2026-10-17. Pisco, cachaça and soju strength ranges vary; always read the bottle.

## Step by step

### How to serve arak with water and ice

1. Use a highball or a small tumbler, clean and dry.
2. Measure the arak (your bar's pour, for example 40 ml) into the empty glass.
3. Add cold water, about twice the arak (80 ml for 40 ml), or ask the guest how strong they like it. Watch it turn milky.
4. Add ice last, to the top or as the guest prefers.
5. Serve with a short stir or a bar spoon in the glass; offer a jug of water at the table.

### How to answer "is my arak off?"

1. Look: milky white and even = normal louche. White flakes on top = oil set by cold.
2. Explain in one line: "That's the anise oil reacting to water and cold — it's how arak should look."
3. If the guest wants it clearer, offer a new glass with less ice, made in the right order.

## Hands-on

Train your nose on the anise family, then practise the arak serve with water until your ratio is consistent. An optional five-millilitre demonstration shows the louche.

> [!WARNING]
> The louche demonstration uses one teaspoon (5 ml) of real arak or ouzo. It is for looking, not drinking; pour it away afterwards. Skip it if you are under 18, do not keep alcohol at home, or will drive.

### What you need

- Anise seed (אניס), star anise (כוכב אניס, *kochav anis*) and fennel seed (זרעי שומר, *zar'ei shumar*), a teaspoon of each, from a spice shop or supermarket.
- Three small cups, a kettle, a highball glass, a jug of cold water, ice, a jigger or a medicine cup, a kitchen scale.
- Optional: 5 ml of arak, ouzo or pastis for the louche demo.
- Your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: a single spice (anise seed only) and a measuring cup instead of the scale.

### Time

35 minutes: 10 for the aroma round, 20 for the serve drill, 5 to log.

### Steps

1. Crush each spice lightly and put it in its own cup. Smell each one. Note how they differ: anise seed (sweet, liquorice), star anise (stronger, warmer), fennel (greener, softer).
2. Pour 100 ml of hot water over each, wait 3 minutes and smell again. This is the family of aromas behind arak, ouzo and pastis.
3. Serve drill: place the highball on the scale and tare. Pour 40 ml of water (your "arak") with the jigger. Then free-pour what you think is 80 ml of cold water. Read the total and work out the water you added.
4. Add ice last. Note the order out loud: "arak, water, ice".
5. Empty, dry and repeat five times. Aim for 80 ml ±5 ml of water each time.
6. Optional louche demo: put 5 ml of real arak in a small clear glass. Add 10 ml of cold water slowly and watch it turn milky. In a second glass, put two ice cubes first, then 5 ml of arak, and look for white flakes forming on the ice. Pour both away.
7. Log your five water pours (target 80 ml, result, error %) in your practice log.

### What you should see

The three spices smell related but not identical: star anise is the boldest, fennel the softest. Your first free pours of water probably miss 80 ml by 10–20 ml; by the fifth, most learners get within ±10 ml, and some within ±5 ml. In the optional demo, the glass with water turns evenly milky, while the glass with ice first may show small white flakes or a film: the anise oil setting in the cold. That is why ice goes in last.

### What this practice cannot teach

Water in your kitchen cannot show how a table of guests changes the ratio to taste or how fast a bottle-service table empties its ice bucket on a busy night; you will learn that on real shifts.

## Common mistakes

- **Ice before water.** The anise oil can set into white flakes and the drink looks spoiled. Fix: arak, then water, then ice.
- **Treating the louche as a fault.** Fix: explain it calmly; it shows the anise oil is there.
- **Calling sambuca "Italian arak".** Sambuca is a sweet liqueur. Fix: use sambuca only where a recipe asks for it.
- **Inconsistent ratios.** Free-poured water swings the strength. Fix: measure the arak and practise the water pour, or let guests pour their own water at the table.
- **Assuming every pisco is the same.** Peruvian and Chilean piscos are made differently. Fix: read the country and the producer notes before substituting.

## Key takeaways

- Arak is a dry, usually grape-based anise spirit, often 50% ABV or more.
- Water and cold make anise oil come out of solution: the drink turns milky (louche). This is normal.
- Serve arak in the order arak, water, ice, at about one part arak to two parts water, or as the guest likes.
- Ouzo, raki and pastis are related anise spirits; sambuca is a sweet anise liqueur.
- Pisco is grape brandy from Peru and Chile, cachaça is Brazil's fresh-cane-juice spirit, and soju is Korea's lower-strength spirit.
