# 02.4 · Whisky and brandy

Whisky is distilled from grain and brandy from fruit, usually grapes, and both are usually aged in oak barrels. They are the spirits guests most often drink neat or on ice, and the ones they ask most questions about: how old, how smoky, which is "smoother". This lesson gives you the legal definitions that matter, the styles on a typical back bar, how to read an age statement, and trains your nose on the aromas barrels and raw materials create.

## Why it matters

A guest points at the shelf: "What's the difference between that Scotch and that bourbon? I want something sweet, not smoky." Another asks whether a VSOP cognac is older than an XO. A third orders a Whiskey Sour and you are out of the house bourbon. Guests who drink whisky and brandy often care about the details, and they notice when the bartender knows them. Correct answers here build trust quickly and avoid wrong substitutions.

## Core ideas

### Whisky in general

Whisky (וויסקי, *viski*; spelled "whiskey" in Ireland and the US) is distilled from a fermented mash of cereal grains such as barley, corn, rye or wheat. Under EU rules it must be distilled to below 94.8% ABV, aged at least three years in wooden casks of 700 litres or less, and bottled at 40% ABV or more. Age statements on a bottle (for example "12 years") refer to the youngest whisky in it under Scotch rules (and in most other places); older whiskies may be in the blend too.

### Scotch whisky

The Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 set the core rules: made in Scotland from water and malted barley (other whole grains may be added), distilled below 94.8%, matured in Scotland in oak casks of no more than 700 litres for at least three years, at least 40% ABV, and nothing added except water and plain caramel colouring. The regulations create five categories:

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Single malt Scotch | 100% malted barley, pot stills, from one distillery |
| Single grain Scotch | From one distillery, may include other grains (usually column-distilled) |
| Blended malt Scotch | Single malts from more than one distillery |
| Blended grain Scotch | Single grains from more than one distillery |
| Blended Scotch | A blend of malt and grain whiskies; most Scotch sold is blended (professional judgment) |

**Peat** is decomposed plant matter dug from bogs. Burning it to dry malted barley gives smoky, medicinal flavours to some whiskies, famously from the island of Islay. "Peated" means smoky; most Scotch is not heavily peated.

### American whiskey: bourbon and rye

US federal rules define bourbon:

- at least 51% corn in the mash;
- distilled at no more than 80% ABV (160 proof);
- aged in new charred oak barrels, entering the barrel at no more than 62.5% ABV;
- bottled at no less than 40% ABV, with no added colouring or flavouring.

"Straight bourbon" has been aged at least two years. Bourbon can be made anywhere in the US. The new charred barrels give bourbon strong vanilla, caramel and coconut-like notes, which is why many guests find it "sweeter" than Scotch. Rye whiskey follows a similar rule with at least 51% rye, which gives a spicier, drier taste and is the classic choice for a Manhattan in many bars.

### Other whiskies

- **Irish whiskey**: as an EU country, Ireland follows the EU three-year minimum; often triple distilled and smooth.
- **Canadian, Japanese and world whiskies**: rules and styles vary; read the label.
- **Israeli whisky**: the Milk & Honey (M&H) distillery in Jaffa, founded in 2012, makes single malt whisky. Israel's heat speeds up maturation, so young Israeli whiskies can taste older than their age (producer and press claim).

### Brandy and cognac

Brandy (ברנדי, *brendi*) is a spirit distilled from wine or fruit. EU "brandy" is made from wine spirit, aged at least one year in oak (or six months in casks under 1,000 litres) and bottled at 36% ABV or more.

**Cognac** (קוניאק, *konyak*) is grape brandy from the Cognac region of France, double distilled in pot stills and aged in oak. Its age labels refer to the youngest brandy in the blend:

| Label | Minimum age of the youngest brandy |
|---|---|
| VS ("Very Special") | 2 years |
| VSOP ("Very Superior Old Pale") | 4 years |
| Napoléon | 6 years |
| XO ("Extra Old") | 10 years (raised from 6 years in April 2018) |

Other brandies you may meet: **armagnac** (another French grape brandy region), **calvados** (apple brandy from Normandy), **pisco** (lesson 02.5), and unaged fruit brandies (eaux-de-vie, such as kirsch from cherries). Pomace brandy, such as Italian grappa, is distilled from the skins and seeds left after winemaking.

### What the barrel does

| Aroma | Hebrew (transliteration) | Usually comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Vanilla, caramel, coconut | וניל, קרמל (*vanil, karamel*) | New or heavily charred American oak (bourbon) |
| Dried fruit (raisin, fig) | פירות יבשים (*perot yeveshim*) | Long ageing, sherry casks, grape base (cognac) |
| Baking spice (clove, cinnamon) | תבלינים (*tavlinim*) | Oak and rye grain |
| Smoke, iodine, seaweed | עשן (*ashan*) | Peat used to dry the malt |
| Bread, cereal, honey | — | Malted barley, younger whisky |

### Substitutions

- Bourbon ↔ another bourbon: fine. Bourbon ↔ rye: acceptable in most mixed drinks; rye is spicier and drier.
- Unpeated Scotch ↔ bourbon: the drink changes noticeably (less vanilla, more cereal). Tell the guest.
- Never substitute a peated Scotch silently; smoke dominates any drink.
- VS cognac ↔ another VS or a decent grape brandy in mixed drinks: fine. Do not use an XO as a mixing brandy without the manager's approval; it is the bar's most expensive stock (professional judgment).

> Legal minimums are from the Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009, US 27 CFR 5.143 and EU Regulation 2019/787; cognac age labels from the BNIC rule change reported by Drinks International; all checked 2026-10-17.

## Step by step

### How to recommend a whisky to an unsure guest

1. Ask two questions: "Sweet or dry?" and "Do you like smoky flavours?"
2. Sweet and not smoky → bourbon or a sherry-cask single malt.
3. Dry and not smoky → rye, or a light blended or Irish whiskey.
4. Smoky → a peated Scotch; warn them it is strongly smoky.
5. Ask how they want it: neat, on ice, or with a splash of water. Serve their choice without comment.

## Hands-on

Build a "barrel aroma kit" from kitchen ingredients, train it blind, then use it to describe three whisky and brandy styles.

> [!CAUTION]
> You toast bread and may use a knife to cut dried fruit. Watch the toaster, and cut on a board, away from your hand.

### What you need

- Seven small jars or cups with lids (or cover with foil): vanilla sugar or a split vanilla pod (not vanilla extract, which contains alcohol), a teaspoon of caramel or dulce de leche, 3–4 raisins or a dried fig cut open, 2 cloves plus a piece of cinnamon, a slice of well-toasted bread, a bag of smoky tea (lapsang souchong) or a pinch of smoked paprika, a small piece of dried seaweed (nori sheet, sold for sushi).
- A blindfold or someone to shuffle the jars; a pen; the [tasting notes template](../../templates/tasting-notes.md).
- Low-cost alternative: any five of the seven; caramel can be made by melting a spoon of sugar in a pan until amber (careful: very hot).

### Time

40 minutes: 15 to prepare the kit, 15 for the blind rounds, 10 to write the style profiles.

### Steps

1. Put each item in its own jar and number the jars on the bottom so you cannot see the number while smelling.
2. Open round: smell each jar and write the aroma word and the likely source from the table in Core ideas (for example "vanilla — new charred American oak").
3. Blind round 1: have someone shuffle the jars. Identify each. Score out of seven.
4. Rest five minutes (smell plain water or your sleeve), then blind round 2. Score again.
5. Style profiles: from the kit, choose two or three jars that together suggest each style and write one sentence a guest would understand:
   - a bourbon (vanilla, caramel, spice);
   - a peated Islay single malt (smoke, seaweed, bread);
   - a VSOP cognac (dried fruit, vanilla, spice).
6. Keep the kit (sealed) for the module practical in lesson 02.9 and log the session in your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).

### What you should see

Vanilla, smoke and caramel are the easiest; cloves and cinnamon get confused with each other; toasted bread and dried fruit are the hardest to name blind. Most beginners score 4–5 of 7 in round 1 and improve by one or two in round 2. Your style sentences should mention flavours, not prices or "smoothness": "sweet vanilla and caramel with some spice" is useful to a guest; "very smooth" is not.

<details>
<summary>Example style sentences</summary>

Bourbon: "Sweet, with vanilla and caramel from new charred oak and a little cinnamon spice." Peated Islay malt: "Strong smoke with a sea-salt, seaweed edge over a bready malt base." VSOP cognac: "Soft dried fruit like raisins, with light vanilla and baking spice from the oak."

</details>

### What this practice cannot teach

A kitchen kit trains your vocabulary for aromas, but not how alcohol strength, body and finish feel in a real whisky or brandy; an optional side-by-side of two real spirits (tiny sips, spit, never before driving) adds that.

## Common mistakes

- **Calling all whisky "Scotch".** Scotch is only whisky made and matured in Scotland. Fix: name the country and style: Scotch, bourbon, Irish, Israeli.
- **Thinking "bourbon must come from Kentucky".** It can be made anywhere in the US. Fix: use the legal rules (51% corn, new charred oak).
- **Reading XO as "a bit older than VSOP".** XO's youngest brandy is at least 10 years, VSOP's at least 4. Fix: learn the table; price usually follows age.
- **Serving a peated whisky to someone who asked for "smooth".** Fix: always ask the smoke question first.
- **Using vanilla extract in the kit.** It contains alcohol and smells of it. Fix: use vanilla sugar or a pod.

## Key takeaways

- Whisky is distilled from grain; EU whisky needs at least three years in wood and 40% ABV.
- Scotch: made and matured in Scotland, at least three years in oak, five legal categories; peat gives smoke.
- Bourbon: at least 51% corn, new charred oak, distilled to no more than 80%, bottled at 40% or more.
- Brandy is distilled from wine or fruit; cognac labels VS, VSOP, Napoléon and XO mean at least 2, 4, 6 and 10 years.
- Ask "sweet or dry?" and "smoky or not?" before recommending, and substitute within the same style.
