# 02.1 · How alcohol is made: fermentation, distillation, maturation and ABV

Every bottle on a back bar starts the same way: something sweet is fermented by yeast, and some of those liquids are then distilled and aged. Once you understand these three steps and the number on the label that measures the result, you can place almost any product in its family, explain it to a guest and work out how strong a drink really is. This lesson walks through the process, teaches the alcohol maths a bartender uses, and ends with a fermentation demonstration you can run in your kitchen.

## Why it matters

A guest at the bar asks, "Is the house whisky stronger than the wine?" Another wants to know why her gin and tonic tastes weaker than the one she had last week. Your manager asks you how many standard drinks are in a new 120 ml cocktail on the menu. None of these questions is about a recipe. They are about how alcohol is made and measured. A bartender who can answer them calmly and correctly sounds professional, serves more responsibly, and makes fewer mistakes when swapping one product for another.

## Core ideas

### Three steps from sugar to spirit

| Step | Hebrew (transliteration) | What happens | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fermentation | תסיסה (*tsisa*) | Yeast eats sugar and makes alcohol (ethanol) and carbon dioxide gas | Beer, wine, cider: usually about 4–15% alcohol |
| Distillation | זיקוק (*zikuk*) | The fermented liquid is heated; alcohol evaporates before water and is collected | A clear, strong spirit, often 60–95% alcohol before bottling |
| Maturation (ageing) | יישון (*yishun*) | The spirit rests in wooden barrels (חבית, *chavit*) for months or years | Colour, softer taste, aromas of vanilla, spice, dried fruit |

Not every drink goes through all three. Beer and wine stop after fermentation. Vodka is distilled but not aged. Whisky, aged rum and brandy go through all three.

### Fermentation

Fermentation is what yeast (a tiny living fungus) does when it has sugar and no air: it turns the sugar into ethanol (the alcohol in drinks) and carbon dioxide (CO2, the gas in beer foam and sparkling wine). The sugar source decides the drink:

- Grapes or other fruit already contain sugar → wine, cider.
- Grains (barley, wheat, corn, rye) contain starch. Starch must first be turned into sugar, usually by malting (sprouting the grain) or by adding enzymes → beer, and the "wash" for whisky.
- Sugar cane juice or molasses (the dark syrup left after making sugar) → the base for rum.
- Cooked agave (a Mexican plant) → the base for tequila and mezcal.

Fermentation stops when the sugar runs out or when the alcohol gets too strong for the yeast. Most yeast slow down and stop somewhere around 14–16% alcohol (professional judgment; it depends on the strain). That is why no beer or wine reaches spirit strength by fermentation alone. Fortified wines such as port reach about 19–22% only because spirit is added to them (lesson 02.7).

### Distillation

Distillation concentrates alcohol. Ethanol boils at about 78 °C, water at 100 °C. When a fermented liquid is heated in a still (the apparatus used for distilling), the vapour that rises is richer in alcohol than the liquid left behind. Cooling that vapour turns it back into a stronger liquid.

- A **pot still** (a big kettle, usually copper) works one batch at a time and keeps more flavour from the base. Many malt whiskies, cognacs and some rums use pot stills, often distilling twice.
- A **column still** (a tall tower with plates inside) runs continuously and can reach a much higher strength with less flavour. Vodka and most grain whisky come from column stills.

The distiller separates the run into three parts. The **heads** come first and contain the most volatile compounds, including methanol, which is harmful. The **heart** is the clean middle part that is kept. The **tails** come last and carry heavy, oily flavours. Where the distiller "cuts" between these parts is a big part of a spirit's style.

> [!CAUTION]
> Never try to distil at home. Heating alcohol vapour is a fire risk, and an untrained still run can concentrate methanol. Everything in this course uses bought products or alcohol-free practice ingredients.

### Maturation

Many spirits leave the still clear. Ageing in oak barrels slowly changes them: the wood adds colour, vanilla and spice aromas and some tannin (the drying feel you know from strong tea), while a little air through the wood softens harsh notes. Hot climates age spirits faster than cold ones. Some laws set a minimum age: Scotch whisky must age at least three years in oak, for example (lesson 02.4). A dark colour alone does not prove age, because some categories may add caramel colouring.

### ABV and proof

ABV (alcohol by volume, אחוז אלכוהול, *achuz alkohol*) is the share of the liquid that is pure ethanol, measured at 20 °C. Labels write it as "40% vol" or "40% alc./vol". A 700 ml bottle at 40% contains 280 ml of pure ethanol.

US labels may also show **proof**, which is simply twice the ABV: 80 proof = 40% ABV, 100 proof = 50% ABV. US federal rules use proof in their definitions; for example, bourbon must be distilled at no more than 160 proof (80% ABV). Israeli and European labels use % vol.

### From ml to grams: the standard drink

Ethanol is lighter than water: 1 ml weighs about 0.789 g. So:

$$\text{grams of alcohol} = \text{ml} \times \text{ABV} \times 0.789$$

A standard drink (מנה סטנדרטית, *mana standartit*) is a fixed amount of pure alcohol used in health advice. Countries disagree on the size. The World Health Organization often uses 10 g; the Israeli Ministry of Health's information sheet on alcohol definitions uses the US definition of about 14 g, which is roughly a 340 ml beer at 5%, a 140 ml glass of wine at 12% or a 42 ml spirit pour at 40%.

| Serve | Volume and ABV | Grams of alcohol | Standard drinks (14 g) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beer, small bottle | 330 ml at 5% | 330 × 0.05 × 0.789 ≈ 13.0 g | ≈ 0.9 |
| Beer, large glass | 500 ml at 5% | ≈ 19.7 g | ≈ 1.4 |
| Glass of wine | 150 ml at 13% | ≈ 15.4 g | ≈ 1.1 |
| Spirit pour | 30 ml at 40% | ≈ 9.5 g | ≈ 0.7 |
| Double spirit pour | 60 ml at 40% | ≈ 18.9 g | ≈ 1.4 |

### ABV of a mixed drink

When you mix ingredients, the alcohol spreads through the whole volume. Add up the pure alcohol and divide by the total volume:

$$\text{ABV of mix} = \frac{\sum (\text{ml} \times \text{ABV})}{\text{total ml}}$$

Example: 60 ml gin at 40% plus 30 ml lime juice and syrup (0%) = 24 ml alcohol in 90 ml = 26.7%. Melted ice from shaking adds water and lowers it further; Module 3 measures that. The course's recipe tools use exactly this formula.

> [!NOTE]
> Health definitions of a standard drink differ by country and can change. The 14 g figure is from the Israeli Ministry of Health sheet linked below, checked 2026-10-17.

## Step by step

### How to work out the strength of any serve

1. Find the ABV on the label (for example "40% vol"). Write it as a fraction: 40% = 0.40.
2. Multiply the volume in ml by the ABV: 45 ml × 0.40 = 18 ml of pure alcohol.
3. Multiply by 0.789 to get grams: 18 × 0.789 ≈ 14.2 g.
4. Divide by 14 to get standard drinks in the Israeli Ministry of Health sense: 14.2 ÷ 14 ≈ 1.0.
5. For a mixed drink, repeat steps 2 and 3 for each alcoholic ingredient, add them up, then divide the total ml of alcohol by the total volume to get the drink's ABV.

### How to place a product in the process

1. Ask: what was the sugar source? (grain, grape, cane, agave, fruit)
2. Ask: was it distilled? If its ABV is above about 16% and it is not a fortified wine, yes.
3. Ask: was it aged? Look for an age statement or a word such as "reposado", "VSOP" or "aged in oak".
4. Say it in one sentence: "Bourbon is fermented from mostly corn, distilled, then aged in new charred oak barrels."

## Hands-on

You will watch fermentation happen, then practise the alcohol maths you will use at the bar.

### What you need

- Two clean empty plastic bottles (500 ml), two balloons, a funnel, warm tap water (about 35–40 °C, warm to the touch, not hot).
- 2 tablespoons of sugar, 2 teaspoons of dried baker's yeast (שמרים יבשים, a few shekels a packet; professional judgment).
- The worksheet in [`labs/module-02/abv-worksheet.md`](../../labs/module-02/abv-worksheet.md) and a calculator (your phone is fine).
- Low-cost alternative: no balloons? Cover each bottle with a small plastic bag held by a rubber band, or use two glasses and watch the foam instead.

### Time

45 minutes: 10 to set up, 30 of waiting while you do the worksheet, 5 to compare the bottles.

### Steps

1. Pour 250 ml of warm water into each bottle.
2. Bottle A: add 2 tablespoons of sugar and 1 teaspoon of yeast. Bottle B (the control): add only 1 teaspoon of yeast, no sugar.
3. Put the caps on, shake each bottle for 10 seconds, then take the caps off and stretch a balloon over each neck.
4. Stand both bottles somewhere warm. Note the time.
5. While you wait, do the ABV worksheet: ten serves to convert to grams and standard drinks, three mixed drinks to work out the ABV, and two proof conversions.
6. After 30 minutes, compare the two balloons. Write what you see in your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
7. Pour both bottles down the sink. This is a science demonstration, not a drink.

### What you should see

Bottle A's balloon starts to lift within 15–30 minutes and the liquid foams; bottle B changes little. The gas is CO2 made by the yeast as it eats sugar. The alcohol is there too, in a tiny amount you cannot smell yet. The control bottle shows that the yeast needs sugar to ferment. If neither balloon moved, the water was probably too hot (it kills yeast) or too cold (it slows it); repeat with water that feels just warm.

<details>
<summary>Worked example from the worksheet</summary>

A 120 ml cocktail with 45 ml rum at 40% and 15 ml orange liqueur at 40%: alcohol = 18 + 6 = 24 ml, which is 24 × 0.789 ≈ 18.9 g, about 1.35 standard drinks of 14 g. ABV of the drink = 24 ÷ 120 = 20%. Full answers are in [`abv-worksheet-solution.md`](../../labs/module-02/abv-worksheet-solution.md).

### What this practice cannot teach

A balloon shows that fermentation makes gas; it cannot show you how distillation cuts or barrel ageing change a spirit's taste, which you learn by comparing real products in later lessons.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up ABV and proof.** "This rum is 80" means 80 proof (40% ABV) on a US label. Fix: if the number is over 75, ask whether it is proof and halve it.
- **Forgetting the 0.789.** Multiplying ml by ABV gives ml of alcohol, not grams. Fix: always finish with × 0.789 before comparing with a gram-based standard drink.
- **Assuming a strong-tasting drink is strong.** Sweetness, ice and citrus hide alcohol; a sweet 120 ml cocktail can hold more alcohol than a beer. Fix: do the maths, not the taste test.
- **Thinking dark means old.** Colour can come from caramel colouring or from the barrel's char. Fix: check the age statement or the legal category.
- **Treating a "shot" as a fixed size.** Pours differ by bar (professional judgment). Fix: know your bar's house pour in ml and calculate from that.

## Key takeaways

- Fermentation makes alcohol from sugar; distillation concentrates it; maturation in wood adds colour and flavour.
- Beer and wine stop at fermentation and stay below about 16% ABV; spirits are distilled.
- ABV is the share of pure alcohol; US proof is twice the ABV.
- Grams of alcohol = ml × ABV × 0.789; the Israeli Ministry of Health uses a standard drink of about 14 g.
- A mixed drink's ABV is total alcohol divided by total volume, including any water from ice.
