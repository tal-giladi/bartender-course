# 02.9 · Labels, storage and substitutions: classify a back bar

This is the practical close of Module 2. You learn to read the Hebrew parts of an alcohol label in Israel, including the health warning the law requires, pull together the storage rules from the whole module, and use a simple method for substitutions. Then you put it all to work: you classify a 30-bottle back bar, run an identification drill and write up the aroma kits you built in earlier lessons.

## Why it matters

On your first week in a new bar, the manager gives you the stock list and says, "Learn the back bar." That means knowing what every bottle is, where it goes, how it is stored once opened, and what to use when it runs out. Bartenders who can do this quickly are trusted with the station sooner. Reading labels correctly also protects you: the label tells you the strength you are serving, whether a product is really alcohol-free, and who to contact about its ingredients.

## Core ideas

### What is on an Israeli alcohol label

Products sold in Israel carry Hebrew labelling. Imported drinks usually keep their original label and add a Hebrew sticker (מדבקה, *madbeka*) from the importer (יבואן, *yevu'an*). According to the US government's guide for exporters to Israel, imported food and drink must show the country of origin, the producer's name and address, the Israeli importer's name and address, the contents and the volume in metric units, with Hebrew always present.

| Hebrew on the label | Transliteration | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| אלכוהול % בנפח | *alkohol achuz banefach* | Alcohol % by volume (ABV) |
| תכולה / מ"ל | *tchula / mililiter* | Contents, in ml |
| יצרן | *yatzran* | Producer |
| יבואן | *yevu'an* | Importer |
| ארץ ייצור | *eretz yitzur* | Country of production |
| רכיבים | *rechivim* | Ingredients |
| כשר / תעודת כשרות | *kasher / te'udat kashrut* | Kosher / kashrut certification |

Many products also carry a kashrut mark. Some venues serve only kosher-certified products, so check the bar's policy before adding a new bottle (professional judgment).

### The statutory health warning

Israeli law (the Alcoholic Beverages Advertising and Marketing Restriction Law, 2012) defines a "strong alcoholic beverage" as one with more than 15.5% alcohol by volume, and prohibits selling an alcoholic drink in a container without the warning set by the Minister of Health. The 2013 warning regulations set two texts:

| Drink | Required warning (statutory) |
|---|---|
| Strong drink, above 15.5% ABV | אזהרה: צריכה מופרזת של אלכוהול מסכנת חיים ומזיקה לבריאות! ("Warning: excessive consumption of alcohol endangers life and harms health!") |
| Other alcoholic drinks | אזהרה: מכיל אלכוהול – מומלץ להימנע משתייה מופרזת ("Warning: contains alcohol – excessive drinking should be avoided") |

The warning must be clear and prominent, in black letters on a white background inside a black frame, and cover at least 15% of the label. This is a legal requirement on the product (statutory); checking that your bar's stock carries it is good practice for a bartender but is mainly the importer's and seller's responsibility (professional judgment). The warning wording is also a quick check of strength: a vermouth at 16% carries the "strong" text, a fino sherry at 15% does not.

### Storage after opening: the whole module on one table

| Category | Store after opening | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Spirits (vodka, gin, rum, tequila, mezcal, whisky, brandy, arak) | Room temperature, upright, closed, out of the sun | High alcohol; they keep for a long time |
| Liqueurs and aperitivo bitters, amari | Room temperature, closed; follow the label | Sugar and alcohol preserve them; flavour slowly fades |
| Cream liqueurs | As the label says, often the fridge | Dairy |
| Bitters | Room temperature, cap on | Very high alcohol, tiny doses |
| Vermouth and aromatised wines | Fridge, closed, dated; about a month (professional judgment) | Wine oxidises |
| Fino and manzanilla sherry | Fridge, use within days | Fragile, like an open white wine |
| Other sherry, port | Cool or fridge, closed; weeks | Fortified wine still oxidises |
| Wine, sparkling wine | Fridge with a stopper; one to a few days (professional judgment) | Oxidation; bubbles escape |
| Beer and cider | Cold, sealed until use; finish once opened | Lose carbonation |

### A method for substitutions

1. **Same category, same style** (London dry for London dry; bourbon for bourbon). Usually fine; tell the guest if they asked for a brand.
2. **Same category, neighbouring style** (rye for bourbon; reposado for blanco). Acceptable in mixed drinks; tell the guest.
3. **Different category** (vodka for gin; mezcal for tequila). A different drink; only with the guest's agreement.
4. Always check three things: **ABV** (strength changes), **sweetness** (balance changes) and **allergens** (cream, nuts).

> [!NOTE]
> The warning texts and the 15.5% threshold are statutory, quoted from the consolidated law and regulations on Nevo, checked 2026-10-17. Import labelling details are from the US International Trade Administration guide (updated 2026-03-30) and should be confirmed against Israeli Ministry of Health guidance for any compliance decision. Storage times are professional judgment.

## Step by step

### How to learn a new back bar

1. Walk the shelves left to right. For each bottle say aloud: category, style, ABV.
2. Group what you find: house (well) products, call brands, premium, wine-based (fridge) products.
3. Check every opened wine-based bottle for a date label and smell; move any undated one to the head bartender.
4. For each house product, name a substitute and what the guest should be told.
5. Write it down as a one-page cheat sheet and learn it before your next shift.

### How to read any bottle in 30 seconds

1. Category word (gin, rum, liqueur, vermouth…).
2. Style or class (London dry, reposado, VSOP, rosso…).
3. ABV, and the warning type (strong or other).
4. Hebrew sticker: importer and country.
5. Any allergen or "contains" statement.

## Hands-on

The module practical has three parts: classify a 30-product back bar, run a timed identification drill, and write up your aroma kits. Everything is on paper or in a spreadsheet; no alcohol is needed.

### What you need

- The lab files in [`labs/module-02/`](../../labs/module-02/README.md): `back-bar-30.csv`, `id-drill.md`, `aroma-kit-writeup.md` and their solution files.
- A spreadsheet program (LibreOffice Calc, Google Sheets or Excel) or a printout and a pen.
- Your aroma kits from lessons 02.2, 02.4 and 02.5, or the ingredients to rebuild them.
- A phone stopwatch and your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: copy the 30 descriptions into a notebook by hand; it takes longer but teaches the labels just as well.

### Time

90 minutes: 45 for the back bar, 15 for the identification drill, 25 for the aroma write-up, 5 to log.

### Steps

1. **Back bar (45 min).** Open `back-bar-30.csv`. For each of the 30 label descriptions fill in: category, style or class, base material, aged (yes, no or unknown), warning type (strong above 15.5% or other), storage after opening, a substitute from the same list (or "none"), and what to tell the guest. Use only your notes and the lessons, not the solution.
2. Check your sheet against `back-bar-30-solution.md`. Score one point per row where category, style and warning type are all right. Target: 24 of 30 (80%).
3. For every wrong row, write one line: what you missed and which lesson covers it.
4. **Identification drill (15 min).** Start the stopwatch and answer all 20 descriptions in `id-drill.md`. Stop the clock. Check with `id-drill-solution.md`. Target: 16 of 20 in under 6 minutes.
5. **Aroma kit write-up (25 min).** Rebuild or open your kits, redo the blind rounds and complete `aroma-kit-writeup.md`: scores, your three hardest aromas, and four guest descriptions.
6. **Log.** Record all three scores and your times in your practice log. If you missed a target, repeat that part in two or three days.

### What you should see

First attempts usually score 20–26 on the back bar (professional judgment). The most common misses are the traps the solution file explains: the spiced spirit drink at 35%, the reposado that is not 100% agave, and the warning type of the vermouths (16% and 18% are already "strong"). In the drill, beginners most often confuse bianco and dry vermouth, and genever and gin. Aroma scores usually rise compared with the original lessons because you now have words for what you smell.

### What this practice cannot teach

Descriptions on paper are tidier than real bottles, which have tiny print, foreign languages and stickers over stickers; spend ten minutes in a shop reading real back labels to close that gap.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading the front label only.** The style, allergen and importer details are often on the back or the Hebrew sticker. Fix: turn every bottle around.
- **Assuming the warning always says the same thing.** There are two statutory texts, split at 15.5% ABV. Fix: use the warning as a quick strength check.
- **Leaving wine-based products on the shelf.** Fix: vermouth, sherry and port go in the fridge once opened, with a date.
- **Substituting across categories without asking.** Fix: same style first, neighbour style next, different category only with the guest's agreement.
- **Ignoring ABV and sweetness when swapping.** Fix: check both on the label before you pour.

## Key takeaways

- Israeli labels carry Hebrew details; imports add a Hebrew sticker with the importer's name and address, origin, contents and volume.
- By law, drinks above 15.5% ABV carry the "strong drink" warning and other alcoholic drinks a milder one; both in a black frame on at least 15% of the label.
- Spirits and most liqueurs keep at room temperature; anything made from wine goes in the fridge once opened.
- Substitute within the same style first, check ABV, sweetness and allergens, and tell the guest what changes.
- Knowing the back bar by category, style and storage is what lets a manager trust you with the station.
