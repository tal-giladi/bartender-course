# 02.7 · Vermouth, fortified wines and aperitifs

Vermouth, sherry and port look like spirits on a back bar, but they are wines: lower in alcohol, made from grapes and, once opened, perishable. Vermouth is essential to the Martini, Manhattan and Negroni; sherry and port appear in classic drinks and are served on their own; aperitivo bitters drive the Spritz and the Negroni. This lesson explains what each is, the styles you need to know, and the storage rule that most beginners get wrong. You will see that rule for yourself in an oxidation demonstration.

## Why it matters

A guest sends back a Manhattan saying it "tastes flat and sour". The bartender before you left the sweet vermouth open on the back bar for a month. Another guest asks for "a dry sherry" and you hand them a sweet cream sherry. These are common, avoidable faults. Treating vermouth and sherry as wine, and knowing which bottle is dry and which is sweet, prevents them.

## Core ideas

### Vermouth

Vermouth (ורמוט, *vermut*) is an **aromatised wine**: wine flavoured with herbs and spices, with added alcohol. Under EU rules an aromatised wine must be at least 75% wine and between 14.5% and just under 22% ABV, and vermouth must take its characteristic taste from wormwood (*Artemisia*, לענה, *la'ana*), a bitter herb. Producers add their own mix of botanicals and sugar.

| Style | Colour and taste | Classic uses |
|---|---|---|
| Dry (extra dry, "French") | Pale, dry, herbal, light bitterness | Martini, some highballs |
| Bianco / blanc | Pale but sweet, vanilla and floral | Spritz variations, served on ice |
| Sweet red (rosso, "Italian") | Red-brown, sweet, spice, bitter edge | Manhattan, Negroni, Americano |
| Rosé | Pink, light, fruity, medium sweet | Highballs, spritzes |

Never swap dry and sweet vermouth without telling the guest; a Martini made with sweet red vermouth is a different drink.

### Fortified wines

A fortified wine is wine with grape spirit added, which raises its strength and helps it keep.

**Sherry** (שרי, *sheri*) comes from the area around Jerez in southern Spain and is made mostly from the Palomino grape. The base wine is fortified to about 15% or 17%, and that choice sets how it ages:

- **Fino and manzanilla** age under a layer of yeast called **flor**, which protects them from air. Pale, bone-dry, salty, nutty. Very fragile once opened.
- **Amontillado** starts under flor, then ages with air. Amber, dry, nutty.
- **Oloroso** ages in contact with air from the start. Darker, dry, rich, walnut.
- **Sweet styles**: Pedro Ximénez (PX) and Moscatel come from sun-dried grapes and are very sweet and dark. Cream and medium sherries are blends of dry sherry with sweet wine.

**Port** (פורט, *port*) comes from the Douro Valley in Portugal. Grape spirit is added during fermentation, which stops the yeast while sugar remains, so port is sweet and usually 19–22% ABV.

- **Ruby style** (Ruby, Reserve, Late Bottled Vintage, Vintage): deep red, fruity, aged mostly in large vats or bottle.
- **Tawny style** (Tawny, Reserve, 10 to 40 year age indications, Colheita): aged in wood, brownish, dried fruit and nuts.
- **White and rosé port**: lighter; white port is often mixed with tonic.

Other fortified wines you may see: Madeira (Portugal) and Marsala (Italy).

### Aperitifs and aperitivo bitters

An aperitif (אפריטיף, *aperitif*) is a drink served before a meal to open the appetite: dry vermouth, fino sherry, a Spritz, a light beer. Two groups matter most for cocktails:

- **Italian aperitivo bitters**: bitter-sweet red or orange liqueurs. The bright red, strongly bitter style is the base of the Negroni; the lighter, orange, less bitter style is the base of the Aperol Spritz (lesson 05.7). They differ a lot in alcohol: the light orange style is typically well under 15% ABV, the red bitter style considerably stronger. Check each label, as strengths differ by market.
- **Amari** (singular amaro): Italian herbal bitter liqueurs, often served after dinner as a digestif.
- **Quinquina and similar aromatised wines**: wine flavoured with quinine bark (the bitter in tonic), used in some classic cocktails.

### The storage rule: treat it like wine

Once opened, oxygen in the air slowly changes wine-based products (oxidation): fresh fruit and herbs fade, flavours turn flat, nutty or sour.

| Product | After opening (professional judgment; follow the producer's label) |
|---|---|
| Vermouth (all styles) | Close tightly, refrigerate, best within about a month; small bottles for slow sellers |
| Fino and manzanilla sherry | Refrigerate, use within days, like an open white wine |
| Amontillado, oloroso | Refrigerate; keep longer than fino, a few weeks |
| Ruby and LBV port | A few weeks, cool and closed |
| Tawny port | Keeps longer than ruby; still close and keep cool |
| Aperitivo bitters, amari (spirit-based) | Room temperature, like liqueurs |

Write the opening date on every opened bottle of vermouth or sherry with a marker or a strip of tape (professional judgment; many bars require it).

> [!NOTE]
> Vermouth's legal definition is from EU Regulation 251/2014; sherry and port facts from their regulatory councils' sites, checked 2026-10-17. Shelf-life times are professional judgment; producers' labels override them.

## Step by step

### How to choose the right vermouth from a spec

1. Read the spec: "dry vermouth" → the pale, dry bottle; "sweet vermouth" or "rosso" → the red bottle.
2. Check the opening date. Older than about a month, or smells flat or sour? Open a fresh bottle and tell the head bartender.
3. Pour from the fridge, close it, return it straight away.

### How to serve a glass of sherry or port

1. Fino or manzanilla: chilled, in a small wine glass, about 75–100 ml (professional judgment; follow the house pour).
2. Oloroso, PX, tawny or ruby port: slightly cool or at cellar temperature, same glass size.
3. Tell the guest the style: "This is a dry fino" or "This is a sweet Pedro Ximénez".

## Hands-on

See oxidation for yourself with alcohol-free juice and an apple, then practise the bar habit that prevents it: dating and storing opened bottles.

> [!CAUTION]
> You cut an apple with a knife. Cut on a board, fingers tucked, blade away from you.

### What you need

- A bottle or carton of white grape juice or clear apple juice, two small clean jars with lids (or two glasses and cling film), an apple.
- A paring knife, a cutting board, masking tape and a marker.
- Your fridge and a spot on the counter away from the sun.
- Optional: a bottle of vermouth that has been open more than a month, and a fresh one (smell only).
- Your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md) and the [tasting notes template](../../templates/tasting-notes.md).
- Low-cost alternative: skip the juice and use two apple slices only, one wrapped tightly in cling film in the fridge, one left open.

### Time

30 minutes of work spread over two days: 10 to set up, 10 at the 48-hour check, 10 for the labels and log.

### Steps

1. Pour 100 ml of juice into each jar. Fill jar A to the top and close it tightly; it goes in the fridge. Leave jar B half-empty and open (cover loosely with a paper towel against dust) on the counter.
2. Cut the apple in half. Wrap half A tightly in cling film and put it in the fridge; leave half B cut side up on the counter.
3. Label both jars and halves with masking tape: product, "opened" and the date and time, as you would in a bar.
4. After 1 hour, look at the apple halves and note the colour.
5. After 48 hours, compare jar A and jar B: colour, smell, and, if you wish, a small taste of A only. Do not taste B if it smells sour or fizzy.
6. Optional: smell a long-open vermouth next to a fresh one and write two words for each.
7. Write your observations, then write a one-line storage rule in your own words for vermouth and for fino sherry.

### What you should see

The open apple half browns within an hour; the wrapped one much less. After 48 hours, jar B is darker, smells less fresh, may smell slightly sour or yeasty and may even fizz a little; jar A in the fridge still smells fresh. That is oxygen and warmth at work. An opened vermouth or fino sherry on a warm back bar fades the same way, only more slowly because of its alcohol. If you compared vermouths, the old one smells flatter and less herbal, sometimes sour or like stale wine.

<details>
<summary>Model storage rules</summary>

Vermouth: "It's wine. Cap it, date it, fridge it, use it within about a month." Fino sherry: "Treat it like an open white wine: fridge, and finish within days."

</details>

### What this practice cannot teach

Juice oxidises faster than vermouth or sherry, so the demonstration exaggerates the speed; only a side-by-side smell of fresh and old products (optional) shows you the real change at the bar.

## Common mistakes

- **Keeping vermouth on the back bar for months.** It fades and sours. Fix: fridge, date label, about a month (or the producer's advice).
- **Mixing up dry and sweet.** Fix: read "dry" or "rosso/sweet" on every pour; never go by bottle colour alone, since some sweet bianco vermouths are pale.
- **Calling sherry "sweet stuff for grandmothers".** Most sherry styles are dry. Fix: ask "dry or sweet?" and name the style when you serve.
- **Pouring fino from a bottle open for two weeks.** Fix: treat fino and manzanilla like an open white wine.
- **Confusing bitters with aperitivo bitters.** Fix: bitters are dashed; aperitivo bitters are poured in ml as a main ingredient.

## Key takeaways

- Vermouth is an aromatised wine flavoured with wormwood, 14.5% to just under 22% ABV in the EU; dry for Martinis, sweet red for Manhattans and Negronis.
- Sherry is fortified wine from Jerez: fino and manzanilla age under flor and are dry and fragile; oloroso ages with air; PX is very sweet.
- Port is sweet fortified wine from the Douro, about 19–22% ABV, in ruby and tawny families.
- Aperitivo bitters and amari are spirit-based and keep at room temperature; their ABVs differ widely, so read the label.
- Once opened, wine-based products oxidise: close, date, refrigerate, and use within weeks (days for fino).
