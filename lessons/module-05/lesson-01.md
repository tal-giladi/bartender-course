# 05.1 · How to read, learn and remember a cocktail spec

A working bartender carries dozens of recipes in their head and pours them without looking anything up. Nobody is born with that memory: it is built from a clear written spec, a small number of patterns that most drinks share, and a habit of testing yourself instead of rereading. This lesson shows how to read an official spec from the International Bartenders Association (IBA), how to turn it into the recipe card used in this course, how the twelve drinks of this module fall into four simple templates, and how to learn them with flashcards and spaced repetition. In the hands-on part you write your twelve cards and run your first recall round.

## Why it matters

Your first trial shift. The head bartender says: "Two Negronis and a Daiquiri, please." If you have to check your phone, you have already lost the job. If you remember "equal parts" but not the garnish, or pour 30 ml of lime into the Daiquiri instead of 20, the drinks come back. Guests and managers do not grade you on what you read last week; they grade you on what comes out of your hands in the next two minutes. A spec you can recall instantly, in ml, with the method, glass and garnish, is the most basic tool of the job.

## Core ideas

### What a spec is

A **spec** (short for specification; in Israeli bars often מתכון, *matkon*, recipe) is the exact, written version of a drink: each ingredient with its amount, the method, the glass, the ice and the garnish. A bar keeps one spec per drink so that every bartender makes it the same way.

The **IBA** is the international association of bartender guilds. It publishes an official list of cocktails, each with a short spec in ml. This course uses the IBA spec as the default for every classic, because it is public, international and written in metric. The IBA groups its list into three categories: "The Unforgettables" (old classics), "Contemporary Classics" and "New Era" drinks.

### Reading an IBA spec

IBA pages are short and assume you already know the craft. Read them carefully:

- **Amounts.** Most are in ml. Some are not: "1 sugar cube", "few dashes", "2 bar spoons", "a splash", "top up with soda". Keep those as they are and learn the house amount your bar uses.
- **Brand names.** A few specs name a brand (the IBA Moscow Mule names Smirnoff vodka). Treat the brand as an example of the category, unless your bar says otherwise.
- **Method.** Short verbs: "stir", "shake", "build", "muddle", "strain", "top". You learned each one in Module 4 ([04.1](../module-04/lesson-01.md)).
- **Glass.** Sometimes named ("old fashioned glass", "wine glass"), sometimes just "chilled cocktail glass". The course card names the glass you will most likely use.
- **Garnish.** Read it literally: "squeeze oil from lemon peel" means express a twist, not drop a lemon wedge in.

### Specs differ, and that is normal

Famous drinks have more than one respected spec. The IBA Manhattan is 50 ml rye and 20 ml sweet vermouth; Difford's Guide, a large reference used by many bartenders, gives a bourbon-and-rye version with 30 ml vermouth. Neither is "wrong". The rule in this course: learn the IBA spec first, know the main variations by name and source, and **at work always use the house spec** (the bar's own written version). Never tell a guest or a colleague that their version is wrong.

### The course recipe card

Every drink in Modules 5 and 6 gets the same card, so you always know where to look:

| Line | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Ingredient table | Each ingredient in ml and oz (1 oz = 30 ml, the bar convention from [lesson 03.1](../module-03/lesson-01.md)); dashes, barspoons and splashes as written |
| Method | The technique, in order |
| Glass and ice | Which glass, chilled or not, and the ice in the glass (none, cubes, crushed, large cube) |
| Garnish | What goes on or in the drink |
| Profile and key technique | What it tastes like and the one movement that makes or breaks it |
| Spec source and variations | The IBA link and the main variations with their source |
| Substitutions | What to use when an ingredient is missing |
| Home-practice version | A water, tea or non-alcoholic build that trains the same volumes and movements |
| Alcohol-free analogue | A guest-ready alcohol-free drink in the same spirit, or "none that is faithful" |
| Common mistakes | What usually goes wrong |

### Four templates behind twelve drinks

Most drinks are built from a few parts. Naming them makes recall much easier, because you learn one pattern and then only the differences.

- **Base**: the main spirit, usually the largest amount (gin, rum, whiskey, vodka, tequila).
- **Modifier**: a smaller ingredient that changes the base's flavour (vermouth, a liqueur, Campari).
- **Sour and sweet**: citrus juice and sugar, which balance each other ([lesson 03.3](../module-03/lesson-03.md)).
- **Lengthener**: a long, usually fizzy mixer (soda, ginger beer, Prosecco).
- **Accent**: dashes of bitters, a twist's oil.

| Template | Pattern | Module 5 drinks | Usual method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spirit-forward stirred | base + modifier (+ bitters) | Martini, Manhattan, Negroni, Old Fashioned | stir |
| Sour | base + citrus + sweet (liqueur or syrup) | Daiquiri, Margarita, Whiskey Sour, Cosmopolitan | shake |
| Highball / long | base + citrus + long fizzy mixer | Mojito, Moscow Mule | build |
| Sparkling or coffee | aperitif + sparkling wine + soda; vodka + coffee liqueur + espresso | Aperol Spritz, Espresso Martini | build; shake |

Notice the method rule from [lesson 04.1](../module-04/lesson-01.md): drinks with only spirits and wines are stirred; drinks with citrus, cream, egg or coffee are shaken; drinks with a fizzy mixer are built in the glass.

### Memory: test yourself, then space it out

Two well-studied learning effects do most of the work:

- **Active recall** (the *testing effect*): trying to remember something from memory, then checking, makes it stick far better than reading it again. Studies going back over a century, including Roediger and Karpicke's 2006 experiments, found that taking a test beats extra study time for long-term memory.
- **Spaced repetition**: reviewing at growing intervals (today, in two days, in a week) works better than cramming. Hard items come back often, easy items less.

A simple way to do both is the **Leitner system**, a flashcard method from 1973: cards live in numbered boxes. You review box 1 every day, box 2 every two or three days, box 3 once a week. A card you recall perfectly moves up one box; a card you miss goes back to box 1. Your weak drinks get more practice without you deciding anything.

Two more habits help bartenders in particular:

- **Say it in order.** Recite every spec in the same fixed order: ingredients with ml (base first), method, glass and ice, garnish. The order becomes a checklist.
- **Interleave.** Mix templates in one session (a stirred drink, then a sour, then a highball) instead of all sours together. It feels harder but trains you to pick the right spec under pressure.

## Step by step

### Turn an IBA spec into a course card

1. Open the drink's IBA page. Copy each ingredient and its amount in the order given.
2. Convert ml to oz at 30 ml per oz (45 ml = 1½ oz, 20 ml = ⅔ oz, 15 ml = ½ oz). Keep dashes, cubes and splashes as written.
3. Rewrite the method as short verbs in order: "stir with ice, strain".
4. Name the glass and the ice in the glass. "Chilled cocktail glass" with no ice is a coupe or martini glass, served "up" (without ice).
5. Write the garnish exactly.
6. Mark the template (stirred, sour, highball, sparkling/coffee) and circle the base, modifier and balancing parts.
7. Add the main variation you know of, with its source. Never mix two specs on one card.

### Recite a spec

Use the same order every time. Example, Negroni:

1. "Gin 30, Campari 30, sweet vermouth 30."
2. "Build in a chilled old fashioned glass with ice, stir gently."
3. "Garnish half an orange slice."

### Run a Leitner session

1. Shuffle the cards in today's boxes. Look only at the name.
2. Say or write the full spec in the fixed order.
3. Turn the card. Score it: every ml exactly right (±5 ml), method, glass and garnish all correct = pass.
4. Pass: move it up one box. Miss: back to box 1, and write what you missed on the card's recall log.
5. Stop after 15 minutes. Short and frequent beats long and rare.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- 12 index cards or paper cut to size, a pen, three envelopes or small boxes labelled 1, 2 and 3.
- The printable cards in [labs/module-05/spec-flashcards.md](../../labs/module-05/spec-flashcards.md) (you may print them, but writing your own teaches more).
- A phone timer and your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: a notes app with one note per drink, or the recipe-drill simulation below.

### Time

45 minutes: 25 to write the cards, 15 for the first recall round, 5 to plan the review schedule.

### Steps

1. Write one card per drink for the twelve drinks of this module: name on the front; on the back, ingredients with ml, method, glass and ice, garnish and template. Copy from the IBA pages linked in lessons 05.2 to 05.7 or from the printable set.
2. Sort the cards into the four templates on the table. Say out loud what the drinks in each group share.
3. Put all twelve in box 1. Run one Leitner round as in the step-by-step above.
4. Record your score (cards passed out of 12) and the cards you missed in the practice log.
5. Plan the next reviews in your calendar: box 1 daily, box 2 every 2–3 days, box 3 weekly, until lesson 05.8.
6. Optional: run the simulation for one round and compare it with your paper score.

### What you should see

On a first round, before the drink lessons, most learners pass 2–4 cards: usually the Negroni (equal parts is easy) and drinks they already know as guests. That is expected. The misses cluster: ml of the smaller ingredients (15 vs 20 ml), garnish, and stirred vs shaken. After a week of short daily sessions, most cards should sit in box 2 or 3.

### What this practice cannot teach

At a real bar you learn the house specs and their small differences from the IBA, and you recall them while talking to guests and with tickets piling up; flashcards train the memory, shifts train it under pressure.

- [ ] I wrote twelve recipe cards in the course format.

## Common mistakes

- **Rereading instead of recalling.** Looking at the card feels like learning but tests nothing. Fix: always say the spec first, then turn the card.
- **Learning ratios only.** "2 to 1 to 1" is useful, but the exam and the job want ml. Fix: say the ml every time.
- **Mixing specs.** Taking the vermouth from one source and the whiskey from another gives a drink nobody wrote. Fix: one card per source; the IBA card is the default.
- **Skipping glass and garnish.** A correct liquid in the wrong glass is still sent back. Fix: recite all four parts in the fixed order.
- **Cramming the night before.** It fades within days. Fix: 10–15 minutes daily with the boxes.

## Key takeaways

- A spec is the exact written drink: ingredients in ml, method, glass and ice, garnish.
- The IBA spec is this course's default; learn variations by name and source, and use the house spec at work.
- Twelve drinks fit four templates: spirit-forward stirred, sour, highball, sparkling or coffee.
- Test yourself (active recall) and space reviews out (Leitner boxes) instead of rereading.
- Recite every spec in the same order so nothing is forgotten.
