# 08.6 · Commercial bar equipment and safety

A home station has a fridge, a freezer and a sink. A commercial bar adds machines that wash glasses at near-boiling temperatures, make hundreds of kilos of ice, carbonate water from a gas cylinder, push beer from kegs in a cold room and record every sale. Each one speeds up service, and each one can hurt someone or make guests ill if it is used or cleaned badly. This lesson walks through the main machines, the daily checks each needs, and the safety rules for gas, heavy kegs, hot water, chemicals and wet floors. In the hands-on part you find and fix the hazards in a described bar and write a daily-check card for each machine.

## Why it matters

A barback goes down to the beer cellar to change a keg and does not come back. A colleague finds him on the floor and runs in to help, and collapses too. Carbon dioxide from a leaking cylinder has filled the low, closed room, and it has no smell and no colour. Incidents like this are why many venues have gas alarms and strict cellar rules. Less dramatic, much more common: a hand burned on a glass straight out of the washer, a back injured lifting a keg alone, a guest's drink served with ice scooped by a glass that cracked. You will use this equipment from your first shift, usually before anyone has time to explain it, so know the rules before you start.

## Core ideas

> [!WARNING]
> Machines, gas, chemicals and heavy loads can cause serious injury. On a real shift, use equipment only after the venue has shown you how, follow the maker's instructions and the venue's procedures, and never repair, open or bypass a machine yourself. Israeli workplace-safety and food-business requirements are in Module 9.

### The glasswasher

A **glasswasher** (מדיח, *mediach*) washes and sanitises a rack of glasses in about two minutes. There are two kinds:

- **High-temperature machines** sanitise with a very hot final rinse. Under the U.S. Food Code (section 4-501.112, as adopted in Minnesota's rules), the rinse entering the machine's manifold must be at least **82 °C** for most machines (**74 °C** for a stationary-rack, single-temperature machine) and not above **90 °C**.
- **Low-temperature machines** sanitise with a chemical sanitiser instead, at the concentration on the product label.

Daily: check the wash and rinse temperature (or the sanitiser level) on the display at opening; keep detergent and rinse-aid containers labelled and connected; clean the filters and drain the tank at close. Safety: let racks cool before you handle glasses, never put hot glasses straight into ice service (a hot glass can crack in contact with ice, and it warms the drink; professional judgment), never reach into a running machine, and wear gloves to change chemical containers. Chipped glasses do not go back into service ([lesson 01.5](../module-01/lesson-05.md)).

### The ice machine

The **ice machine** (מכונת קרח, *mechonat kerach*) freezes water into cubes and drops them into a storage bin. Ice is food: it goes into the guest's drink. Rules:

- Take ice only with a clean scoop, never with a glass or your hands; keep the scoop in its holder outside the ice ([lesson 01.6](../module-01/lesson-06.md)).
- Nothing is stored in the ice bin: no bottles, no fruit, no drinks to chill.
- Keep the bin door closed. Coca-Cola's bar gun guide says to clean the ice scoop and its holder daily.
- The machine itself is descaled and sanitised on the maker's schedule. Parts Town, a parts supplier, describes the Manitowoc schedule as cleaning and sanitising every **six months**; manufacturers' guides say hard water or heavy use needs it more often. This is usually done by a technician or a trained manager, not by a new bartender.
- If the ice looks cloudy, smells, has black specks or slime around the bin, tell the manager and stop using it.

### The soda gun

A **soda gun** (bar gun) mixes carbonated water with syrups from boxes under the bar, giving soda, tonic and cola from one nozzle ([lesson 03.6](../module-03/lesson-06.md)). It runs on CO2 and a carbonator. Sugar builds up quickly on the nozzles: Coca-Cola's guide gives a **daily** clean of nozzles, diffuser, holster and sheathing with sanitiser and a dedicated brush. At opening, run soda for a few seconds and taste each button: flat or warm soda usually means an empty CO2 cylinder, an empty syrup box or a carbonator fault, and you report it.

### The POS

The **POS** (point of sale, קופה, *kupa*) is the till and order system. It sends tickets from servers to the bar, records every sale, and its numbers feed the stock reconciliation in [lesson 08.4](lesson-04.md). Basic rules (professional judgment): ring a drink in before or as you make it, never after; use modifiers ("double", "no ice", "alcohol-free") so the ticket and the stock are right; voids and free drinks need the manager's approval and a reason; keep your login to yourself and log out when you leave the terminal.

### The draught system

Draught beer is pushed from **kegs** in a cold room through lines to the taps by gas (CO2 or a CO2 and nitrogen mix). Module 7 covers pouring. Operations you should know about: the Brewers Association's Draught Beer Quality Manual (2019) says lines should be cleaned with a caustic line cleaner **every two weeks**, with faucets taken apart and cleaned at the same time and an acid clean every three months. Line cleaning uses strong chemicals and is done by trained staff or a line-cleaning service; a new bartender's job is to know when it was last done and to report sour, cloudy or foaming beer.

### CO2 cylinders and cellar gas

> [!CAUTION]
> Carbon dioxide can kill without warning in a cellar or cold room. If a gas alarm sounds, do not go in, do not let anyone else go in, and do not try to rescue someone who has collapsed inside: get out, keep others out, raise the alarm and call emergency services (in Israel, 101 for ambulance and 102 for fire and rescue). Only trained, protected emergency personnel go in.

The Brewers Association manual describes CO2 as colourless and odourless, denser than air, and able to persist in low, enclosed spaces; at 10% in air it can cause unconsciousness or death, and lower levels cause headache, sweating, rapid breathing, dizziness and confusion. WorkSafe Victoria, an Australian workplace-safety regulator, sets out the controls for cellars and cool rooms:

- cylinders stored **upright** and **secured** with a chain or bracket, with as few spare cylinders as needed;
- a **gas monitor and alarm**, with sound and light inside the cellar and at the entrance;
- leak tests of cylinders and lines with bubble solution **weekly**, and a new washer and a leak test at **every cylinder change**;
- warning signs at the entrance, routine access only for trained people, and emergency procedures every staff member knows.

The manual adds: keep the protective cap on cylinders until use and never let them get hot. Changing a cylinder is a trained task; do it only after the venue has shown you.

### Kegs and heavy loads

The Government of Ontario's guidance gives a full 50–58.6 litre keg as about **61–73 kg**. Lifting, lowering or carrying kegs causes back and shoulder injuries, and two people lifting together still risk injury. Its controls: use **keg dollies or lifting devices**; without them, **roll, push, pull or slide** kegs instead of lifting; store kegs so they do not need to be lifted from the floor or stacked; follow written procedures. The same applies, on a smaller scale, to cases of bottles and full ice buckets: carry what you can hold close to your body, and ask for help or a trolley for the rest.

### Everyday hazards

| Hazard | Control |
|---|---|
| Wet, sticky floor behind the bar | Non-slip shoes, rubber mats, mop spills at once, "wet floor" sign |
| Broken glass, especially in ice | Call "glass!", stop, burn (melt out) and empty the whole ice well, clean, refill ([lesson 01.6](../module-01/lesson-06.md)) |
| Hot water, steam from the glasswasher | Open the door and step back; let racks cool |
| Cleaning chemicals | Labelled, below and away from ice, glass and garnish; never mixed ([lesson 01.7](../module-01/lesson-07.md)) |
| Electricity near water | Dry hands on plugs and switches; report damaged cables (professional judgment) |
| Knives | As in [lesson 01.4](../module-01/lesson-04.md): board on a damp cloth, call "knife" when carrying |

> [!NOTE]
> Temperatures, cleaning intervals and gas controls are from the sources named, checked 2026-10-17; they are U.S., Australian and Canadian guidance and manufacturers' schedules, not Israeli law. Machine models, chemicals and procedures differ between venues: always follow the maker's manual and the venue's training.

## Step by step

### Daily equipment checks at opening

1. Glasswasher: filled, heated; rinse temperature (or sanitiser level) on the display within range; chemicals connected; filters in place.
2. Ice machine: bin full or filling; ice clear, no smell, no specks; scoop clean and in its holder; bin door closed.
3. Soda gun: run each button a few seconds and taste; nozzles clean; holster clean.
4. POS: on, logged in, menu and prices correct, ticket printer has paper.
5. Draught (if you are trained for it): taps pour clear, cold beer; note the date of the last line clean.
6. Cellar: gas alarm shows normal before you enter; cylinders upright and chained.
7. Floor: mats down, no spills, walkways clear of cases and kegs.
8. Write anything wrong in the handover or maintenance log and tell the shift manager.

### If the cellar gas alarm sounds

1. Do not enter. If you are inside, leave at once.
2. Keep everyone out; do not go back in for anyone.
3. Tell the shift manager; call emergency services (101 ambulance, 102 fire and rescue in Israel) if anyone is inside or unwell.
4. Ventilate only from outside if the venue's procedure says how; otherwise wait for trained help.

### Moving a keg

1. Use the keg dolly or lift if there is one.
2. Without one: roll the keg on its bottom rim, slide or push it; never carry it.
3. Never lift a keg from the floor to a height alone; ask for help and a lifting aid.
4. Keep the route clear and dry; wear closed shoes.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- The worksheet [labs/module-08/equipment-hazard-walk.md](../../labs/module-08/equipment-hazard-walk.md).
- A pen, and 10 minutes at home with your own fridge, freezer and sink area.
- Optional: if you know someone who works in a bar, ask to see the back area and the ice machine before service (never enter a cellar or cold room without the staff's permission and instructions).
- Low-cost alternative: everything here is on paper.

### Time

45 minutes: 20 for the hazard walk, 15 for the daily-check cards, 10 for the home check.

### Steps

1. **Hazard walk.** Read the description of a bar's back area on the worksheet. List every hazard you find (there are twelve), say why it is dangerous and what you would do.
2. **Daily-check cards.** Write a five-line daily-check card for the glasswasher, the ice machine and the soda gun, in your own words, from the lesson.
3. **Home check.** Apply the same eyes to your practice space: is the floor dry, are chemicals away from food and ice, are cables away from water, is anything heavy stored high? Fix what you find and write it in your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
4. Compare the hazard walk with the solution.

### What you should see

Most learners find eight to ten of the twelve hazards on the first read; the ones usually missed are the glass used as an ice scoop on the ice machine (it looks like a habit, not a hazard), bottles chilling in the ice bin, and the colleague who wants to go back into the cellar. Your daily-check cards should each name a check, what "normal" looks like and who to tell if it is not normal. At home, the most common finds are cleaning products next to food and a wet floor near the sink.

### What this practice cannot teach

Reading about a glasswasher, a cellar or a keg is not the same as using one: real machines, chemicals and gas need hands-on training at the venue, and this lesson prepares you to ask the right questions and recognise danger, not to operate equipment untrained.

- [ ] I found the hazards in the hazard walk and wrote what I would do for each.
- [ ] I wrote daily-check cards for the glasswasher, ice machine and soda gun.
- [ ] I can say what to do, and what not to do, when a cellar gas alarm sounds.
- [ ] I checked my own practice space and fixed what I found.

## Common mistakes

- **Scooping ice with a glass "just once".** A chipped rim leaves glass in the ice. Fix: scoop only, every time.
- **Chilling bottles in the ice bin.** Dirty bottles contaminate ice that goes into drinks. Fix: nothing but ice in the bin.
- **Running into a cellar to help.** Two casualties instead of one. Fix: stay out, raise the alarm, call for help.
- **Lifting a keg alone.** Back injuries are common and lasting. Fix: dolly, lift, or roll and slide.
- **Ignoring flat soda or a cold glasswasher.** Fix: they are signs of a fault; report them at opening.

## Key takeaways

- Glasswashers sanitise with a hot final rinse (at least 82 °C for most high-temperature machines under the U.S. Food Code) or with chemicals; check the display daily.
- Ice is food: scoop only, nothing stored in the bin, scoop and holder cleaned daily, machine sanitised on the maker's schedule.
- Clean soda gun nozzles and holster daily; taste each button at opening.
- CO2 is invisible, odourless and heavier than air: cylinders upright and chained, alarms obeyed, never enter or re-enter a gassed cellar.
- Move kegs with aids or by rolling and sliding, never by lifting alone.
