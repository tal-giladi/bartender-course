# 08.7 · Communication and teamwork on shift

Behind a busy bar, two or three people work in a space a metre wide, carrying glass, ice and knives, while servers shout orders over music and guests wave from three directions. What keeps it safe and fast is a small set of habits: short call-outs everyone understands, orders repeated back before anything is poured, early and specific requests to the barback, an 86 list that reaches every server, and clean handovers. This lesson teaches those habits in English and in the Hebrew you will hear in Israeli bars. In the hands-on part you drill the call-outs and the check-back with a partner or a recording, and write an 86 announcement and a mid-shift handover.

## Why it matters

A bartender turns with a tray of clean glasses just as a colleague steps behind her to reach the speed rail. Nobody said "behind". Six glasses on the floor, the ice well burned, five minutes lost and a cut hand. Ten minutes later a server calls "two Negronis, one without ice" across the bar; the bartender hears "two Negronis", makes both on ice, and both come back. Neither mistake was about skill. Both were about communication, and both are what a head bartender notices on your trial shift.

## Core ideas

### Who you talk to during service

- **Other bartenders** on the same bar: share space, bottles and tools.
- **The barback**: restocks ice, glass, bottles, juice and garnish; clears empties ([lesson 01.1](../module-01/lesson-01.md)).
- **Servers**: bring tickets from tables, collect drinks at the **pass** (the spot where finished drinks wait to be collected).
- **The shift manager**: decides on 86s, comps, problems with guests, and anything unsafe.
- **Guests**: Module 9 covers greeting, ordering and refusing service; here the focus is the team.

### Call-outs

A **call-out** is a short, loud, standard phrase that tells people around you what you are doing, so they do not walk into you. Use it every time, even when it feels obvious. Hebrew usage below is common Israeli bar and kitchen usage (professional judgment, 2026-10-17).

| Situation | English | Hebrew (transliteration) |
|---|---|---|
| Passing behind someone | "Behind!" | מאחוריך / מאחורייך (*me'achorecha* / *me'achorayich*) |
| Coming round a blind corner | "Corner!" | פינה (*pina*) |
| Carrying something hot (glasswasher rack, kettle) | "Hot!" or "Hot behind!" | חם (*cham*) |
| Carrying a knife | "Knife!" | סכין (*sakin*) |
| Something broke | "Glass!" | זכוכית (*zchuchit*) |
| Squeezing past | "Excuse me, coming through" | סליחה, עובר / עוברת (*slicha, over / overet*) |
| I heard you and will do it | "Heard!" | קיבלתי (*kibalti*) |
| Item is finished | "86 [item]" | נגמר [item] (*nigmar*) |

"Glass!" means everyone stops scooping from that well until it is burned and refilled ([lesson 01.6](../module-01/lesson-06.md)).

### Check-back: closing the loop

Health-care teams use **closed-loop communication** (also called check-back) for instructions that must not be misheard: the sender gives the message to a named person, the receiver repeats it back, and the sender confirms it (American Hospital Association, TeamSTEPPS). A bar uses the same three steps for spoken orders and requests:

1. **Send:** "Dana, two Negronis, one no ice, for table 4."
2. **Repeat back:** "Two Negronis, one no ice, table 4."
3. **Confirm:** "Yes." (or the correction: "No, one on the rocks, one up.")

Repeat the drink, the number, every **modifier** (no ice, up, double, alcohol-free, no salt, an allergy) and where it goes. Then ring it in on the POS straight away ([lesson 08.6](lesson-06.md)) so the ticket, the stock and the bill all match. If the noise means you are not sure, ask again: one more sentence is faster than a remake.

### Asking the barback

A good request is **specific** (what, how much, where), **early** (before you run out, [lesson 08.4](lesson-04.md)) and **checked back**:

- Weak: "We need limes!"
- Strong: "Yossi, one bottle of lime juice and a rack of highballs to station 2, please." "Lime juice and highballs to 2." "Yes, thanks."

In the service simulation, asking the barback costs you 3 seconds and the item arrives a minute later; going yourself costs 45 seconds of your time. That difference is the whole reason bars have barbacks.

### The 86 list

To **86** an item means it is finished for the night (bar and restaurant slang; its origin is uncertain, Wikipedia lists several theories). When something runs out with no backup:

1. Tell the shift manager.
2. The manager (or you, if that is the house rule) tells every server and marks the item on the 86 board; the POS item is switched off if the system allows.
3. Tell guests honestly and offer something you can make.
4. Take it off the list when stock arrives, and tell everyone again.

An 86 that reaches the bar but not the servers just moves the problem to the table: a guest orders, waits, and is told ten minutes later that the drink does not exist.

### Sharing a station

Two bartenders on one well stay fast when they agree on small things (professional judgment): every bottle goes back to the same spot after each pour; nobody takes a tin, jigger or strainer another person is using; you say "I've got the next ticket" before starting it; on a big round, you split it by passes ("I'll do spirits, you do ice and tops") rather than by drinks. Problems between colleagues are sorted out after service, not in front of guests.

### Mistakes and handovers

If you make a mistake (wrong drink, spill, broken glass), say so at once, fix it, and log it ([lesson 08.4](lesson-04.md)). Hiding a mistake turns it into a mystery for the whole team. When you go on a break or hand your station to someone, tell them: open tickets and what is half-made, open tabs, anything 86'd, what prep is low, and any guest to watch (for example someone who has had enough to drink; Module 9).

> [!NOTE]
> The check-back method is from health-care team training (AHA, TeamSTEPPS), adapted to the bar. Call-out wording and Hebrew usage are professional judgment and vary by venue; learn the house words on your first shift (checked 2026-10-17).

## Step by step

### Take a spoken order

1. Look at the person; listen to the whole order.
2. Repeat it back: number, drink, every modifier, destination.
3. Wait for "yes" or a correction.
4. Ring it in on the POS at once.
5. Say "heard" or "two minutes" so they know it is coming.

### Announce an 86

1. Check there really is no backup (storeroom, other station).
2. Tell the shift manager: "We are out of Aperol, no backup."
3. Servers and the 86 board get the message; the POS item goes off.
4. Offer alternatives to the guests who ordered it.

### Hand over mid-shift

1. Finish or clearly mark half-made drinks.
2. Tell your colleague: open tickets, open tabs, 86 list, low prep, guests to watch.
3. They repeat back the key points.
4. Wash your hands before you leave and when you come back.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- The drill sheet [labs/module-08/call-out-cards.md](../../labs/module-08/call-out-cards.md): 12 call-out situations, 8 spoken orders and two writing tasks.
- A partner (a friend or family member), or a phone to record yourself.
- Paper and pen, a timer, your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: if you practise alone, record the eight orders yourself, wait at least a few hours, then play them back in random order with background music on.

### Time

40 minutes: 10 for call-outs, 15 for the check-back drill, 15 to write the 86 announcement and the handover.

### Steps

1. **Call-outs.** Your partner reads a situation card at random; you say the call-out in English and in Hebrew, loudly, within 2 seconds. Go through all 12 twice. Then move around your kitchen carrying a tray (empty or with plastic cups) while your partner moves too, and use the calls for real.
2. **Check-back.** Put music on at a normal party volume. Your partner reads the eight orders from across the room, one at a time, at normal speed. For each: repeat it back, wait for "yes" or a correction, then write it down as you would ring it in (drink, number, modifiers, destination).
3. Score yourself: an order counts as correct only if every number, drink, modifier and destination is right **after** the check-back. Then count how many your repeat-back caught that you would otherwise have got wrong.
4. **86 announcement.** Write the exact words you would use to the shift manager and to a server when the Aperol runs out at 21:30.
5. **Handover.** Write a mid-shift handover for a colleague taking your station: two open tickets, one open tab, the Aperol 86, low lime juice, and a guest at seat 3 who has had several drinks.
6. Compare with the model answers below and log your scores.

### What you should see

Call-outs feel silly for the first few minutes and automatic by the second round; aim for every call within 2 seconds. In the check-back drill with music on, most learners mishear or forget one or two modifiers in eight orders, and the repeat-back catches most of them: that is the point of the method. A target for this drill is 8 of 8 correct after check-back (professional judgment). If you missed one even after repeating, you probably repeated what you expected to hear rather than what was said: slow down and repeat the modifiers first.

### What this practice cannot teach

A kitchen with music is not a packed bar at midnight, and a friend reading orders is not a stressed server; the drill builds the habit of calling out and checking back so that it is already automatic when the real noise starts.

### Completion checklist

- [ ] I can give all 12 call-outs in English and Hebrew within 2 seconds.
- [ ] I did the check-back drill with background noise and scored my orders after check-back.
- [ ] I wrote an 86 announcement for the manager and for a server.
- [ ] I wrote a mid-shift handover that covers tickets, tabs, 86s, prep and guests to watch.

## Common mistakes

- **Silent movement behind the bar.** Collisions with glass and knives. Fix: call "behind", "corner", "hot", "knife" every time.
- **"Yeah, got it" instead of a repeat-back.** You confirm what you think you heard. Fix: repeat the number, drink, every modifier and the destination.
- **Vague barback requests at the last second.** Fix: specific item, amount and station, asked early.
- **An 86 that stays at the bar.** Servers keep selling it. Fix: manager, servers, board and POS, every time.
- **Hiding a mistake.** Fix: say it, fix it, log it.

## Key takeaways

- Call out every movement that can surprise someone: behind, corner, hot, knife, glass.
- Use check-back for spoken orders and requests: send, repeat back, confirm; then ring it in.
- Ask the barback early and specifically; 3 seconds of asking saves 45 seconds of fetching.
- An 86 must reach the manager, the servers, the board and the POS.
- Hand over with tickets, tabs, 86s, low prep and guests to watch, and sort out conflicts after service.
