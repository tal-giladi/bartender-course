# 09.5 · Israeli law: age verification, alcohol sale and licensing

This lesson is the legal core of the course. It sets out, with official citations, what Israeli law says about serving alcohol to minors and to drunk guests, when ID is checked and why, which sales are banned at night, what a bar's business licence requires, and what the smoking law asks of bar staff. Each rule is labelled as statutory (in a law or regulation), a licence condition (binding on a licensed business through its licence), official guidance, or house policy, and the research date is 18 October 2026. It is a summary for working bartenders, not legal advice. In the hands-on part you decide twelve legal cases, practise ID checks with fictional cards, and run the possible-minor scenario.

## Why it matters

Two young men order beers at 22:00. One looks about 17. You are busy, they seem nice, and his friend says "He's with me, he's 19." If he is 17 and you serve him, Israeli law presumes you knew he was a minor unless you can prove he showed you an ID saying he was an adult. Police use trained minors to test bars, with their parents' consent (State Comptroller, 2016), and a suspected sale to a minor can get the bar closed by a police order for up to 15 days. Serving or recommending alcohol to a possible minor without an ID check is a safety-critical failure in this course's final assessment (lesson 11.1).

## Core ideas

### Four kinds of rules

| Kind | What it is | Example | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Statutory** | a law of the Knesset or a regulation under it | no sale to minors (Penal Law s. 193A) | Sefer HaHukim, Nevo consolidated texts |
| **Licence condition** | a condition in the bar's business licence, binding on the business | the minors sign, CCTV over the bar | the uniform specification (מפרט אחיד, *mifrat achid*) for the licensing item, published on gov.il |
| **Official guidance** | advice from a ministry or authority, not itself binding | signs of intoxication, first aid | Ministry of Health, NHS, NSW (as used in lessons 09.3–09.4) |
| **House policy** | the venue's own rules | "we check ID for anyone who looks under 30" | your manager |

### Minors: the statutory rules

A **minor** (קטין, *katin*) is a person under 18. Penal Law section 193A (as amended in 2010):

| Subsection | Rule | Maximum penalty |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | encouraging or inducing a minor to drink alcohol | 3 months' imprisonment |
| (a1) | selling alcohol to a minor | 6 months |
| (a2) | supplying alcohol to a minor, or buying it for a minor, in a public place, unless in the presence and with the consent of the person responsible for the minor (parent, guardian) or within the family circle | 6 months |
| (b) | the owner or a worker of a business where alcohol is sold for drinking on the premises may not sell or serve alcohol to a minor or encourage a minor to drink there | 6 months |
| (c) | the owner or a worker may ask anyone who wants to be served alcohol to show a document (תעודה, *te'uda*) from which their age can be checked | a power, not a penalty |
| (c1) | if a minor was sold, served or supplied alcohol by the owner or a worker, they are **presumed to have known** it was a minor, unless they prove (on the civil standard) that the minor showed them a document saying they were not a minor | presumption |
| (d)–(e) | a business selling alcohol must display a sign in the form set by regulation; not displaying it counts as breaching a licence condition | licence enforcement |

What this means behind the bar:

- **The ID is your defence.** Under (c1), "he looked 20" is not a defence; "he showed me an ID that said he was 20" is.
- **Parents cannot order for a minor at the bar.** The family-circle exception is in subsection (a2) on supply in public places; subsection (b) for businesses that serve alcohol on the premises contains no such exception in the text checked. Serve the teenager a soft drink.
- **Encouraging counts.** Offering a minor a "taste" or a free shot is an offence even without a sale.

### Licence conditions about minors and staff

A pub or bar whose main activity is serving alcohol is licensed under item 4.8 of the Business Licensing Order (2013); a restaurant that also serves alcohol is licensed under item 4.2. The Israel Police conditions published for both items include (licence conditions):

- **No one under 18 may be employed selling alcohol.** That is one reason this course is for adults.
- **A sign above the entrances**, 50 × 40 cm, rigid (not cardboard), black on white, 1.80–2.20 m from the floor, lit when dark, saying that selling or serving alcohol to anyone under 18 is forbidden under section 193A of the Penal Law.
- **"Anyone who wants to drink must show an ID document from which their age can be verified."** In a licensed bar, this turns the law's power to ask into a condition of the licence.
- For item 4.8: CCTV including a camera over the bar, a maximum-occupancy sign outside ("the business is intended for ___ visitors") and no more guests than that number; at outdoor or temporary events, all drinks in soft plastic cups.

### Checking ID

The law does not list which documents count; it says a document from which age can be verified. A safe working standard (professional judgment; follow house policy):

- **Accept** a physical, valid, government-issued document with a photo and date of birth: Israeli ID card (תעודת זהות, *te'udat zehut*), driving licence, passport.
- **Do not rely on** a photo of an ID on a phone, a student card, or a friend's word. They are easy to fake or borrow, and they may not count as the guest "showing a document" if something goes wrong.
- **Who:** everyone in the group who will drink and who looks younger than the house age (often 25 or 30), every time, even regulars you are unsure about.
- **Cut-off date:** subtract 18 years from today's date. On 18 October 2026, anyone born on or before 18 October 2008 is 18 or over; anyone born on 19 October 2008 or later is a minor. Many bars write the day's cut-off date by the POS.

The check takes about 15 seconds:

1. Ask politely: "Can I see your ID, please?" / אפשר לראות תעודה מזהה, בבקשה? (*efshar lir'ot te'uda mezaha, bevakasha?*).
2. Take it in your hand, out of any wallet or cover.
3. Compare the photo with the face (eyes, nose, ears, chin), not the hair.
4. Read the date of birth and compare it with the cut-off date.
5. Check it is not expired and has not been altered (raised edges, scratched digits, a different font).
6. If in doubt, ask a simple question the real owner answers instantly (their full date of birth, or their surname's spelling) or call the manager.
7. Hand it back. Do not photograph or keep it (professional judgment).

If there is no valid ID or you are not sure: refuse alcohol politely and offer a soft drink. "Sorry, without ID I can't serve alcohol. It's the law. I can make you something without alcohol." Log ID refusals if the house asks ([incident log](../../labs/module-09/incident-log.md)).

### Alcohol-free drinks and minors

The Licensing of Businesses Law defines an intoxicating drink (משקה משכר, *mashke meshaker*) as a fermented or alcoholic drink containing 2% ABV or more. By that definition a 0.0% beer is not an intoxicating drink. This course did not confirm that the Penal Law uses exactly the same line, and many venues do not serve "beer" of any kind to minors as house policy (professional judgment). Ask your manager, and never serve a drink with any alcohol to a minor. This answers the open question in [lesson 07.5](../module-07/lesson-05.md).

> [!CAUTION]
> **Unconfirmed legal point.** The course could not confirm whether the Penal Law uses the same 2% ABV line as the Licensing of Businesses Law (checked 2026-10-18). Do not rely on it: treat every drink that contains any alcohol, including "alcohol-free" beer up to 0.5%, as off limits for minors, and ask your manager or a lawyer for the current rule.

### The 23:00–06:00 sale ban

Licensing of Businesses Law section 2(a1) (statutory): a business selling alcohol may not sell, serve or supply alcohol from 23:00 to 06:00. The exemptions:

- Businesses listed in the **First Schedule**, for drinking **on the premises**: restaurants, cafés and catering; businesses selling alcohol served for drinking on the premises (bars, pubs); hotels (unless mainly for minors); venues for shows, cinemas, theatres, discotheques, outdoor events; event halls and gardens.
- Licensed duty-free warehouses for travellers.
- Wine in a large food store (licensed area ≥ 280 m²) from 23:00 to 02:00 on Thursdays and the day before a holiday eve, if the same purchase includes at least 300 ILS of other goods.

For a bartender this means: drinks served at the bar for drinking there are not affected, but **selling a closed bottle or a drink to take away after 23:00 is not covered by the exemption** (it is not for drinking on the premises). Your bar's own opening hours come from its licence and local bylaws; this course found no national closing time for bars.

### Police powers

- A police officer of the rank of רב-פקד (*rav-pakad*, chief inspector) or above may order a business closed for **up to 15 days** on reasonable suspicion of an offence under Penal Law section 193A or the night-sale ban, if the business received a written warning in the previous two years; for a repeat within three years, up to 30 days without a new warning (Licensing of Businesses Law s. 23(a1)–(a2)). Up to 30 days is also possible to keep public order (s. 23(a)). In 2014 the police held 1,196 hearings against businesses that sold alcohol to minors and closed 630 of them (State Comptroller, 2016).
- The 2010 law also gave police the power to seize or pour out alcohol drunk in a public place (not inside licensed bars, restaurants and venues) when it may disturb public order, and from minors drinking without a responsible adult. Those powers were enacted as a temporary provision; this course did not confirm their current status (checked 2026-10-18). Either way, most bars do not let guests leave with open glasses (house policy).
- Supplying alcohol to a drunk person is covered by section 193(c), see [lesson 09.4](lesson-04.md).

> [!CAUTION]
> **Unconfirmed legal point.** The 2010 police power to seize or pour out alcohol in public places was a temporary provision, and the course could not confirm whether it is still in force (checked 2026-10-18). Do not quote it to guests or police as current law; ask your manager or a lawyer.

### Smoking

The Prevention of Smoking in Public Places and Exposure to Smoking Law (statutory) bans smoking in the places in its Schedule. Item 11 covers restaurants, cafés, bars (בית משקה, *beit mashke*), clubs and discotheques. Smoking is allowed only in a **completely separate room** designated by the management, with proper ventilation, not disturbing the rest of the venue, and no larger than a quarter of the public area; an uncovered outdoor smoking area must be either up to 15 m² or contiguous, no more than a quarter of the outdoor seating, with the rest clearly marked no-smoking. Holding a lit or activated smoking product in a non-smoking area is also banned.

The **holder** of the place (in practice the owner, the manager and staff acting for them) must post no-smoking signs, may not put out ashtrays in non-smoking areas, and must supervise and do everything possible to prevent smoking. The legal defence is to show that you asked the smoker to stop (or took reasonable steps) and, if they continued, complained to an inspector or tried to. So when a guest lights up at the bar: "Sorry, smoking is only allowed in the smoking area. It's the law." If they refuse, tell the manager.

## Step by step

### Check ID and refuse a possible minor

1. Ask everyone in the group who will drink for ID if any of them looks under the house age.
2. Physical, valid, photo ID only; take it in your hand.
3. Photo against face; date of birth against today's cut-off; expiry; signs of tampering.
4. Hand it back; serve if 18+.
5. No ID or a doubt: "Sorry, without ID I can't serve alcohol. It's the law." Offer a soft drink.
6. Do not serve the group a drink that will be passed to the minor; tell the manager if you suspect it.

### When a guest lights a cigarette indoors

1. Ask politely and at once: smoking only in the smoking area.
2. Point to the area or the door.
3. If they continue, tell the manager (the holder's legal defence includes complaining to an inspector).

2. **Cut-off dates.** Write today's cut-off date of birth (today minus 18 years) and the cut-off for the three other dates on the sheet.
3. **ID drill.** Time yourself on each of the ten fictional ID cards (assume today is the drill date on the sheet): serve or refuse, and why. Target: a correct decision in under 15 seconds per card.
4. **Simulate.** Run the possible-minor scenario. Aim for at least 6 of 8 points and no safety-critical choice.

5. Compare with the solution and log your score, your average time per ID and your simulation result.

### What you should see

Most learners get the minors cases right and slip on three: the parent ordering beer for a 16-year-old (not allowed in a bar), the closed bottle sold to take away at 23:30 (not covered by the bar's exemption), and the phone photo of an ID (do not rely on it) (professional judgment). In the ID drill, the dangerous cards are the ones born a day or two after the cut-off and the expired or mismatched ones; slow down on dates and speed up everything else.

### What this practice cannot teach

Paper cards cannot show a real document, a nervous teenager with a borrowed ID, or a group pressing you at a busy bar; follow your venue's ID policy from your first shift, ask the manager when unsure, and remember that real legal questions belong to a lawyer, not to a course.

## Common mistakes

- **"He looks old enough."** Under the presumption in s. 193A(c1), only an ID shown to you protects you. Fix: ID everyone who might be under the house age.
- **Letting a parent order for a teenager.** Fix: soft drinks for the minor; the bar-specific rule has no family exception.
- **Accepting a phone photo or a friend's word.** Fix: physical, valid, photo ID in your hand.
- **Thinking the 23:00 rule closes the bar.** Bars are exempt for drinking on the premises. Fix: remember the exemption does not cover takeaway sales.
- **Ignoring a smoker to avoid a scene.** The holder must act. Fix: ask politely at once; tell the manager if they continue.
- **Treating house policy as law, or law as optional.** Fix: know which rules are statutory, which are licence conditions and which are the venue's own.

## Key takeaways

- Selling or serving alcohol to anyone under 18 in a bar is an offence (Penal Law s. 193A); the seller is presumed to have known unless the minor showed an ID saying they were adult.
- Licence conditions for bars require a lit sign over the entrances, ID from anyone who wants to drink, and no staff under 18 selling alcohol.
- Check physical, valid, photo ID against today's cut-off date of birth; refuse politely without ID.
- The 23:00–06:00 ban does not apply to drinks served for drinking on the premises in bars and restaurants, but does apply to takeaway sales.
- Smoking is banned in bars except in a separate room or a set outdoor area; staff must ask smokers to stop and involve the manager.
