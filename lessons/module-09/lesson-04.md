# 09.4 · Responsible service: intoxication and refusing service

Serving alcohol is the job; serving it responsibly is the part of the job that protects guests, staff and the bar. In Israel it is also the law: the owner or a worker of a business that sells alcohol may not supply alcohol to a drunk person or encourage them to drink. This lesson teaches you to notice intoxication early, slow it down before it becomes a problem, refuse service calmly when you must, and handle the hard cases: the guest who wants "the strongest thing you've got", the guest who is about to drive, and the guest who may have alcohol poisoning. In the hands-on part you sort twelve guest descriptions, calculate the alcohol in five drinks, rehearse refusals on video and run two simulation scenarios.

## Why it matters

It is 01:00. A regular you like, who has tipped well all night, asks for "one more round, and shots for everyone". His words are slurred and he grabs the bar to stay upright. Serving him is easy and refusing him is awkward. But under section 193(c) of the Israeli Penal Law, supplying alcohol to a drunk person in a business that sells alcohol carries up to six months' imprisonment, and the person who pours is a "worker" under that section. More importantly, the next hour of his night (a fall, a fight, a car) may depend on what you say now. Continuing to serve a clearly intoxicated guest is a safety-critical failure in this course's final assessment (lesson 11.1).

## Core ideas

### What the law says (Israel)

| Rule | Type |
|---|---|
| The owner or a worker of a business where alcohol is sold who supplies alcohol to a drunk person, or encourages them to drink, is liable to 6 months' imprisonment (Penal Law s. 193(c)) | statutory |
| Disorderly behaviour in a public place while drunk: 3 months (s. 193(a)) | statutory (applies to the guest) |
| Selling or serving alcohol to a minor, or encouraging a minor to drink (s. 193A) | statutory; see [lesson 09.5](lesson-05.md) |

The section does not list signs of drunkenness, and this course found no Israeli official guide to recognising intoxication at a bar (searched 2026-10-18). The signs below come from official guidance in New South Wales, Australia, where the law treats a person as intoxicated when their speech, balance, coordination or behaviour is noticeably affected and alcohol is the likely cause. Use them as a practical standard, not as Israeli law.

### How alcohol builds up

Alcohol reaches the bloodstream from the first sip and its effects start within about 10 minutes; as the blood alcohol concentration rises, effects grow from lowered inhibitions and slurred speech to loss of coordination, confusion, memory problems and, at very high levels, breathing problems, coma and death (gov.il definitions page). How fast depends on the amount, the person's size and health, food, medication and how quickly they drink. A bartender cannot measure any of that, but can see the signs and count the drinks.

### Grams of alcohol and standard drinks

A **standard drink** is a fixed amount of pure alcohol used to compare drinks. Countries define it differently; the gov.il definitions page uses the U.S. figure of 14 g, and this course found no official Israeli definition. To compare drinks:

$$\text{grams of alcohol} = \text{ml} \times \frac{\text{ABV \%}}{100} \times 0.789$$

0.789 g/ml is the density of ethanol ([lesson 02.1](../module-02/lesson-01.md)).

| Drink | Alcohol | Grams |
|---|---|---|
| 330 ml beer at 5% | 16.5 ml | ≈ 13 g |
| 150 ml wine at 12.5% | 18.75 ml | ≈ 15 g |
| 45 ml spirit at 40% | 18 ml | ≈ 14 g |
| Margarita: 50 ml tequila 40% + 20 ml triple sec 40% | 28 ml | ≈ 22 g |
| Five 15 ml pours of 40% spirits (Long Island-style) | 30 ml | ≈ 24 g |

A cocktail with two spirits is often one and a half to two standard drinks. When a guest has had "three cocktails", they may have had five or six standard drinks (professional judgment based on the arithmetic).

### Signs: green, amber, red

The traffic-light grouping is this course's working tool (professional judgment), built on the NSW categories of speech, balance, coordination and behaviour. Signs are not conclusive on their own: illness, disability, medication or tiredness can look similar, so look for a pattern and changes over time.

| Level | What you may see | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **Green**: relaxed, in control | normal speech, steady, drinking at a normal pace | serve normally; keep counting |
| **Amber**: early signs | louder, more talkative, faster drinking, lowered inhibitions, buying rounds of shots, mild clumsiness | slow down: offer water and food, suggest an alcohol-free or lower-alcohol drink, no doubles or shots, serve one drink at a time, tell your colleagues and manager |
| **Red**: clear signs | slurred or confused speech; swaying, stumbling, holding on to the bar; spilling or dropping drinks, fumbling money; aggression, crying, falling asleep, vomiting | **no more alcohol**; refuse privately, offer water, food, an alcohol-free drink and a way home; involve the manager; watch their safety |

Once you have refused a guest, do not serve them alcohol again that night, even if they seem to recover (NSW guidance). Their friends may not buy alcohol for them either: handing a drink to the friend who passes it on is still supplying it.

### Refusing service

A refusal goes best when it is private, calm, short and about the rule, not the person (professional judgment):

1. **Get the manager on board** if you can, before or right after; never refuse alone in a dangerous situation.
2. **Talk privately**, at the end of the bar, at eye level, without an audience.
3. **Use "I" and the rule**: "I can't serve you any more alcohol tonight. It's the law and the house rule."
4. **Do not argue or diagnose**: avoid "You're drunk." Say what you will do, not what they are.
5. **Offer something**: water, food, coffee, a soft drink, a taxi.
6. **Stay firm**: repeat the same sentence calmly if they push. Do not negotiate "just one more".
7. **Protect safety**: keep the bar between you; do not touch the guest; call security or the police (100) if they become aggressive.
8. **Record** the refusal in the incident log: time, what you saw, what you did, who was told.

In Hebrew: אני לא יכול / יכולה להגיש לך עוד אלכוהול הערב (*ani lo yachol / yechola lehagish lecha od alkohol ha'erev*, "I can't serve you more alcohol tonight", said by a man / woman); אפשר להביא לך מים או משהו בלי אלכוהול? (*efshar lehavi lecha mayim o mashehu bli alkohol?*, "Can I bring you water or something without alcohol?").

### "The strongest thing you've got"

Requests for triple measures, "extra strong", rounds of shots or a drink "to get drunk fast" are common. The response (professional judgment):

- **Check the guest first.** Any amber or red sign: no stronger drink. Offer water, food or an alcohol-free option.
- **Sober guest:** serve the house spec. A spirit-forward classic (a Martini, a Negroni) at the standard measure is an honest "strong" drink. A double is fine if house policy allows and it is charged as a double.
- **Never** free-pour above the spec to please someone, add alcohol to a drink as a favour, run drinking games or "challenge" drinks, or describe a drink as a way to get drunk. Encouraging a drunk person to drink is part of the offence in section 193(c).
- Do not refill a guest's glass without asking, and do not keep sending rounds to a table without checking who is drinking.

### Drivers

The gov.il definitions page lists people who plan to drive among those who should avoid alcohol entirely. Your job is not to measure anyone's limit; it is to make the alcohol-free choice easy and good ([lesson 07.5](../module-07/lesson-05.md)): offer a 0.0% beer or a proper zero-proof drink, say the ABV of anything that is not 0.0%, and offer to call a taxi. If a clearly intoxicated guest is about to drive, tell the manager at once; the manager decides whether to call the police (100). Do not physically take anyone's keys (professional judgment).

### Alcohol poisoning

> [!CAUTION]
> Signs of alcohol poisoning include confusion, slurred speech or not being able to speak, poor coordination, vomiting, pale or blue-tinged skin, slow or irregular breathing, seizures and passing out (NHS). Call Magen David Adom on **101**. Stay with the person; if they are awake, keep them sitting up; if they pass out, put them in the recovery position and check their breathing. Do not give more alcohol, food or coffee, do not put them in a cold shower, and do not try to make them vomit. Tell the manager. This course does not teach first aid: take a certified first-aid course.

Never put an intoxicated guest out onto the street alone. Move them to a quiet, well-lit place, keep someone with them or check on them often, and help them get home safely with a friend or taxi (NSW guidance).

## Step by step

### Monitor every guest

1. Note age and state at the greeting ([lesson 09.1](lesson-01.md)).
2. Count drinks per guest roughly, in standard drinks, not glasses.
3. Watch speech, balance, coordination and behaviour each time you serve.
4. At amber: slow down, water and food, no doubles or shots, tell the team.
5. At red: stop alcohol and follow the refusal steps.

### Refuse service

1. Tell the manager.
2. Speak privately, calmly, at eye level.
3. "I can't serve you more alcohol tonight; it's the law and our policy."
4. Offer water, food, an alcohol-free drink, a taxi.
5. Repeat calmly if pushed; do not argue or touch.
6. Keep watching the guest's safety; call 100 or 101 if needed.
7. Write it in the incident log.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- The cards [labs/module-09/intoxication-cards.md](../../labs/module-09/intoxication-cards.md): twelve short guest descriptions, five drinks to calculate and three refusal situations. Reference answers: [labs/module-09/intoxication-cards-solution.md](../../labs/module-09/intoxication-cards-solution.md).
- The printable [incident log](../../labs/module-09/incident-log.md).
- A calculator, a phone to film yourself, a partner to play the guest (optional), and your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: without a partner, film yourself delivering the refusal to the camera and play the guest's lines from the card in your head.

### Time

50 minutes: 15 for the cards, 10 for the calculations, 15 for the filmed refusals, 10 for the simulations and logging.

### Steps

1. **Sort the guests.** For each of the twelve descriptions, decide green, amber or red, and write the one action you take next. Compare with the solution.
2. **Calculate.** Work out the grams of alcohol and the number of 14 g standard drinks for the five drinks on the card. Compare.
3. **Refuse on camera.** Choose the three refusal situations. For each, film yourself (or perform with a partner) saying the refusal in English, then the key sentence in Hebrew. Keep each under about 30 seconds.
4. **Review the video** against the eight refusal steps: private, "I" and the rule, no diagnosis, an offer, firm, calm voice and body, safety, and the log. Score yourself out of 8 for each.
5. **Log it.** Fill in one entry of the incident log for refusal situation 1 as if it had happened.
6. **Simulate.** Run the intoxicated-guest and strong-drink scenarios. Aim for at least 6 of 8 points and no safety-critical choice in each.

### What you should see

Most learners place the clear red cards correctly and hesitate on the amber ones, especially the cheerful, generous guest buying shots (professional judgment). In the calculations, any cocktail with two spirits comes out at more than one standard drink. On video, the usual problems are saying "you're drunk", over-explaining, smiling nervously or backing down at the second "come on"; your second take is almost always better. In the simulations, a safety-critical flag means the scenario fails however many points you scored.

### What this practice cannot teach

Video and cards cannot reproduce a crowded bar at 01:00, a guest twice your size who is angry, or the pressure of a big-spending regular; the scripts give you words that come out automatically, but always refuse with your manager's support and never put yourself in danger.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting for red.** By the time speech is slurred, the guest has had too much. Fix: act at amber with water, food, pace and alcohol-free options.
- **Counting glasses, not alcohol.** Fix: think in standard drinks; a two-spirit cocktail is often 1.5–2.
- **"You're drunk."** Labels start arguments. Fix: "I can't serve you more alcohol tonight; it's the law and our policy."
- **Serving the friend.** The friend's round goes straight to the refused guest. Fix: no alcohol for them in any way; tell the group kindly.
- **Pouring stronger to please.** Fix: house measures only; spirit-forward classics or a charged double for sober guests.
- **Putting an intoxicated guest out alone.** Fix: a safe place, someone with them, a friend or taxi; 101 if they are unwell.

## Key takeaways

- Israeli law (Penal Law s. 193(c)) forbids the owner or workers of an alcohol-selling business to supply alcohol to a drunk person or encourage them to drink.
- Watch speech, balance, coordination and behaviour; act early at amber, stop alcohol at red, and do not start again that night.
- Count standard drinks: grams = ml × ABV × 0.789; many cocktails are more than one.
- Refuse privately and calmly, about the rule not the person, with an offer of water, food, an alcohol-free drink and a way home; tell the manager and log it.
- Signs of alcohol poisoning mean 101, staying with the person and no more alcohol.
