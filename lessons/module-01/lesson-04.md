# 01.4 · Prep and garnish tools: juicers, peelers and knives

Before a bar opens, someone squeezes the juice and cuts the garnish. This prep work uses a few simple tools, two of them sharp. This lesson introduces the juicers, peelers and knives a bartender uses, teaches how to handle them safely, and shows the three citrus cuts you will make most often: the wheel, the wedge and the peel. In the hands-on part you prepare a garnish tray and measure how much juice your limes actually give.

## Why it matters

A Friday shift, 21:30. The lime wedges run out, you grab a knife and cut fast on a wet board while talking to a guest. The board slides, the knife slips, and you spend the rest of the night in the emergency room while your colleagues work a bartender short. Knife cuts are one of the most common bar injuries (professional judgment), and almost all come from the same few habits: a moving board, fingers in front of the blade, a knife left where no one can see it. Prep done safely, before service, prevents them. Good prep also makes drinks better: juice squeezed the same day and peels with no bitter white pith.

## Core ideas

> [!CAUTION]
> This lesson involves knives and peelers. Work on a stable board, keep fingers behind the blade, and never try to catch a falling knife: step back and let it fall.

### Juicers

| Tool | What it is | Best for | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hand press (Mexican elbow) | Two hinged cups; the lower one has holes. Place half a lime cut side down and squeeze | Lemons and limes, one at a time | Fast and clean; the standard in many bars |
| Reamer | A ridged cone you twist into half a fruit | Small amounts, oranges | Cheap; more seeds and pulp to strain |
| Electric or lever juicer | A motor-driven cone or a large lever press | Litres of juice before service | Bar and kitchen equipment, not needed at home |

Fresh citrus juice (מיץ טבעי, *mitz tiv'i*, "natural juice") changes taste within hours; Module 3 covers yield, freshness and storage in detail. For now: squeeze close to service, strain out seeds, label the bottle with the name and time.

### Peelers and zesters

- **Y-peeler** (קולפן, *kolfan*): a blade across the top of the handle. It cuts wide, thin strips of peel for twists. The best tool for most bar peels.
- **Channel knife**: a small notched blade that cuts a long, thin strip (a "string" twist).
- **Fine grater (zester)**: grates zest or nutmeg over a drink.

A **peel** or **twist** is a strip of citrus skin. Its oils carry most of the aroma. The white layer under the skin is the **pith**; it is bitter, so a good peel has as little as possible.

### Knives and boards

- **Paring knife** (סכין קטנה, *sakin ktana*): a small knife, 8–10 cm blade. Used for wheels, wedges and trimming peels. This is the bar knife.
- **Chef's knife**: a larger knife for pineapples and big batches of fruit. Rare behind the bar.
- **Cutting board** (קרש חיתוך, *kresh chituch*): plastic or wood, placed on a damp cloth or a non-slip mat so it cannot slide.

A sharp knife is safer than a blunt one: a blunt blade needs more pressure and slips off the fruit's skin.

### Safe knife habits

These follow the UK Health and Safety Executive's guidance for catering kitchens, adapted for a bar:

1. Use the right knife for the job, and keep it sharp.
2. Cut on a stable board, never in your hand or on the bar top.
3. Hold the fruit with a **claw grip**: fingertips tucked under, knuckles forward, so the side of the blade rests against your knuckles and never reaches your fingertips.
4. Carry a knife with the blade pointing down, by your side. Say "knife" (סכין, *sakin*) when you walk behind someone.
5. Never leave a knife loose on the counter edge, under a towel, or in a sink full of water.
6. If a knife falls, step back. Do not try to catch it.
7. A knife is not a can opener, a bottle opener or a screwdriver.
8. When you finish, wipe the knife from the spine side, then store it in its place.

### Hygiene for garnish

Garnish goes straight into a drink that a guest puts in their mouth, so treat it as food. Wash your hands before prep (with soap, at least 20 seconds, as the CDC advises). Rinse the fruit under running water before cutting, as food codes require for raw produce. Keep cut garnish covered and cold, and throw it away at the end of the shift unless the bar's rules say otherwise. Use tongs or picks, not fingers, to put garnish in a drink.

## Step by step

### Citrus wheel

1. Rinse the fruit and dry it.
2. Cut off both ends (about 5 mm).
3. Lay the fruit on its side. With a claw grip, cut slices 4–5 mm thick, across the fruit.
4. For a wheel that sits on the rim, make one cut from the centre to the edge.

### Citrus wedge

1. Cut off both ends.
2. Cut the fruit in half lengthwise (from end to end).
3. Lay each half cut side down. Cut lengthwise into 3 or 4 wedges.
4. Optional: cut a small slit across the middle of the flesh so the wedge sits on a rim.
5. Remove visible seeds with the tip of the knife.

### Wide peel (twist)

1. Hold the fruit in your non-dominant hand, fingers on the sides, away from the path of the blade.
2. Place the Y-peeler at the top of the fruit and draw it down, pressing lightly, to cut a strip about 2 cm wide and 5–6 cm long.
3. Look at the back: you should see only a thin layer of white. If the pith is thick, lay the peel skin side down on the board and shave it off with the paring knife, blade moving away from your fingers.
4. Optional: trim the edges straight with the knife for a neat rectangle.

### Juicing with a hand press

1. Roll the fruit on the board under your palm (this softens it a little).
2. Cut it in half across the middle.
3. Place one half in the press, **cut side down** (towards the holes). It feels backwards, but the press turns the skin inside out and pushes the juice through.
4. Squeeze firmly over a jug or jigger. Strain seeds if needed.

## Hands-on

> [!CAUTION]
> Use a board on a damp cloth, a claw grip and a sharp paring knife. Work slowly; speed comes later. If you cut yourself, stop, wash and cover the cut, and throw away any garnish that may have blood on it.

### What you need

- 3 limes, 1 lemon, 1 orange (about ₪10–20, professional judgment).
- Paring knife, cutting board, damp cloth, Y-peeler, hand press or reamer, a jigger or measuring jug, a small container with a lid (your garnish tray).
- Your practice log ([template](../../templates/practice-log.md)) and phone stopwatch.
- Low-cost alternative: a fork twisted into the half fruit works as a reamer; any vegetable peeler works for peels.

### Time

45 minutes: 5 to set up and wash, 25 to cut and juice, 10 to clean up, 5 to log.

### Steps

1. Wash your hands. Rinse and dry the fruit.
2. Set the board on the damp cloth and check it does not move.
3. Cut 6 lemon or orange wheels. Time yourself.
4. Cut one lime into 8 wedges. Time yourself.
5. Cut 4 wide peels from the orange with the Y-peeler. Turn each over and check the pith.
6. Juice the other 2 limes with the press. Measure the juice of each lime separately in the jigger or a jug and write the ml.
7. Put the garnish in the covered container and refrigerate it (use it for drink practice in later lessons within a day).
8. Wipe and store the knife, wash the board and the press, and wipe the surface.
9. Log: times for wheels, wedges and peels; ml of juice per lime; one thing to improve.

### What you should see

Wheels of even thickness that lie flat, wedges that all look the same, and peels thin enough that some light shows through them. Juice per lime varies a lot with size, ripeness and variety: write your own number and use it for planning in Module 3. A first attempt usually takes 1–2 minutes for six wheels and about 1 minute for eight wedges (professional judgment). If your wheels are uneven, slow down and let the knife do the work: long smooth strokes, not pressing.

<details>
<summary>How to judge your peels</summary>

Hold each peel up to a light. A good peel is orange or yellow all over, with only a thin even white layer behind. Gaps or tears mean too much pressure. A thick white layer means the peeler went too deep: press less, or trim the pith with the knife. Squeeze a good peel skin side towards your face (eyes closed) — you should smell a strong burst of oil.

</details>

### What this practice cannot teach

At a bar you cut dozens of limes in a hurry, in a crowded space, with colleagues passing behind you; build the safe habits now at a slow pace so they hold up under that pressure.

### Completion checklist

- [ ] My board was stable on a damp cloth for the whole session.
- [ ] I used a claw grip for every cut and carried and stored the knife safely.
- [ ] I cut 6 wheels, 8 wedges and 4 peels, and checked the peels for pith.
- [ ] I measured the juice of two limes separately and logged the ml.
- [ ] Garnish is covered and refrigerated; tools and surfaces are clean.

## Common mistakes

- **A sliding board.** The most common cause of cuts. Fix: damp cloth or rubber mat under every board, every time.
- **Fingers flat on the fruit.** Fix: claw grip, knuckles guiding the side of the blade.
- **Thick, pithy peels.** They make drinks bitter. Fix: press lightly with the Y-peeler and trim the pith with the knife.
- **Putting the lime in the press the wrong way round.** Cut side up gives little juice and sprays. Fix: cut side down, towards the holes.
- **Knife left in the sink.** Someone reaches into the water and gets cut. Fix: wash the knife at once and put it in its place.
- **Garnish by hand.** Fingers carry germs to the drink. Fix: wash hands before prep; use tongs or a pick at service.

## Key takeaways

- The hand press is the bar's standard juicer; the reamer and electric juicers have their places.
- A Y-peeler gives wide twists; a channel knife long thin ones; keep pith to a minimum.
- A stable board, a claw grip, a sharp knife and safe carrying and storage prevent most cuts. Never catch a falling knife.
- Wheels, wedges and peels should be even and consistent; time yourself to build speed safely.
- Garnish is food: wash hands and fruit, cover and chill it, and serve it with tongs or picks.
