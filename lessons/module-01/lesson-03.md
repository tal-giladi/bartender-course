# 01.3 · Measuring, mixing and straining tools

Every cocktail passes through the same few tools: something to measure with, something to mix in and something to strain through. This lesson introduces each tool, explains the units bartenders use, and shows how to hold and use a jigger so your measures are accurate. You then repeat the water drill from lesson 01.1, this time with a jigger, and see how much more consistent you become.

## Why it matters

A guest orders two Daiquiris. You free-pour the lime: the first gets 25 ml, the second 15 ml. One is sharp and thin, the other sweet and flat, and the guests swap glasses and notice. The recipe was right; the measuring was not. Bars also count every millilitre of spirit: pouring 5 ml extra in each of 200 drinks is a full litre bottle given away every night. Accurate tools, used the same way every time, protect both the drink and the business.

## Core ideas

### Units: ml, oz, parts, dashes and barspoons

This course uses millilitres (ml) first. Many recipes, especially American ones, use fluid ounces (oz).

- **1 US fluid ounce = 29.57 ml** (exactly 29.5735 ml). The UK (imperial) ounce is slightly smaller, 28.41 ml.
- **Bar convention: 1 oz ≈ 30 ml.** Behind the bar, nobody measures 29.57 ml. Recipes converted for metric bars round 1 oz to 30 ml, ½ oz to 15 ml and ¾ oz to 22.5 ml (often written 20 or 25 ml in metric specs).
- **cl (centilitre) = 10 ml.** Many European menus and older printed specs use cl: 4.5 cl = 45 ml. The current IBA specs on its website use ml (checked 2026-10-17).
- **Parts.** Some recipes give ratios, such as "2 parts gin, 1 part lime, 1 part syrup". Parts keep a drink balanced at any size (lesson 03.1).
- **Barspoon ≈ 5 ml** and **dash ≈ 0.8 ml** (a quick shake of a bitters bottle). Both are approximate; this course uses these values everywhere, including in the calculators.

| Bar oz | ml (bar convention) | ml (exact US) |
|---|---|---|
| ¼ oz | 7.5 | 7.4 |
| ½ oz | 15 | 14.8 |
| ¾ oz | 22.5 | 22.2 |
| 1 oz | 30 | 29.6 |
| 1½ oz | 45 | 44.4 |
| 2 oz | 60 | 59.1 |

### Jiggers

A jigger (ג'יגר, *jigger*) is a small double-ended measuring cup, usually stainless steel, with a different volume at each end. Sizes differ by country: 30/60 ml and 30/45 ml are common in metric bars, 25/50 ml in the UK, and 1 oz/1½ oz (30/45 ml) in the US. Many jiggers have inner lines (for example 15 and 22.5 ml inside the 30 ml cup). Three shapes are common:

| Shape | What it looks like | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Double (hourglass) | Two cones joined at the narrow ends | The classic; quick to flip |
| Japanese | Taller, slimmer cones | Easier to fill to the brim without spilling |
| Bell | Rounded cups with a flat base between | Stable, easy to hold for beginners |

A measuring cup with ml marks on the inside (sometimes called an angled measure) is also used, especially for small amounts. Whatever you use, learn the volume of each line.

### Shakers

A shaker (שייקר, *sheyker*) mixes, chills and dilutes a drink with ice in about 10–15 seconds of hard shaking (Module 4 teaches the movement).

| Type | Parts | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Boston, tin-on-tin | A large tin and a smaller tin that seals inside it | The usual choice in busy bars (professional judgment); light, does not break, chills fast |
| Boston, tin-on-glass | A large tin and a mixing glass | You can see the drink; the glass can break |
| Cobbler | Tin, a top with a built-in strainer, and a cap | Common at home; caps can stick when cold and it is slower to clean |
| French (Parisian) | A metal base and a metal cap, no strainer | Elegant; needs a separate strainer |

### Mixing glass and bar spoon

A **mixing glass** is a thick glass or steel vessel (usually 500–700 ml) used for stirring drinks that contain only spirits, wines and syrups, such as a Martini or Negroni. A **bar spoon** (כף בר, *kaf bar*) is a long spoon, about 25–40 cm, with a twisted stem that spins smoothly between your fingers when you stir. Its bowl holds about 5 ml. Some have a flat disc or a fork at the top end, used for layering or picking up garnishes.

### Strainers

A strainer holds back ice and solids when you pour the drink into the glass.

| Strainer | What it is | Used with |
|---|---|---|
| Hawthorne | A flat plate with a spring coiled around the edge; pushing the plate forward ("closing the gate") makes the gap smaller | Shaker tins; also mixing glasses |
| Julep | A perforated, spoon-shaped strainer held inside the mouth of the glass | Mixing glass, for stirred drinks |
| Fine strainer | A small conical mesh sieve | Held under the Hawthorne to catch ice shards and pulp ("double straining") |

### Muddler

A muddler is a short, thick stick (wood, plastic or steel) for pressing fruit, herbs or sugar in the bottom of a tin or glass. Choose an unvarnished or plastic one: varnish can flake into drinks.

### Other small tools

Pour spouts (speed pourers) fit into bottles and give a steady stream; free pouring with them is taught in lesson 03.2. A bottle opener, a waiter's friend (wine key), tongs and cocktail picks complete the kit; you meet them when you need them.

## Step by step

### How to measure with a jigger

1. Hold the jigger between your thumb and index finger (or index and middle finger), close to the joint, so it stays level.
2. Hold it over the tin, not over the bar top, so drips fall into the drink.
3. Pour from the bottle until the liquid reaches the brim (or the inner line). Look at it from the side for lines, from above for the brim.
4. Stop the pour cleanly: lift the bottle neck and turn it slightly so the last drop does not run down the outside.
5. Tip the jigger quickly and fully into the tin with a turn of the wrist. Do not tilt it slowly; liquid clings.
6. Put the jigger back in its fixed place, or move straight to the next ingredient.

### How to fill to a line you cannot see well

1. Hold the jigger at eye level against a light background.
2. Fill slowly near the line; the curved surface of the liquid (meniscus) should sit on the line.
3. If you go over, pour the excess back into the bottle (water practice only; at work, follow the bar's rule).

### How to strain from a shaker tin

1. Separate the tins and set the small one aside.
2. Place the Hawthorne strainer on the large tin, spring inside.
3. Hold the strainer in place with your index finger on its top, the tin in the same hand.
4. Push the strainer forward to close the gap if you want less ice and pulp to pass.
5. Pour into the glass in one steady stream. For double straining, hold a fine strainer over the glass with the other hand and pour through both.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- A jigger (or a medicine cup / measuring spoons), a kitchen scale with 1 g steps, a glass or tin, water and a towel.
- The drill sheet: [labs/module-01/measurement-drill.md](../../labs/module-01/measurement-drill.md) and, if you prefer a spreadsheet, [labs/module-01/measurement-drill.csv](../../labs/module-01/measurement-drill.csv).
- The tool cards: [labs/module-01/tool-id-cards.md](../../labs/module-01/tool-id-cards.md).
- Your practice log ([template](../../templates/practice-log.md)).
- Low-cost alternative: a 15 ml tablespoon gives you the 15 ml drill; two tablespoons give 30 ml. The habits are the same.

### Time

45 minutes: 30 for the measuring drill (three volumes × five pours, with weighing), 15 for the tool cards.

### Steps

1. Put the empty tin or glass on the scale and tare.
2. Measure 30 ml of water with the jigger, using the steps above, and tip it into the tin. Read the weight.
3. Write the weight on the drill sheet. Tare again (you do not need to empty the tin; just tare before each pour).
4. Do five pours of 30 ml, then five of 15 ml (inner line or the small end), then five of 45 ml (or 60 ml if that is your large end).
5. For each pour, mark it "in" if it is within tolerance: ±5%, or ±1 g for 15 ml because your scale only shows whole grams. That is 28.5–31.5 g for 30 ml (so 29–31 g on the scale), 14–16 g for 15 ml and 43–47 g for 45 ml.
6. Compare with your free-pour baseline from lesson 01.1.
7. Read each tool card and write the tool's name and use without looking at the answers. Then check against the solution file.
8. Log the session: target "4 of 5 in tolerance at each volume".

### What you should see

With a jigger most learners get 4 or 5 of 5 pours in tolerance at 30 and 45 ml on the first session, compared with 0–2 of 5 when free pouring (professional judgment). The 15 ml line is hardest because a tiny tilt is a big percentage. Typical faults: the jigger not level (under-pour), liquid left clinging because the tip was too slow (under-pour), or a "dome" over the brim (over-pour). If you are consistently 1–2 g under, you are not tipping fully.

<details>

A good first session looks like this: 30 ml → 30, 29, 30, 31, 28 g (4/5 in); 15 ml → 15, 14, 16, 13, 15 g (4/5 in); 45 ml → 44, 45, 46, 45, 44 g (5/5 in). Compared with a free-pour spread of 15 ml, the jigger spread is 2–3 ml.

### What this practice cannot teach

Water is thinner than syrup or cream liqueur, which cling to the jigger more, and in a real bar you measure while talking and looking up at guests; you build that in Module 4 and the service simulation.

### Completion checklist

- [ ] I can name each tool on the tool cards and say what it is for.
- [ ] I can convert ½, ¾, 1, 1½ and 2 oz to ml using the bar convention.
- [ ] I measured 15, 30 and 45 (or 60) ml five times each and weighed every pour.
- [ ] At least 4 of 5 pours were in tolerance at each volume, or I logged what to fix and will repeat the drill.
- [ ] The session is in my practice log, next to my free-pour baseline.

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring over the bar top.** Drips land on the bar, not in the drink. Fix: always hold the jigger over the tin.
- **Under-filling the jigger.** A cup filled "nearly" to the brim is 1–3 ml short. Fix: fill to the brim (or meniscus on the line), checked from the side.
- **Slow tip.** Liquid clings to the steel. Fix: a quick, full turn of the wrist.
- **Mixing up oz and ml.** Reading "1½" in a ml recipe, or "45" in an oz one, ruins a drink. Fix: check the unit at the top of the recipe before you start.
- **Wrong strainer.** A julep strainer on a tin lets ice through; a Hawthorne without a fine strainer leaves ice shards in a coupe. Fix: Hawthorne on tins, julep or Hawthorne on the mixing glass, add a fine strainer for drinks served without ice.
- **Using a varnished wooden muddler.** Flakes of varnish end up in drinks. Fix: unvarnished wood, plastic or steel.

## Key takeaways

- 1 US fl oz = 29.57 ml; behind the bar 1 oz is treated as 30 ml, ½ oz as 15 ml and ¾ oz as 22.5 ml.
- A barspoon is about 5 ml and a dash about 0.8 ml in this course.
- Measure with a level jigger, filled to the brim or line, over the tin, tipped quickly.
- Shake in tins (Boston tin-on-tin is the bar standard), stir in a mixing glass, strain with a Hawthorne or julep, and add a fine strainer for drinks served up.
- A jigger turns a 15 ml spread into a 2–3 ml spread: that is what consistency means.
