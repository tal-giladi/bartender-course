# 01.1 · What a bartender does and how this course trains you

Bartending is a practical trade: you prepare drinks to a standard, serve people safely and keep a busy station clean and stocked. This first lesson explains what the job really involves, who does what behind a bar, how this course turns home practice into job-ready skills, and what home practice can never replace. You finish by setting up your practice log and measuring how accurate your eye is before any training.

## Why it matters

Picture your first trial shift (משמרת ניסיון, *mishmeret nisayon*) at a busy Tel Aviv bar on a Thursday night. The head bartender hands you a bar spoon and says, "Set up the well, then back up Dana." If you do not know what the well is, what "back up" means, or which jobs are yours and which are not, you lose the shift before you pour a drink. Bar owners hire people who understand the job and move with purpose. Knowing the roles and the language is the first thing that makes you look hireable.

## Core ideas

### The job in one sentence

A bartender (ברמן / ברמנית, *barmen / barmanit*) prepares and serves drinks to a recipe, looks after guests, and keeps the bar safe, clean and stocked. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (a government agency that describes jobs) lists the core duties: greet guests and take orders, mix drinks to recipes, pour beer and wine, check identification, watch for intoxication, collect payment, clean the bar and restock. Making cocktails is only one part. On many shifts most of your time goes to beer, wine, simple mixed drinks, cleaning and talking to people.

### Who does what behind a bar

Bars differ, but most have the same four roles. Small bars merge them; big ones add more.

| Role | Hebrew used in Israeli bars | Main tasks | How people usually get there |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barback — the bartender's assistant | usually just "barback" (בַּרְבֶּק, *barbek*) or עוזר בר (*ozer bar*) | Brings ice, glasses, bottles and kegs; empties bins; washes and polishes glass; preps garnish | First bar job, often no experience needed |
| Bartender | ברמן / ברמנית (*barmen / barmanit*) | Takes orders, makes and serves drinks, handles payment, keeps the station clean, refuses service when needed | From barback or waiting tables, or with home training like this course |
| Head bartender | ברמן ראשי (*barmen rashi*) | Runs the shift behind the bar, trains staff, sets specs, checks quality and stock | Years behind the bar |
| Bar manager | מנהל/ת בר (*menahel/et bar*) | Ordering, costing, menus, staff schedules, licences and compliance | Head bartender with business skills |

You will also hear אחמ"ש (*achmash*, short for אחראי משמרת, "person in charge of the shift"). This is the shift manager, and on a given night it may be the head bartender, a floor manager or the owner. If something goes wrong (an injury, a fight, a guest who must be refused), the achmash is the person you tell.

> [!NOTE]
> The Hebrew role names above are what you will commonly hear in Israeli bars, as of 2026-10-17. They are professional judgment, not official job titles. Many bars mix Hebrew and English freely.

### Words you will hear on day one

| English | Hebrew (transliteration) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Shift | משמרת (*mishmeret*) | Your working period, for example 19:00–03:00 |
| Station | עמדה (*emda*) | Your own working area behind the bar |
| Prep / mise en place | הכנות (*hachanot*) | Everything cut, filled and ready before guests arrive |
| Order | הזמנה (*hazmana*) | What a guest or a waiter asks for |
| Tips | טיפים (*tipim*) | Gratuities, often shared between staff |
| Out of stock | נגמר (*nigmar*) | "It's finished" — say it as soon as you notice |

Mise en place (French, "everything in its place") is the kitchen term for preparing your station before service. Bartenders use it too.

### What this course is, and what it is not

This course teaches the knowledge and movements of an entry-level bartender: tools, glassware, ice and hygiene (Module 1), spirits and other drinks (Modules 2 and 7), measuring and flavour (Module 3), technique (Module 4), classic cocktails (Modules 5 and 6), operations and speed (Module 8), guests and Israeli law (Module 9), and costing (Module 10). Module 11 is a self-scored final assessment.

It is **not** an accredited qualification. Finishing it does not give you a licence, a government certificate or a recognised diploma. Module 9 covers what Israeli law requires of a bar and its staff, with official citations. In practice the employer decides whether you are good enough (professional judgment). Your proof is what you can do on a trial shift. The International Bartenders Association (IBA), the world body of national bartender associations, runs its own courses through the IBA Academy; this course uses the IBA's published cocktail specifications as its default recipes but is not connected to or endorsed by the IBA.

### How the course trains you

Every lesson ends with something you do with your hands. You practise mostly with water, ice, tea, citrus, sugar syrup and alcohol-free products, because the movements and volumes are the same and water is cheap. You measure your results on a kitchen scale (1 ml of water weighs about 1 g), time yourself with a phone stopwatch, and film yourself so you can compare your movements with a standard. Simulations in later modules give you busy-service practice and recipe drills.

The course is for adults 18 and over. Where a lesson suggests tasting real alcohol, it is optional, small (sip and spit), and never before driving.

### What home practice cannot teach

Be honest with yourself about the limits:

- **A real rush.** Twenty guests shouting orders, a full ticket rail and a waiter waiting is a different kind of pressure.
- **Reading a crowded room.** Spotting who is next, who is drunk and who is about to start a fight comes from real shifts.
- **Commercial equipment.** Draught beer systems, glasswashers, ice machines and point-of-sale (POS) tills work differently in every bar.
- **Real draught foam and pouring beer** at volume.
- **Working beside a team** in a small space, with call-outs and shared tools.

Plan to close this gap: once you finish Module 8, ask a local bar if you can do a few barback shifts, or work events. You will learn faster because the basics are already in your hands.

## Step by step

### How to work through each lesson

1. Read the lesson once from top to bottom (10–20 minutes).
2. Gather what the Hands-on section lists. Use the low-cost alternative if you do not have the item.
3. Do the practice. Measure, time or film, as the lesson asks.
4. Write the result in your practice log, including what went wrong.
5. Take the lesson quiz. If you score under 70%, reread the sections behind the questions you missed.
6. Before the next lesson, repeat the drill once more if your numbers were far from the target.

### How to set up your practice log

1. Copy the [practice log template](../../templates/practice-log.md) into a notebook, a document or a spreadsheet.
2. Add one row per practice session: date, lesson, drill, time spent, target, result, what went wrong, fix for next time.
3. Once a week, fill in the weekly review at the bottom.
4. Keep the log for the whole course. In Module 11 you use it to write your final performance report.

## Hands-on

This first drill shows you how accurate your eye is before training. Most beginners are surprised. Keep the numbers: in lesson 01.3 you repeat the drill with a jigger and see the difference.

### What you need

- A kitchen scale that reads in 1 g steps (roughly ₪40–80 if you need to buy one; price range is professional judgment, not checked at a shop).
- A glass or mug, a jug of water and a towel.
- Your practice log ([template](../../templates/practice-log.md)).
- Low-cost alternative: no scale? Use a measuring cup or a medicine cup with ml marks and read it at eye level. It is less precise, so write "cup" in the log.

### Time

30 minutes: 10 to set up the log, 15 for the pours, 5 to write up.

### Steps

1. Put the empty glass on the scale and press tare (zero).
2. Without any measure, pour what you think is 30 ml of water into the glass. Stop when it "looks right".
3. Read the weight in grams. Since 1 ml of water weighs about 1 g, this is your pour in ml.
4. Write it down, empty the glass, dry it, tare again.
5. Repeat for five pours in total.
6. Work out your average and your error for each pour: error % = (your pour − 30) ÷ 30 × 100. A pour of 36 g is +20%.
7. Enter the session in your practice log: drill "free-pour baseline 30 ml", target "30 ml ±5%", result "average and range".

### What you should see

Most beginners land between 20 and 45 ml, with a spread of 10 ml or more between pours (professional judgment). That spread matters more than the average. A drink that gets 36 ml of lime one night and 24 ml the next tastes like two different drinks. If all five pours were within 28.5–31.5 ml (±5%), you have a good eye, but you will still use a jigger at work, because consistency over hundreds of drinks is what bars pay for.

### What this practice cannot teach

Water in a still kitchen is not a speed pourer on a bottle in a loud bar; free pouring with a pourer is covered in lesson 03.2.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking bartending is mostly cocktails.** Most shifts are beer, wine, simple mixed drinks, cleaning and restocking. Fix: give the "boring" lessons (glass, ice, hygiene, stock) the same attention as recipes.
- **Overstating your training.** Calling yourself "certified" after an online course loses trust fast. Fix: say "I completed a self-study course and can show you my technique", then prove it on the trial shift.
- **Skipping the log.** Without numbers you cannot see progress. Fix: one line per session, even a five-minute one.
- **Reading without doing.** Reading about a jigger does not make your pours accurate. Fix: never mark a lesson done until its checklist is ticked.
- **Ignoring the barback route.** Many good bartenders started by carrying ice. Fix: if no one hires you as a bartender, take barback shifts and ask to be trained.

## Key takeaways

- A bartender mixes drinks, but also serves, checks ID, refuses service, takes payment, cleans and restocks.
- Most bars have barbacks, bartenders, a head bartender and a bar manager; the achmash runs the shift.
- This course is self-study and not accredited; your proof of skill is what you can do on a trial shift.
- You practise with water and measure results by weight, time and video.
- Home practice cannot replace a real rush, real equipment or a real team; plan barback or event shifts later.
