# 01.6 · Ice: types, handling and why it matters

Ice is the ingredient used in more drinks than any other, and the one beginners think about least. It chills the drink, adds the water that softens alcohol and joins the flavours, and holds the drink cold in the glass. The size, shape and dryness of the ice change how fast all of that happens. Ice is also food: guests swallow it, so it must be handled as cleanly as any garnish. This lesson covers the types of ice, what they do, and the hygiene rules around them, ending with a melt test you measure on your scale.

## Why it matters

Saturday night, a glass slips from a barback's hand and shatters on the edge of the ice well. Did a piece go in? Nobody can tell: glass in ice is nearly invisible. The bartender who keeps scooping risks a guest swallowing a shard. The one who knows the procedure stops, warns the team, empties and "burns" the ice, cleans the well and refills it. It costs five minutes and some ice. The other choice can cost a guest a serious injury and the bar its reputation. Knowing ice also decides taste: the same Mojito made with wet crushed ice or dry cubes can be a different drink.

## Core ideas

### Words you will hear

Ice is קרח (*kerach*). Cubes are קוביות קרח (*kubiyot kerach*), crushed ice is קרח גרוס (*kerach garus*), the ice machine is מכונת קרח (*mechonat kerach*), and the scoop is often just the סקופ (*skup*) or כף קרח (*kaf kerach*). These are common Israeli bar usage (professional judgment, 2026-10-17).

### Types of ice

| Type | What it is | Main uses |
|---|---|---|
| Cubed | Solid cubes about 2.5 cm (1 inch) on a side; the default when a recipe just says "ice" | Shaking, stirring, most drinks on the rocks, highballs |
| Cracked | Cubes broken into 2–4 pieces with the back of a bar spoon | Faster chilling when stirring; some swizzled drinks |
| Crushed (pebble) | Small pieces, from a crusher or cubes bashed in a cloth bag | Juleps, swizzles, tiki drinks, some Mojitos; packed into the glass |
| Large cube or sphere | One 5–6 cm piece | Spirit-forward drinks on the rocks, e.g. Old Fashioned; slow melting |
| Spear | A long column that fits a tall glass | Highballs, served with a clear appearance |
| Machine "hollow" or flake ice | Hollow tubes or thin wafers from cheaper machines | Not ideal for shaking or stirring; melts fast |

Clear ice has no cloudy centre. Cloudiness is trapped air and dissolved minerals. Clear ice is made by freezing water slowly from one direction (directional freezing): professional bars use block-ice machines; at home, an insulated box with an open top in the freezer works, though it takes a day or more.

> [!WARNING]
> Dry ice (frozen carbon dioxide) and liquid nitrogen are not ice and are not used in this course. Both can cause severe burns and internal injury if swallowed.

### How ice chills, and why that means dilution

Ice cools a drink mostly by **melting**. Turning ice into water absorbs a lot of heat from the liquid around it. So you cannot chill a drink with ice without also adding water to it. This added water is called **dilution**, and it is a planned part of every shaken and stirred drink: it softens alcohol and joins the flavours. Too little and the drink is hot and harsh; too much and it is thin. Module 3 measures dilution properly, with a simulation.

Two things change how fast ice works:

1. **Surface area.** Ice melts where it touches the liquid. The same 100 g of ice broken into small pieces has much more surface than one big cube, so crushed ice chills and dilutes fastest, large cubes slowest.
2. **Wet or dry ice.** Ice straight from a freezer is very cold (below 0 °C) and dry: it sticks to your fingers. Ice that has sat in a well is at 0 °C and wet on the surface, and that surface water goes straight into the drink. Wet ice dilutes more. Difford's Guide suggests letting very cold freezer ice sit a few minutes before use so drinks get enough dilution.

Two practical rules follow:

- **Fill the glass with ice.** A full glass stays colder, so each cube melts more slowly. A few cubes floating in a drink melt fast and water it down.
- **Never reuse shaker ice.** Ice that was shaken is broken and wet. Strain the drink onto fresh ice in the glass.

### Ice is food

The ice in a guest's glass is something they eat and drink. Food-safety codes, such as the U.S. FDA Food Code, treat ice for drinks as food: it must be made from drinking water, kept covered and clean, and handled with utensils. The Israeli Ministry of Health's requirements for bars are covered in Module 9. The practical rules:

- **Use the scoop.** Always take ice with a scoop (or tongs for single cubes).
- **Store the scoop outside the ice**, in a holder or a clean container, or with its handle sticking out of the ice. The handle touches hands; it must not lie in the ice.
- **Never a glass in the ice.** A glass can chip or break, and the pieces disappear in the ice.
- **No hands in the ice**, and nothing else: no bottles, cans, fruit or wine chilling in the serving ice. Ice used to chill bottles is a separate supply that is never served.
- **Keep the well and the machine clean.** Empty and clean the ice well at closing. Ice machines need regular cleaning to the manufacturer's schedule; that is a manager's task, but you should know it exists.

### Burning the ice

"Burning the ice" means throwing away all the ice in a well and melting what is left with warm or hot water so nothing can hide in it. You burn the ice when:

- a glass breaks in or near the ice well, or a glass was used to scoop and might be chipped;
- something falls in (a bottle cap, a garnish, a phone);
- you suspect contamination (a hand, a spill, a dirty scoop).

Difford's Guide's advice for a busy bar: if you cannot clean the well at once, mark the ice as contaminated so no one uses it, and work from another station until it is done.

## Step by step

### Scoop ice into a glass

1. Take the scoop from its holder by the handle.
2. Scoop from the top of the ice, filling the scoop.
3. Hold the glass by its base next to (not over or in) the well and pour the ice in. Fill the glass to the top for drinks on the rocks and long drinks.
4. Return the scoop to its holder, handle up.

### Make cracked and crushed ice by hand

1. Cracked: hold a cube in your palm (or on a cloth on the board) and tap it sharply with the back of a bar spoon. It breaks into 2–4 pieces.
2. Crushed: put cubes in a clean cloth bag (a Lewis bag) or a clean tea towel folded around them. Hit with a rolling pin or a wooden mallet until the pieces are pea-sized.
3. Use crushed ice at once; it melts fast.

### Burn the ice after glass breaks

1. Stop. Say "glass!" (זכוכית, *zchuchit*) so the team stops using that well.
2. Do not reach into the ice with your hands.
3. Remove any large pieces of glass you can see with tongs, into the broken-glass container.
4. Scoop out or drain as much ice as you can into a bin or sink.
5. Pour warm or hot water into the well to melt the rest and wash out small pieces. Drain it.
6. Look and feel (with a cloth, not fingers) for glass; repeat the rinse if unsure.
7. Clean and dry the well, then refill with fresh ice. Throw away any garnish or open containers that were near the breakage.
8. Tell the shift manager (achmash). In a rush, mark the well "do not use" and work from another station until it is done.

## Hands-on

> [!WARNING]
> Use only normal water ice from your freezer. Do not use dry ice. When crushing ice, keep fingers clear of the rolling pin, and wipe up melt water from the floor so no one slips.

### What you need

- Two identical glasses, about 300 ml each.
- 200 g ice cubes (two trays), a clean tea towel or cloth bag, a rolling pin.
- Kitchen scale, room-temperature water, a strainer, a bar spoon or teaspoon, a phone stopwatch.
- Optional: a kitchen or aquarium thermometer.
- Your practice log ([template](../../templates/practice-log.md)).
- Low-cost alternative: no rolling pin? A heavy pan base works. No scale? Measure liquid volume in a measuring jug before and after.

### Time

45 minutes: 10 to set up, 15 for two melt tests, 10 for the scoop and burn-the-ice rehearsal, 10 to clean and log.

### Steps

1. Put each glass on the scale, tare, and add exactly 200 g of room-temperature water to each. Label them A and B.
2. Weigh 100 g of cubes for glass A.
3. Crush 100 g of cubes in the towel and weigh it for glass B (weigh after crushing; some may melt).
4. Add the ice to both glasses at the same moment. Stir each with the same gentle movement for 30 seconds.
5. Strain the liquid from each glass into a container on the scale and weigh it. Melted ice = liquid weight − 200 g.
6. If you have a thermometer, measure each liquid's temperature.
7. Repeat once with cubes straight from the freezer versus cubes that sat in a bowl for 10 minutes (both cubed), to see dry versus wet ice.
8. Rehearse the scooping steps 10 times with a bowl of ice and a glass.
9. Rehearse "burn the ice" on your bowl: drop a bottle cap in (stand-in for glass), and go through the steps.
10. Log the melted-ice numbers for every test.

### What you should see

The crushed ice melts more than the cubes in the same 30 seconds, and the crushed-ice water is colder. Expect a clearly larger number for B, often around twice as much melt or more, but your result depends on your ice and freezer (professional judgment: measure, do not trust the number here). The wet ice that sat out usually adds a few more grams of water than the dry freezer ice. This is the basic trade-off every bartender manages: faster chilling always means faster dilution.

<details>
<summary>Example result and interpretation</summary>

A (cubes): 200 g water + 100 g ice → 224 g liquid after 30 s → 24 g melted. B (crushed): 200 g + 100 g → 251 g → 51 g melted. B is colder and has more than twice the added water. Interpretation: crushed ice is right for a drink built to be very cold and fairly diluted (a julep, a swizzle); cubes are right for a shaken or stirred drink where you control dilution with time.

</details>

### What this practice cannot teach

A bar ice well holds many kilograms of machine ice that is wet and at 0 °C all night, and a real glass breakage is loud, sudden and in a crowd; the rehearsal builds the order of steps, not the calm.

## Common mistakes

- **Scooping with a glass.** Fix: scoop only, every time, even at home; build the habit now.
- **Scoop left buried in the ice.** Fix: holder or handle-out, always.
- **Half-filling glasses with ice.** The drink warms and dilutes faster. Fix: fill the glass.
- **Pouring the shaker ice into the glass.** It is broken and wet and over-dilutes. Fix: strain onto fresh ice.
- **Chilling bottles in the serving ice.** The outside of a bottle is not clean. Fix: a separate ice bucket for chilling, never served.
- **"It was only a small piece of glass."** You cannot find all of it. Fix: burn the ice, every time.

## Key takeaways

- "Ice" in a recipe means solid cubes about 2.5 cm; crushed, cracked, large cubes and spears have specific uses.
- Ice chills by melting, so chilling always adds water; smaller and wetter ice does both faster.
- Fill the glass with ice and never reuse shaker ice.
- Ice is food: scoop only, scoop stored outside the ice, nothing else in the well.
- Any glass breakage near ice means burning the ice: stop, warn, empty, melt, clean, refill.
