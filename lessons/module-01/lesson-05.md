# 01.5 · Glassware and when to use each glass

The glass is part of the drink. It decides whether a cocktail stays cold, whether you can smell it, how much ice fits and how long the bubbles last. Bars use a small set of standard shapes, and every recipe names one. This lesson introduces those glasses, what each shape does, and how to handle glass safely, because broken glass behind a bar is a serious hazard. You finish by measuring your own glasses and deciding which bar glass each one can stand in for.

## Why it matters

A guest orders a Negroni. A new bartender reaches for a tall glass, fills it with ice and pours the 90 ml of drink into it. The glass looks half empty, the drink waters down faster under all that ice, and the guest feels short-changed. A rocks glass would have looked full and kept the balance right. Worse, a chipped rim on any glass can cut a guest's lip. Picking the right glass, and checking it before you use it, takes two seconds and avoids both problems.

## Core ideas

### The words

Glass in Hebrew is כוס (*kos*), plural כוסות (*kosot*). In Israeli bars you will often hear glasses described simply: כוס נמוכה (*kos nemucha*, "low glass") for a rocks glass and כוס גבוהה (*kos gvoha*, "tall glass") for a highball, plus כוס יין (*kos yayin*, wine glass). A shot glass, and the shot itself, is commonly called צ'ייסר (*chaser*) in Israel.

> [!NOTE]
> The Hebrew usage above is professional judgment as of 2026-10-17. Capacities are typical ranges: real glasses vary by manufacturer, and every bar has its own set.

### "Up", "on the rocks" and "long"

- **Up** (served up): chilled by shaking or stirring, strained, served with no ice, usually in a stemmed glass.
- **On the rocks** (rocks): served over ice in a short glass.
- **Long**: topped with a mixer (soda, tonic, ginger beer) over ice in a tall glass.

### The main glasses

| Glass | Shape | Typical capacity | Used for (examples) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cocktail (martini) glass | Stem, cone-shaped (V) bowl | 90–300 ml; classic sizes about 120–180 ml | Drinks served up: Martini, Cosmopolitan |
| Coupe | Stem, shallow rounded bowl | about 150–250 ml (professional judgment) | Drinks served up: Daiquiri, Sidecar; harder to spill than a V-shape |
| Nick & Nora | Stem, small tall rounded bowl | about 120–180 ml (professional judgment) | Smaller stirred drinks served up |
| Rocks / old fashioned | Short, straight, heavy base | 180–300 ml | Spirit-forward drinks on ice: Old Fashioned, Negroni; neat spirits |
| Double old fashioned | Wider, taller rocks | 350–470 ml | Same drinks with a large ice cube; some sours on ice |
| Highball | Tall, straight | 240–350 ml | Spirit + mixer: gin and tonic, Cuba Libre |
| Collins | Taller and narrower than a highball | about 300–410 ml | Tom Collins, Mojito, fizzes |
| Wine glass | Stem, large rounded bowl | about 350–600 ml (professional judgment) | Wine; spritzes such as the Aperol Spritz |
| Flute | Stem, tall narrow bowl | about 150–250 ml (professional judgment) | Sparkling wine; French 75 |
| Copper mug | Metal mug with handle | about 350–500 ml (professional judgment) | Moscow Mule |
| Shot glass | Small, thick | about 30–60 ml (professional judgment) | Shots, measuring by eye at home (not at work) |

The glass for each classic cocktail is in its recipe card in Modules 5 and 6. The IBA specs, this course's default, name a glass for every drink.

### Why the shape matters

- **Stem → temperature.** A drink served up has no ice to keep it cold. Holding the stem keeps your warm hand away from the bowl.
- **Bowl width → aroma.** A wide bowl puts more of the drink's surface near the nose. A narrow flute shows less surface, so bubbles escape more slowly.
- **Height and width → ice and dilution.** A tall glass full of ice keeps a long drink cold. Filling a glass fully with ice, rather than adding a few cubes, keeps the drink colder and slows melting (lesson 01.6).
- **Thick base → strength.** Rocks glasses have a heavy base so you can build, stir or muddle directly in them.
- **Size → how full the drink looks.** A drink should fill its glass to about 1 cm below the rim. Too big a glass makes a correct drink look short.

### Chilling glasses

Drinks served up should go into a cold glass. Three ways:

1. Store glasses in a glass chiller or freezer (best, if the bar has space).
2. Fill the glass with ice and water while you make the drink; dump it just before you strain.
3. At home, put glasses in the freezer 10–15 minutes before practice.

### Glass safety

> [!CAUTION]
> Broken or chipped glass can cut you and your guests. Never use a glass to scoop ice, never carry glasses with your fingers inside them, and if glass breaks near ice, all of that ice must be thrown away (lesson 01.6).

- **Check before you use it.** Look around the rim and down the side against the light. A chip or crack means the glass is out of service: put it in a separate container for broken glass, not back on the shelf.
- **Hold it low.** Hold stemmed glasses by the stem and tumblers by the lower third. Never by the rim: that is where the guest's lips go.
- **Never stack glasses** inside each other. They stick and crack when you pull them apart.
- **Avoid sudden temperature changes** (thermal shock). A glass hot from the glasswasher can crack if filled with ice at once. Let it cool or rinse it with cold water first.
- **Carry on a tray** or in a rack, not with fingers in the glasses.

## Step by step

### Choose the glass for a drink

1. Read the recipe: is the drink served up, on the rocks or long?
2. Up → a stemmed glass (cocktail, coupe or Nick & Nora), chilled. Choose the size so the drink fills it to about 1 cm below the rim.
3. On the rocks → a rocks glass, or a double rocks for a large cube.
4. Long with a mixer → highball; long with soda and a lot of ice or crushed ice → collins.
5. Wine-based or spritz → wine glass. Sparkling wine → flute.
6. If the bar does not have the named glass, use the closest one with the same function (stem for up, short for rocks, tall for long), and note it.

### Check a glass before use

1. Pick it up by the base or stem.
2. Hold it against a light. Turn it once, looking at the rim, then down the side.
3. Run your eye (not your finger) around the rim for chips.
4. Look for lipstick, water spots or detergent smell. Dirty → back to the wash; chipped or cracked → broken-glass container.

### Chill a glass with ice and water

1. Use the ice scoop to put ice in the glass. Never dip the glass into the ice.
2. Add water to fill.
3. Make the drink.
4. Dump the ice water into the sink, give the glass a shake, and strain the drink into it at once.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- Every drinking glass in your kitchen (aim for 5–8 different ones).
- Kitchen scale (1 g steps), water, towel, a marker or tape for labels.
- The worksheet: [labs/module-01/glass-capacity-worksheet.md](../../labs/module-01/glass-capacity-worksheet.md).
- The tool and glass cards (cards 15–18): [labs/module-01/tool-id-cards.md](../../labs/module-01/tool-id-cards.md).
- Low-cost alternative: if you have no scale, fill each glass from a measuring jug and read how much you poured.

### Time

40 minutes: 25 to measure, 10 to map, 5 to log.

### Steps

1. Check each glass for chips and cracks as described above. Put aside any damaged glass; do not use it.
2. Put the first glass on the scale and tare it.
3. Fill it with water to the brim. Write the weight in grams: this is the brim capacity in ml.
4. Pour out water until the level is about 1 cm below the rim. Write the weight: this is the working capacity, the most a drink should fill.
5. Repeat for every glass.
6. On the worksheet, decide which bar glass each one can stand in for (rocks, highball, collins, coupe, wine...), using the shape and the capacity table in this lesson.
7. Now place 90 ml of water in your "rocks" glass with ice, and 90 ml in your largest tall glass with ice. Look at both from a guest's view. Photograph them side by side.
8. Do cards 15–18 of the tool and glass cards.
9. Log the session.

### What you should see

Brim capacity is always more than working capacity, by 15–40 ml for most glasses. Most kitchen tumblers fall in the rocks or highball range; most homes do not have a real coupe or cocktail glass, so a small wine glass is the usual stand-in for drinks served up (it has a stem, which is what matters). The photo should make the point of the lesson clear: the same 90 ml looks generous in the right short glass and mean in a tall one.

## Common mistakes

- **Holding glasses by the rim.** Guests see it and it is unhygienic. Fix: stem or lower third, always.
- **Using a chipped glass "just this once".** Fix: the moment you see a chip, the glass goes to the broken-glass container.
- **Scooping ice with the glass.** It can chip or break in the ice, and you cannot see the pieces. Fix: always the scoop.
- **Too big a glass for the drink.** A correct pour looks short. Fix: choose by working capacity.
- **Putting a hot glass straight into ice.** It can crack. Fix: let it cool or rinse it with cold water first.
- **Stacking glasses.** They lock together and crack. Fix: store them single, upside down on a mat or rack.

## Key takeaways

- Served up → stemmed glass, chilled; on the rocks → short glass; long → tall glass; wine and spritz → wine glass; sparkling → flute.
- The stem keeps hands from warming the drink; the bowl shape controls aroma; the height and ice control dilution and bubbles.
- Choose the glass so the drink fills it to about 1 cm below the rim.
- Check every glass for chips before use; hold glasses low, never stack them, never scoop ice with them.
- In Israel a rocks glass is often just "kos nemucha" and a shot is often called a "chaser".
