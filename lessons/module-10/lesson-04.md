# 10.4 · Menu design and writing descriptions

A menu is the bar's quietest salesperson: it is read by every guest, it decides what gets ordered, and it makes promises the bartender has to keep. This lesson shows how to lay out a cocktail menu so guests find what they want quickly, how to write short descriptions that are tempting and true, how to use sales and gross profit to decide which drinks to keep, move or replace (menu engineering), and how to recommend and upsell without misleading anyone or pushing more alcohol. In the hands-on part you rewrite eight descriptions, classify a bar's ten drinks, and write the guest-facing menu for your own twelve drinks.

## Why it matters

A guest reads "Tropical Sunset — a light and refreshing house favourite" and orders it. It arrives strong, with almond syrup she is allergic to, and the menu never said so. The bar lost a guest and nearly caused harm with one line of text. Another bar's menu says "Negroni — gin, Campari, sweet vermouth, orange. Bitter, strong, stirred." Nobody is surprised, the guest who wants something lighter asks for a recommendation, and the bartender sells her a spritz she loves. Good menus save the bartender explanations, protect guests and quietly steer sales to the drinks the bar makes well and profitably.

## Core ideas

### Structure: help guests choose by style

Most guests choose by style first (short and sour, long and fizzy, strong and stirred) and by base spirit second. Group the menu that way, as you did in [lesson 06.8](../module-06/lesson-08.md):

| Element | Practice (professional judgment unless stated) |
|---|---|
| Sections | 3–5 by style, e.g. "Fresh and sour · Long and fizzy · Stirred and strong · No alcohol" |
| Order inside a section | lightest first, strongest last |
| Number of drinks | about 10–16 cocktails is easy to read and to prep for; more slows guests and the bar |
| Name | the drink's real name; if you invent a name, the description must say what it is |
| Description | 8–20 words: base spirit first, then 2–3 key flavours, then texture or strength |
| Strength cue | a word ("light", "medium", "strong") or the ml of spirit; never a selling point by itself |
| Alcohol-free | its own section, written with the same care, not "mocktails" at the bottom |
| Allergens | a line such as "Please tell us about any allergies"; mark common ones (nuts in orgeat, egg white, fish in Worcestershire) (Module 9) |
| Price | the total price in shekels, including VAT and any compulsory charge (lesson 10.2) |
| Languages | Hebrew and English are common on Israeli menus ([lesson 06.7](../module-06/lesson-07.md), professional judgment) |

### Prices on the page

One Cornell study (Yang, Kimes and Sessarego, 2009) gave diners at a single upscale restaurant one of three menus: prices as plain numbers, with a dollar sign, or written in words. Guests with plain numbers spent significantly more. It was one restaurant, at lunch, in the US, and the authors warn against generalising, so treat it as a hint, not a rule. Common layout advice, such as not lining prices up in a column so guests compare them, or a "golden triangle" where eyes land first, has little solid evidence behind it (professional judgment). In Israel, whatever the format, the price shown must be the total price in shekels (Consumer Protection Law, section 17D).

### Honest descriptions

Israel's Consumer Protection Law forbids a business from doing anything, in writing or speech, that may mislead a consumer about a material matter, and lists among those matters the quality, nature, quantity, components, producer, trade name and place of production of a product (section 2, statutory). For a menu that means:

| Write | Only if |
|---|---|
| a brand ("Patrón Paloma") | that brand is what is poured, every time |
| "fresh", "freshly squeezed" | the juice is squeezed in house, that day |
| "homemade", "house syrup" | it is made at the bar |
| "local", "Israeli", "from the Galilee" | it is true, and you can name the product and producer |
| "light", "refreshing" | the drink is genuinely low in alcohol or long; a stirred all-spirit drink is not "light" |
| "double", "large", a size in ml | the glass actually holds that |
| "alcohol-free" | it contains no alcohol; "low-alcohol" if it has some (NA spirits may contain traces: check the label) |

A good description is the standard recipe in guest language. Compare:

- Unclear: "Tropical Sunset — a light and refreshing house favourite."
- Honest: "Tropical Sunset — two rums, lime, orange curaçao and almond syrup (nuts). Sweet-sour, strong, over crushed ice."

Do not sell a drink by its strength or by getting drunk ("the strongest drink in town", "two of these and you're done"). It encourages heavy drinking, which this course and responsible bars avoid (professional judgment; Module 9 covers responsible service and the law).

### Menu engineering: popularity × gross profit

**Menu engineering** (Kasavana and Smith, Michigan State University, early 1980s) sorts each drink by two numbers: how often it sells, and how much gross profit it earns per serving (lesson 10.2). Both are compared with the menu's averages:

- **Popularity** = drinks sold ÷ all drinks sold. Average = 100% ÷ number of drinks (10 drinks → 10% each).
- **Average gross profit** = total gross profit of all drinks sold ÷ number of drinks sold (a sales-weighted average).

| Class | Popularity | Gross profit | Typical action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Star** | high | high | keep, make it visible, never let quality slip |
| **Plowhorse** | high | low | re-cost; a small price rise or a cheaper product of equal quality; do not remove |
| **Puzzle** | low | high | move it, rename it, describe it better, recommend it |
| **Dog** | low | low | replace, unless it has a reason to stay (a classic guests expect, an alcohol-free option) |

These actions are common practice, not laws; every case needs judgment (Loaded; Sandeep and Vinti's resort case study). Use at least four weeks of sales so one busy night does not decide.

Example: a bar's ten drinks over four weeks, 1,690 drinks sold, average popularity 10%, average gross profit 37.55 ILS. The Aperol Spritz sells 18.9% of all drinks but earns 34.27 ILS: a **plowhorse** (its pour cost is 22.2%; a 2 ILS rise or a better Prosecco price fixes it). The Espresso Martini, 14.2% and 39.60 ILS, is a **star**. The Dry Martini earns 46.03 ILS but sells 2.4%: a **puzzle** worth recommending. The Margarita, 8.3% and 35.25 ILS (pour cost 28.3%, because of an expensive tequila), is a **dog** by the numbers, but it is a classic guests ask for: re-cost it before you think of removing it. The full table is in the lab.

### Recommending and upselling responsibly

**Upselling** means offering something better or more (a premium spirit, a larger serve, food), and **suggestive selling** means recommending a specific drink. Both are normal parts of the job and help guests find what they will enjoy. Rules for doing it well (professional judgment):

1. **Start from the guest** ([lesson 07.6](../module-07/lesson-06.md)): what do they like, how strong, how sweet?
2. **Be specific and give the price**: "With Patrón instead of the house tequila it's 8 shekels more. Would you like that?" Never let the bill surprise anyone.
3. **Offer quality, not quantity**: a better spirit, a fresh garnish, a snack; not "make it a double" or another round.
4. **Offer water and alcohol-free drinks** as naturally as cocktails.
5. **Never upsell to a guest who shows signs of intoxication** and never suggest shots or doubles to speed up drinking (Module 9).
6. **Recommend puzzles and stars you genuinely like**: guests notice honesty and come back.

> Law checked 2026-10-18 (Consumer Protection Law on Nevo, sections 2 and 17D). ## Step by step

### Write the guest-facing menu

1. Group your drinks into 3–5 style sections plus "No alcohol"; order each section lightest to strongest.
2. For each drink write: name, 8–20 word description (base spirit, key flavours, texture or strength), allergen marks, price.
3. Check every description against the standard recipe: brands, "fresh", "homemade", "local", strength.
4. Add the allergy line and make sure every price is a total price in shekels.
5. Read it as a guest: can you tell which drink is strong, which is long, which has nuts?

### Engineer the menu

1. Export four or more weeks of sales per drink; take menu price and cost from the standard recipes.
2. Calculate net price, gross profit and popularity for each drink.
3. Calculate the average popularity and the sales-weighted average gross profit.
4. Classify each drink; choose one action per drink; re-check after the next four weeks.

### What you should see

Most first-draft descriptions are either too vague ("refreshing house favourite") or too long; after the rewrite each one says the base spirit, the main flavours and the strength in under 20 words. In the menu-engineering table the long, easy drinks sell most but often earn less per glass, and the stirred classics earn the most per glass but sell less; usually only one or two drinks are true stars. Your own menu should let a stranger answer three questions in seconds: which drink is strongest, which is longest, which contains nuts.

### What this practice cannot teach

Sales data from a real bar is messy: specials, events, a drink that sold out for a week, staff who push their own favourites. Guests also react to a menu's look, paper and lighting in ways a spreadsheet cannot show. You learn those effects by changing a real menu and watching the next month's sales.

## Common mistakes

- **Descriptions that promise what the glass does not deliver.** A brand that is not poured, "fresh" juice from a carton, "light" on a stirred drink. Fix: write the description from the standard recipe.
- **Fancy names with no explanation.** Guests do not order what they cannot picture. Fix: base spirit and two or three flavours after every invented name.
- **Hidden allergens.** Orgeat (almonds), egg white, Worcestershire (fish) not mentioned. Fix: mark them and add an allergy line.
- **Removing every dog at once.** Some dogs are classics or alcohol-free options guests expect. Fix: re-cost and re-describe first; replace only with a reason.
- **Judging by pour cost only.** A low-percentage drink can be a plowhorse in shekels. Fix: use gross profit per drink for menu engineering.
- **Upselling quantity of alcohol.** "Make it a double?" to a guest on their fourth drink. Fix: upsell quality, give the price, offer water and alcohol-free options, and never to an intoxicated guest.

## Key takeaways

- Group drinks by style, lightest first, with a real alcohol-free section and an allergy line.
- A description is the standard recipe in guest language: base spirit, key flavours, strength, allergens, in 8–20 words.
- Misleading guests about components, quantity, producer or name is prohibited by the Consumer Protection Law (section 2).
- Menu engineering compares each drink's popularity and gross profit with the menu averages: stars, plowhorses, puzzles, dogs.
- Upsell quality with a clear price; never push more alcohol.
