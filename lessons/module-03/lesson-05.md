# 03.5 · Dilution and temperature: what ice really does

Ice does two jobs at once: it makes a drink cold and it adds water. You cannot have one without the other, and the amount of each depends on how you mix, for how long, with what ice, and how strong the drink is. This lesson explains the physics in plain words, gives the measured numbers from published experiments, and shows how to measure dilution yourself with a scale. In the hands-on part you predict the result with the dilution simulation, then shake, stir and build the same non-alcoholic mix and weigh what the ice added.

## Why it matters

Two bartenders make the same sour from the same spec. One shakes for 4 seconds because the bar is busy; the drink is warm, strong-tasting and sweet. The other leaves the tin sitting in ice while taking a payment, then shakes again; the drink is thin and watery. Both used the right ingredients. Dilution and temperature are the invisible fourth and fifth ingredients of every shaken and stirred drink, and a working bartender controls them with time, ice and method, every single drink.

## Core ideas

### Chilling is melting

Ice cools a drink mainly by **melting**. Melting 1 g of ice absorbs about 80 calories of heat from the liquid around it, while warming 1 g of ice by one degree absorbs only about 0.5 calories. So nearly all the cooling comes from ice turning into water, and that water ends up in the drink. Dave Arnold, who measured this in his lab, summed it up: there is no chilling without dilution, and no dilution without chilling.

Two consequences:

- **Very cold freezer ice does not change much.** In Arnold's numbers, the extra cold in 150 g of ice at −10 °C equals the cooling of melting only about 9.5 g of ice. Freezer ice is usually around −18 °C, bar machine ice is near 0 °C; both chill drinks to almost the same place (Difford's Guide).
- **Wet ice adds water you did not ask for.** The thin film of water on ice that has sat in a well goes straight into the tin ([lesson 01.6](../module-01/lesson-06.md)).

### Why stronger drinks get colder and more diluted

Pure water cannot get below 0 °C with ice: at 0 °C it starts to freeze back. Alcohol lowers the freezing point, so a drink with more alcohol can keep melting ice and keep getting colder, below 0 °C. More melting means more water. So, for the same method and time:

- a strong stirred drink ends colder and more diluted than a weak one,
- a non-alcoholic mix stops near 0 °C and gets **less** dilution than a cocktail would.

That is why your water-and-syrup practice drinks dilute less than the real cocktails they stand in for.

### Shake, stir, build: what the experiments show

| Method | Time to full chill | Typical final temperature | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shake** (ניעור, *ni'ur*) | about 12–15 seconds | about −5 to −8 °C | Arnold: drink passed 0 °C in under 10 s and levelled off near −7 °C by 17 s; shaking style and ice type made little difference |
| **Stir** (ערבוב, *irbuv*) | minutes to reach the same point; usually stopped earlier | Arnold's best range for stirred drinks: about −0.5 to −7 °C | Fast stirring took about 45 s to get below 0 °C; Arnold suggests about 45 s of continuous stirring |
| **Build** on ice in the glass | keeps changing for many minutes | starts warmer, chills slowly | The drink keeps diluting while the guest drinks it |

**Dilution** (דילול, *dilul*) is measured as the water added, as a percentage of the drink before ice:

$$\text{dilution \%} = \frac{\text{weight after} - \text{weight before}}{\text{weight before}} \times 100$$

Published figures vary because they measure different drinks, ice and methods:

- Arnold rejects the often-repeated "every drink should be 25% water". In his measurements a shaken whiskey sour gained water equal to about **63%** of its starting volume, and a stirred Manhattan about **50%**.
- Difford's Guide reports about **20%** dilution from dry, freezer-style ice in a shaken drink.

Working ranges for this course, to be checked against your own measurements: shaken cocktails roughly half to three quarters of their starting volume in water; stirred spirit-forward drinks roughly 40–50%; non-alcoholic mixes shaken at home roughly 20–30%. These ranges are wide on purpose: the strength of the drink, the ice and the time all move the number.

### What dilution does to strength

Water adds volume but no alcohol, so the drink's strength falls:

$$\text{ABV after} = \frac{\text{ABV before}}{1 + \text{dilution}}$$

Example: a sour of 60 ml spirit at 40% with 22.5 ml juice and 22.5 ml syrup starts at 24 ml alcohol in 105 ml, about 22.9% ABV ([lesson 02.1](../module-02/lesson-01.md) shows how). After 60% dilution it is 22.9 ÷ 1.6 ≈ **14.3%** ABV, in a glass of about 168 ml. Dilution is why a cocktail with 60 ml of spirit is not "as strong as two shots in a glass".

### The model and the measurement

The course's dilution simulation is an **educational model**: it draws curves of dilution against time for each method, based on Arnold's published measurements of alcoholic drinks. Use it to predict, then compare with your scale. The model was fitted to cocktails, not to plain water and syrup, so at 0% ABV your own measurement is the better guide, especially for stirring.

> Temperatures and dilution figures come from Dave Arnold's experiments (2009–2010) and Difford's Guide, accessed 2026-10-17. They are physics and stay valid; the exact numbers in your kitchen depend on your ice, tin and timing.

## Step by step

### Measure dilution by weight

1. Mix the drink in a tin or jar without ice. Weigh it (tare the empty tin first). This is **weight before**.
2. Weigh the ice you will use (for example 150 g) so every run is the same.
3. Add ice and shake, stir or build for the planned time, timed with a phone.
4. Strain into a clean glass on the tared scale. This is **weight after**.
5. Calculate dilution %. Some liquid always stays on the ice (the **holdback**), so your number reads a little low; keep the method the same every run and the comparison stays fair.
6. If you have a probe thermometer, take the temperature in the glass straight away.

### Control dilution behind the bar

1. Use fresh, dry ice for every drink; never reuse ice from a shaken drink.
2. Fill the tin or mixing glass well with ice; a few cubes melt fast and chill poorly.
3. Shake hard for about 12–15 seconds; longer adds little.
4. Stir steadily; taste and stop when the drink is cold and the alcohol is softened (often 30–45 seconds).
5. Strain at once. A drink left sitting on ice keeps diluting.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- The practice sour (non-alcoholic): 60 ml water, 22.5 ml lemon juice, 22.5 ml 1:1 syrup per run, at room temperature.
- A shaker (or a jar with a tight lid), a mixing glass or second jar, a bar spoon or long spoon, a strainer, a glass.
- Ice cubes from the freezer, enough for 8 runs of 150 g; a kitchen scale; a phone stopwatch; a probe thermometer if you have one.
- The [dilution worksheet](../../templates/dilution-worksheet.md) (8 rows) and the [lab notes](../../labs/module-03/dilution-experiment.md).
- Low-cost alternative: a jam jar with a tight lid for shaking and a tall glass for stirring.

### Time

75 minutes: 10 to predict with the simulation, 50 for eight runs, 15 to calculate and compare.

### Steps

1. Open the simulation presets below. For each run in step 3, write your prediction for dilution % on the worksheet.
2. Let the ice sit out for 2 minutes before each run so it is equally "wet" every time (or always use it straight from the freezer; just be consistent).
3. Do these runs, with 150 g fresh ice each time: shake 5 s, shake 10 s, shake 15 s, shake 30 s; stir 15 s, stir 30 s, stir 45 s; build (pour over the ice in a glass, wait 2 minutes, then strain).
4. Weigh before and after each run; take the temperature if you can.
5. Calculate dilution % for every run and compare with your prediction and the model.
6. Taste the shake-5 s, shake-15 s and stir-45 s samples side by side. Which tastes best? Use lesson 03.3: colder and more diluted drinks taste less sweet.
7. Log your best shake time and stir time.

### What you should see

Shaking dilutes and chills fast: most of the change happens in the first 10 seconds and the 30-second run is only a little more diluted than the 15-second run. Expect roughly 20–30% dilution and a temperature near 0 °C after 15 seconds of shaking. Stirring is slower: after 45 seconds you will usually have less dilution and a warmer drink than after 15 seconds of shaking. Building on ice without stirring is the slowest. Your non-alcoholic mix will not go much below 0 °C; a real cocktail would get colder and take on more water. If two runs with the same method and time differ by more than about 5 percentage points, look at your ice (wet versus dry, amount) or your timing.

## Common mistakes

- **Under-shaking.** A 4–5 second shake leaves the drink warm, strong-tasting and too sweet. Fix: a hard 12–15 seconds.
- **Leaving a drink sitting on ice.** It keeps diluting. Fix: strain as soon as you stop.
- **Too little ice.** A few cubes melt fast and chill poorly. Fix: fill the tin or glass with ice.
- **Reusing shaker ice.** It is broken and wet and dilutes the next drink too fast; it is also a hygiene issue. Fix: fresh ice every drink.
- **Using one dilution figure for everything.** "25%" is not a rule. Fix: shaken, stirred and built drinks differ, and so do strong and light drinks; measure.
- **Forgetting dilution when batching.** Fix: lesson 03.1 batch water, using your measured percentage.

## Key takeaways

- Ice chills mainly by melting, so chilling always means dilution.
- Stronger drinks can get colder than 0 °C, so they melt more ice and dilute more.
- Shaking reaches about −5 to −8 °C in 12–15 seconds; stirring is slower and leaves drinks warmer and less diluted.
- Dilution % = (after − before) ÷ before × 100; ABV after = ABV before ÷ (1 + dilution).
- There is no single right dilution: measure, taste, and keep your method consistent.
