# 03.6 · Carbonation and how to keep it

Soda water, tonic, ginger beer, cola, beer and sparkling wine all carry bubbles of carbon dioxide, and guests notice at once when they are missing. A flat highball is one of the most common faults behind a bar, and almost always comes from how the drink was built, not from the soda. This lesson explains where the bubbles come from, what makes them escape, and the building habits that keep them in the glass. In the hands-on part you build one highball carefully and one carelessly, then watch, taste and weigh the difference.

## Why it matters

A guest orders a gin and tonic, takes a sip and says, "This is flat." You opened the tonic an hour ago, poured it warm from the back bar onto three cubes and stirred it hard. The guest is right, and a remade drink costs you the tonic, the gin and the guest's patience. Highballs are among the simplest and most ordered drinks at many bars, so they are judged hard: the fizz is the drink. Handling carbonation well is a small set of habits that you can practise at home with a bottle of soda water.

## Core ideas

### What carbonation is

**Carbonation** (a carbonated drink is מוגז, *mugaz*; soda water is סודה, *soda*) is carbon dioxide gas (CO2, פחמן דו-חמצני, *pachman du-chamtsani*) dissolved in a liquid. Producers force CO2 into cold water under pressure and seal the bottle. Inside the closed bottle, the gas above the liquid is almost pure CO2 at high pressure, which keeps a lot of CO2 dissolved. When you open it, the pressure drops and the extra gas starts to leave as bubbles.

The rule behind this is **Henry's law**: the amount of gas that dissolves in a liquid is proportional to the pressure of that gas above it. Two bar consequences follow:

- **Pressure:** once open, a bottle loses fizz until it is resealed; a tightly closed cap lets pressure build up again and slows the loss.
- **Temperature:** cold liquids hold more CO2 than warm ones. Warm soda goes flat faster and foams more when poured.

### Where bubbles form

Bubbles do not appear from nowhere. They grow at **nucleation sites**: tiny scratches and imperfections in the glass, the rough surfaces of ice, bits of pulp and other solids in the drink. More sites mean faster loss of CO2. So:

- crushed or chipped ice flattens a fizzy drink faster than solid cubes,
- pulp from unstrained juice makes a drink foam and flatten,
- a dusty or scratched glass gives streams of bubbles that empty the drink of gas.

### Agitation flattens

Anything that shakes the liquid speeds up the escape: pouring from a height, splashing onto ice, stirring hard, and of course shaking. Never shake a carbonated ingredient in a closed tin (Module 4 returns to this): the pressure can force the tin open and spray the drink.

### Carbonation changes taste

Dissolved CO2 forms a little **carbonic acid**, and soda water has a pH between about 5 and 6. Fizzy drinks therefore taste slightly sharper than the same mix made with still water, and the tingle reads as freshness. A sour mix that is balanced with still water may need a touch more sweetness when topped with soda (Camper English). You will taste this in the hands-on part.

### Dilution in a highball

A highball (a spirit topped with a larger amount of a mixer, served on ice in a tall glass, see [lesson 01.5](../module-01/lesson-05.md)) is **built**: made directly in the glass. It is not shaken or stirred to full chill, so the ice keeps melting while the guest drinks. Cold soda and a glass full of cold ice keep it fizzier and less watery for longer, because less ice needs to melt to cool it.

### Keeping soda behind the bar

| Habit | Why |
|---|---|
| Keep mixers in the fridge, not on the back bar | cold holds more CO2 and melts less ice |
| Small bottles or cans for low-volume mixers | an open bottle loses fizz every time it is opened (professional judgment) |
| Reseal tightly, right after pouring | pressure builds back and slows the loss |
| Do not shake or drop bottles | agitation creates foam and loses gas on opening |
| Label and date opened bottles; taste before use | flat mixer makes a flat drink |

Many bars use a **soda gun**, a hand-held dispenser that mixes carbonated water with syrup on demand; it is cleaned and checked daily (Module 8).

> [!WARNING]
> CO2 is stored under high pressure in cylinders, soda-siphon chargers and home carbonators. Use only the maker's bottles, chargers and instructions, never over-fill or over-pressurise, and keep cylinders upright and secured. Many home carbonators are made for water only; adding juice or syrup before carbonating can make them foam over. Never use dry ice in drinks.

> [!NOTE]
> The physics here does not go out of date. Product advice (mixer brands, bottle sizes, soda guns) changes; check your venue's equipment manuals. Checked 2026-10-17.

## Step by step

### Build a highball that stays fizzy

1. Chill everything you can: mixer in the fridge, glass from the fridge or filled with ice water while you prepare.
2. Fill the glass to the top with solid, dry ice cubes.
3. Measure the non-carbonated ingredients into the glass (spirit, juice, syrup). If there are several, mix them first in a tin or glass so the soda needs less stirring.
4. Open the mixer just before pouring.
5. Pour the mixer gently down the inside of the glass or down the stem of a bar spoon, low and steady, not from a height onto the ice.
6. Lift once, gently: slide the bar spoon down to the bottom and draw it up once to bring the heavier ingredients up. Do not stir round and round.
7. Garnish and serve straight away. Reseal the mixer bottle at once.

### Store an opened bottle

1. Cap it tightly immediately after pouring.
2. Put it back in the fridge, upright.
3. Write the opening time on the label if your bar does that.
4. Before using it again, pour a little into a glass and taste: if it is flat, do not use it for a drink.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- Two identical tall glasses, ice cubes (enough to fill both) plus a little crushed ice, a bar spoon or long spoon, a jigger, a kitchen scale, a phone timer, kitchen paper.
- Two 330 ml cans or two small bottles of soda water: one from the fridge, one left at room temperature for a few hours.
- Lemon juice and 1:1 syrup, still water.
- The worksheet [labs/module-03/fizz-test.md](../../labs/module-03/fizz-test.md) and your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: supermarket soda water in small bottles is enough; do not split one big bottle into a jug to warm half of it, because it loses gas while it waits. Any tall glasses and a teaspoon will do.

### Time

45 minutes: 10 to prepare, 20 for the fizz test, 10 for the taste test, 5 to log.

### Steps

1. **Careful highball (A).** Cold glass, filled with ice cubes. Add 22.5 ml lemon juice and 15 ml syrup. Open the cold soda and pour about 150 ml gently down the bar spoon. One gentle lift.
2. **Careless highball (B).** Room-temperature glass with three cubes and a handful of crushed ice. Same lemon and syrup. Open the warm soda, pour about 150 ml from 20 cm above the glass. Stir round ten times.
3. Wipe the outside of both glasses dry (cold glasses collect water from the air, which adds weight) and weigh each at once. Write the weights down.
4. Taste each with a straw at 0, 5 and 10 minutes. Rate fizz from 1 to 5 and note sweetness and sourness.
5. At 10 minutes, wipe and weigh again. Weight lost is mostly CO2 that escaped (melted ice stays in the glass, so it does not change the weight).
6. **Tartness test.** Make your balance-point mix from lesson 03.3 twice: once topped with still water, once with cold soda water. Taste both. Which is sharper?
7. Optional: open the dilution lab's build preset and see how the model's dilution of a built drink keeps climbing over time.
8. Record everything and write one sentence on what made the biggest difference.

### What you should see

Highball A keeps steady, fine bubbles and still tastes lively at 10 minutes. Highball B foams when poured, settles quickly and is noticeably flatter and more watery by 5 minutes; the crushed ice and stirring speed this up. B usually loses more weight than A, often a gram or two more, though a scale with 1 g steps may only just show it (professional judgment): the taste difference is the stronger evidence. In the tartness test the soda version tastes sharper and fresher than the still version, even though the recipe is the same.

### What this practice cannot teach

Draught systems, soda guns and sparkling wine each have their own pressure, temperature and pouring rules (Modules 7 and 8), and a real bar's mixers may differ from supermarket soda in fizz and sweetness.

## Common mistakes

- **Warm mixer from the back bar.** It goes flat fast and melts more ice. Fix: keep mixers cold.
- **Too little ice.** A few cubes melt fast and the drink warms. Fix: fill the glass with solid cubes.
- **Pouring from a height onto ice.** Fix: pour low and gently down the side or a spoon.
- **Stirring round and round.** Fix: one gentle lift from the bottom.
- **Leaving bottles open.** Fix: reseal at once and keep them in the fridge.
- **Shaking a carbonated ingredient.** It can force the tin open. Fix: add soda after shaking, in the glass.

## Key takeaways

- Carbonation is CO2 held in the liquid by pressure; cold liquids hold more of it (Henry's law).
- Bubbles escape at nucleation sites (scratches, ice surfaces, pulp) and with agitation.
- Build highballs cold, on a full glass of solid ice, soda last, poured gently, one lift.
- Carbonated drinks taste a little more sour; balance with that in mind.
- CO2 equipment is pressurised: follow the maker's instructions, never use dry ice.
