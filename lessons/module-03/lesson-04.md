# 03.4 · Syrups and juices: making, measuring and storing

Almost every sour, collins and daiquiri-style drink is built on two homemade ingredients: sugar syrup and fresh citrus juice. They are cheap and simple, which is exactly why bars get them wrong: the syrup is measured in cups one day and grams the next, the juice is a day old, and nobody knows when the bottle was opened. This lesson shows how to make syrups by weight, what the ratios and degrees Brix mean, how to swap one syrup for another, how much juice your fruit really gives and how long each keeps. In the hands-on part you make both syrups, measure them, juice and measure your citrus, and run a freshness taste test.

## Why it matters

The head bartender's spec says 15 ml of syrup. You use the 2:1 bottle someone made last week instead of the 1:1, and every sour that night is too sweet. Or the lime juice was squeezed yesterday afternoon and left out: the drinks taste dull and slightly bitter, and the guest who ordered a second one switches to beer. Syrups and juice are where consistency is made or lost before service even starts. Knowing what is in the bottle, and how old it is, protects the drink and the guest.

## Core ideas

### Sugar syrup and its ratios

**Sugar syrup** (סירופ סוכר, *sirop sukar*), also called simple syrup, is sugar dissolved in water. It mixes into a cold drink instantly, where dry sugar would not. Two strengths are standard:

- **1:1 ("simple")**: one part sugar to one part water.
- **2:1 ("rich")**: two parts sugar to one part water. Thicker, sweeter per ml, and adds less water to the drink.

This course makes syrups **by weight** (grams on a scale), because a cup of sugar can hold more or less sugar depending on how it settles; weight is the same every time. Some bars and books mix by volume (cups); that gives a slightly less sweet syrup, so always ask which method a spec assumes.

### Degrees Brix

**Degrees Brix (°Bx)** is the percentage of sugar by weight in a solution: 1 °Bx = 1 g of sugar in 100 g of syrup.

| Syrup | Made by weight | °Brix | Density (g/ml) | Made by volume (Difford's Guide) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1:1 | 500 g sugar + 500 g water | 50 | 1.23 | about 48 °Bx |
| 2:1 | 1,000 g sugar + 500 g water | 66.7 | about 1.33 | about 65 °Bx |

Bars measure Brix with a **refractometer**, a small optical tool; at home you can check the density instead (weight ÷ volume). Lesson 03.2 used these densities to weigh syrup.

### How much syrup a batch gives

Sugar takes up less space dissolved than you would expect, so 1 kg of ingredients does not make 1 litre.

$$\text{volume (ml)} = \frac{\text{sugar (g)} + \text{water (g)}}{\text{density}}$$

500 g sugar + 500 g water = 1,000 g ÷ 1.23 ≈ **813 ml** of 1:1 syrup. 1,000 g sugar + 500 g water = 1,500 g ÷ 1.33 ≈ **1,130 ml** of 2:1.

### Swapping 1:1 and 2:1

2:1 is not twice as sweet per ml. Made by weight, a ml of 2:1 holds about 0.89 g of sugar (1.33 × 0.667) and a ml of 1:1 about 0.62 g (1.23 × 0.5), so 2:1 is about **1.4 times** as sweet per ml; Difford's Guide, making both by volume, found 1.35 times. Either way, to swap, use about two thirds of the amount:

| 1:1 syrup | 2:1 syrup for the same sweetness |
|---|---|
| 7.5 ml | 5 ml |
| 15 ml | 10 ml |
| 22.5 ml | 15 ml |
| 30 ml | 20 ml |

The drink made with 1:1 will be slightly larger and a little more diluted (Difford's Guide estimates 5–8% more dilution). That is usually fine; just do not mix the two bottles up.

### Other syrups

The same method works for **honey syrup** (honey thinned with warm water so it pours), **demerara syrup** (brown sugar, more caramel taste), and **flavoured syrups** (ginger, herbs, spices). Commercial bottled syrups exist too; check their sweetness against your house syrup before swapping. Modules 5 and 6 tell you which drinks use which.

### Citrus juice: yield

**Yield** is how much usable juice you get from your fruit. It depends on the fruit's size, season, temperature (room-temperature fruit gives more than cold) and your juicer. In one test by Dave Arnold, a machine juicer got about 20% more juice than hand pressing from the same pile of limes. Do not trust a number from a book: measure your own, per fruit and per 100 g, as you started in [lesson 01.4](../module-01/lesson-04.md). Module 10 turns yield into cost per ml.

### Citrus juice: freshness and storage

Fresh juice changes from the moment it is squeezed. In a blind tasting run by Dave Arnold, most tasters preferred hand-squeezed lime juice that was about **4 hours old** to juice squeezed minutes before; almost nobody picked the freshest. He kept the juice covered, and called the result a first finding that needs more testing. Beyond that point, juice loses its bright aroma and many bartenders find lime juice tastes dull or bitter by the next day (professional judgment).

Fresh juice is **untreated** (not pasteurised), and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration warns that untreated juice can carry harmful bacteria from the fruit and should be kept refrigerated; children, older adults, pregnant women and people with weak immune systems are most at risk. So:

- wash the fruit before cutting,
- squeeze close to service, strain out seeds and pulp,
- keep it cold in a clean, covered, labelled bottle,
- use it within the shift and throw away what is left at close, unless the venue's written policy says otherwise (professional judgment).

### Labels and shelf life

Every bottle you make gets a label (תווית, *tavit*): name, date and time made, your initials. Keep syrups refrigerated in clean bottles. Shelf life depends on how clean the process was: as a working rule, 1:1 syrup is kept about one to two weeks and 2:1 longer, because more sugar leaves less free water for microbes (professional judgment; no official shelf life was found for bar syrups). Throw a syrup away if it is cloudy, has anything floating, fizzes or smells sour or yeasty. Never pour new syrup on top of old ("topping up"): the old, possibly contaminated syrup ends up in the new batch.

> [!NOTE]
> Brix and density are physical values. Shelf-life figures for syrups and juice here are professional judgment, not official limits; follow your venue's written food-safety policy and local rules (Module 9 covers Israeli requirements). Checked 2026-10-17.

## Step by step

### Make 1:1 syrup by weight (hot method)

1. Wash your hands and use a clean pot, spoon and bottle.
2. Put the pot on the scale and tare. Weigh 500 g water, then 500 g white sugar.
3. Warm gently, stirring, until the liquid is completely clear. Do not boil: boiling evaporates water and makes the syrup stronger than 1:1.
4. Take it off the heat and let it cool, covered.
5. Pour into a clean bottle through a funnel. Label: "Syrup 1:1", date, time, initials. Refrigerate.

### Make 1:1 syrup cold (no heat)

1. Weigh sugar and water into a clean jar or blender in equal weights.
2. Shake or blend until clear, 1–3 minutes. Superfine sugar dissolves fastest.
3. Bottle and label as above.

### Make 2:1 syrup

1. Weigh 2 parts sugar to 1 part water (for example 400 g and 200 g).
2. Warm gently and stir; it takes longer to dissolve. Do not boil.
3. Cool, bottle, label "Syrup 2:1". If sugar crystals form later, warm the bottle gently in hot water to redissolve.

### Prepare juice for service

1. Wash the fruit. Roll it under your palm on the board for more juice.
2. Cut and press as in [lesson 01.4](../module-01/lesson-04.md).
3. Strain through a fine strainer into a clean bottle.
4. Label name, time and initials. Keep it in the fridge or on ice.
5. At close, discard leftover juice (or follow your venue's policy).

## Hands-on

### What you need

- A kitchen scale (1 g steps), a small pot, a spoon, a measuring jug with ml marks, two clean bottles or jars with lids, a funnel, labels or tape and a marker.
- 450 g white sugar, water.
- 3–4 lemons or limes, a hand press, a fine strainer, a jigger.
- The worksheet [labs/module-03/syrup-juice-log.md](../../labs/module-03/syrup-juice-log.md) and your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: the cold method in a jar needs no pot or stove; any jug with ml marks will do.

> [!WARNING]
> Hot syrup sticks to skin and burns more than hot water. Warm it gently, keep the pot handle turned inward, let it cool before bottling, and never pour hot syrup into a closed bottle. Cut citrus on a stable board as in lesson 01.4.

### Time

75 minutes of activity, plus a 4-hour wait for the freshness test: 25 to make both syrups, 15 to measure them, 20 to juice and measure citrus, 15 for the sweetness swap and freshness tastings.

### Steps

1. **1:1 syrup.** Weigh 250 g sugar and 250 g water. Make it with the hot or cold method. When cool, weigh it (subtract the jar) and pour it into the jug to read the volume. Density = grams ÷ ml.
2. **2:1 syrup.** Weigh 200 g sugar and 100 g water, warm until clear, cool, weigh and measure the same way.
3. Label both bottles and put them in the fridge.
4. **Juice yield.** Weigh each fruit. Juice them one by one, strain, and measure each in the jigger or jug. Work out ml per fruit and ml per 100 g of fruit.
5. **Freshness.** Put 40 ml of the juice in a small covered, labelled jar in the fridge. Use the rest now.
6. **Sweetness swap.** Make two cups of 100 ml cold water + 20 ml fresh juice. Add 15 ml of 1:1 to one and 10 ml of 2:1 to the other. Have someone shuffle them and taste blind. Can you tell which is which?
7. **Four hours later**, repeat one cup (100 ml water + 20 ml juice + 15 ml 1:1) with the stored juice and one with juice squeezed now. Taste both blind.
8. Record everything on the worksheet.

### What you should see

The 1:1 syrup weighs about 500 g and fills about 405 ml (density about 1.23). The 2:1 weighs about 300 g and fills about 225 ml (density about 1.33). If your 1:1 reads much heavier per ml, you boiled off water. The two sweetness-swap cups taste very close; many people cannot pick them apart, though the 2:1 cup can feel slightly richer. Juice yield varies widely, so your own numbers are the useful ones; write them down, you will use them in Module 10. In the freshness test the 4-hour juice may taste rounder or simply different; Arnold's tasters preferred it, but your fruit and palate may disagree.

### What this practice cannot teach

A bar makes syrup in litres, juices kilos of fruit before service and follows a written label and discard policy; you practise the method and the measurement, and learn the venue's own rules on your first shifts.

- [ ] Both bottles are labelled with name, date, time and initials, and are in the fridge.
- [ ] I measured juice yield per fruit and per 100 g and recorded it.
- [ ] I compared 15 ml 1:1 with 10 ml 2:1 in a blind tasting.

## Common mistakes

- **Boiling the syrup.** Water evaporates and the syrup gets stronger, so every drink gets sweeter. Fix: warm until clear, no more.
- **Mixing up 1:1 and 2:1.** A 2:1 poured as 1:1 adds about 40% more sugar. Fix: label clearly, keep them in fixed places, use one house syrup where possible.
- **Measuring sugar in cups.** The result changes with how the sugar settles. Fix: weigh.
- **Topping up old syrup with new.** Fix: finish or discard the old bottle, wash it, then refill.
- **Juice left out all shift.** It warms, loses aroma and is a food-safety risk. Fix: keep it in the fridge or on ice and discard at close.
- **Not straining juice.** Pulp and seeds clog strainers and speed pourers and make drinks cloudy. Fix: always fine-strain.

## Key takeaways

- Make syrups by weight: 1:1 is 50 °Brix (1.23 g/ml), 2:1 is about 67 °Brix (about 1.33 g/ml).
- Swap 1:1 for 2:1 with about two thirds of the amount (15 ml → 10 ml).
- Juice yield varies: measure your own fruit per piece and per 100 g.
- Fresh juice is best squeezed shortly before service, kept cold and used within the shift.
- Label everything with name, date, time and initials; never top up old with new.
