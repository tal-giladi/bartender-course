# 03.3 · Taste and balance: sweet, sour, bitter, salt and aroma

A guest says a drink is "too sour". Is it really too much lemon, or too little sugar, too warm, too diluted, or missing the aroma that would have made it taste rounder? This lesson gives you the vocabulary and the cause-and-effect rules to answer that question: the five basic tastes, aroma, texture and temperature, and how they push and pull on each other. In the hands-on part you mix plain water, lemon and syrup, change one thing at a time, and find your own balance point; no alcohol is needed.

## Why it matters

Recipes are written for an ideal lemon, an ideal ice machine and an ideal guest. Real lemons vary, ice gets wetter through the night, and guests ask for "less sweet". A bartender who only follows numbers can repeat a recipe; a bartender who understands balance can taste a drink, name what is wrong and fix it with one small change before it leaves the bar. That skill is the difference between "it's fine" and a guest who comes back. Lesson 03.7 uses everything here to diagnose broken drinks.

## Core ideas

### Taste is only part of flavour

**Taste** (טעם, *ta'am*) is what the tongue detects. There are five basic tastes:

| Taste | Hebrew | Where it comes from in a drink |
|---|---|---|
| Sweet | מתוק (*matok*) | sugar syrup, liqueurs, juices, vermouth, honey |
| Sour | חמוץ (*chamutz*) | citrus juice, other acids, a little from carbonation |
| Bitter | מר (*mar*) | bitters, tonic, aperitivo liqueurs, citrus pith, over-brewed tea |
| Salty | מלוח (*maluach*) | a few drops of salt solution, a salt rim, tomato juice |
| Umami (savoury) | אומאמי (*umami*) | tomato juice and other savoury ingredients; rare in cocktails |

**Aroma** is what the nose detects, and most of what people call "flavour" is aroma. You smell a drink twice: through the nose as you lift the glass, and from inside the mouth as you swallow and breathe out (called **retronasal smell**). Retronasal smell plays the biggest role in the sensation of flavour. That is why a citrus twist, whose oils you smell rather than taste, changes a drink so much, and why food tastes flat when you have a cold.

**Texture** (or mouthfeel) is what the mouth feels: **body** (thin or rich; sugar adds body), **astringency** (the dry, gripping feeling from tannin in strong tea or red wine), the tingle of **carbonation** and the warmth or burn of alcohol.

**Flavour** (טעם וריח, *ta'am ve-re'ach*) is all of these together.

### How tastes push and pull

The tastes do not simply add up; they change how each other is perceived.

- **Sweet and sour balance each other.** More sugar makes the same acid taste less sharp; more acid makes the same sugar taste less sweet. This is the most used rule behind the bar (professional judgment). It also means "too sour" can be fixed with sugar or with less acid, and you choose by what the spec says.
- **Salt suppresses bitterness.** In a 1997 study, sodium salts reduced bitterness more than they reduced sweetness, so sweet and other pleasant flavours stood out more. A few drops of salt solution in a bitter drink make it rounder without tasting salty.
- **Cold reduces sweetness.** Sweet taste responses get stronger as temperature rises (research on the TRPM5 taste channel, 2005, measured between 15 and 35 °C). A drink balanced at room temperature tastes less sweet and sharper when ice cold, and a cold drink tastes sweeter as it warms in the glass.
- **Dilution lowers everything.** Water reduces sweetness, sourness, bitterness and alcohol burn together. A drink that tastes too strong and too sweet in the tin may be right after proper ice (lesson 03.5).
- **Carbonation adds a sharp edge.** Dissolved CO2 forms a little carbonic acid, so fizzy drinks taste slightly more sour (lesson 03.6).

### Strength of the usual ingredients

| Ingredient | What it adds | Rough strength |
|---|---|---|
| Lemon juice | sour, aroma | about 5–6% citric acid |
| Lime juice | sour, aroma | a little less citric acid than lemon, plus its own aroma |
| 1:1 sugar syrup | sweet, body | half sugar by weight |
| 2:1 sugar syrup | sweet, more body | two thirds sugar by weight (lesson 03.4) |
| Bitters | bitter, aroma | a dash (0.8 ml) is noticeable |
| Saline (20% salt solution) | salty, less bitter | 1–2 drops per drink |

Fruits vary by season and variety, so the same ml of juice can be sharper or softer from one week to the next. That is why bartenders taste.

### What "balanced" means

A **balanced** drink is one where no element sticks out unpleasantly: you taste the base, the sweet and the sour together, the finish is clean, and you want another sip. There is no single correct point: the spec sets the target, and guests have preferences. Many sour-style drinks start near 2 parts spirit to ¾ part citrus to ¾ part syrup (professional judgment; Module 6 covers families), but a non-alcoholic mix behaves differently because it has no alcohol warmth or body.

### Tasting like a professional

- **Change one variable at a time.** If you change sugar and temperature together, you cannot tell which did what. Keep volume, glass, temperature and order the same; change only the thing you are testing.
- **Small sips, water between.** After about six samples your palate tires (professional judgment). Take a break, drink water, smell your sleeve to reset your nose.
- **Taste with a straw or clean spoon**, never from the guest's glass (hygiene; see [lesson 01.7](../module-01/lesson-07.md)).
- **Write it down** with numbers (1–5 for sweet, sour, bitter) and words. Memory is unreliable after the third cup.

> [!NOTE]
> The taste interactions here come from sensory research and long bar practice; they are stable. Citrus acidity varies by fruit and season, so numbers in the table are typical values (checked 2026-10-17), not guarantees.

## Step by step

### Taste a drink in a fixed order

1. **Look and smell** before you taste: aroma, clarity, any off-smell.
2. **Temperature and dilution:** is it cold enough, thin or harsh? (Fix these first; they change everything else.)
3. **Sweet versus sour:** which one arrives first, which one lingers?
4. **Bitter and salt:** is bitterness pleasant or sharp? Is anything salty?
5. **Texture:** thin, rich, gripping, fizzy?
6. **Finish:** clean, or does one note hang on?
7. **Decide one change**, make it, taste again.

### Run a one-variable test

1. Write the base recipe and what you will change (for example, syrup 5 → 25 ml in 5 ml steps).
2. Make every sample the same volume, at the same temperature, in the same kind of glass.
3. Number the cups on the bottom so you can taste without seeing which is which (ask someone to shuffle them).
4. Taste in order from mildest to strongest, with water between.
5. Rate each from 1 to 5 and pick your preferred sample.
6. Repeat with the order reversed to check yourself.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- 2–3 lemons, a hand press, a jigger, a measuring jug, sugar syrup (1:1), cold water, ice.
- 6 small identical glasses or cups and a marker, straws or teaspoons.
- Strong black tea (two bags in 200 ml water, brewed 10 minutes, cooled) and salt.
- The worksheet [labs/module-03/balance-experiment.md](../../labs/module-03/balance-experiment.md) and the [tasting notes template](../../templates/tasting-notes.md).
- Low-cost alternative: bottled lemon juice works for the method, though it tastes duller; disposable cups instead of glasses.

> [!CAUTION]
> Cut citrus on a stable, dry board with the knife technique from [lesson 01.4](../module-01/lesson-04.md).

### Time

50 minutes: 10 to prepare, 15 for the syrup ladder, 10 for salt and bitterness, 10 for temperature, 5 to log.

### Steps

1. **Syrup ladder.** In each of five cups put 100 ml cold water and 20 ml lemon juice. Add syrup: 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 ml. Number the cups underneath.
2. Taste them from least to most sweet. Rate sweet and sour from 1 to 5 and circle your favourite. Taste again in reverse order. Your favourite is your **balance point** for this mix.
3. **Salt on bitter.** Make saline: dissolve 5 g salt in 20 g warm water (a 20% solution by weight). Split the strong tea into two cups of 50 ml. Add 2 drops of saline to one. Taste both. Rate bitterness and astringency.
4. **Temperature.** Make your balance-point mix twice. Stir one with ice for 20 seconds and strain it; leave the other at room temperature. Taste the cold one, then the warm one. Which tastes sweeter? Which is sharper?
5. **Aroma.** Take the cold sample, cut a strip of lemon peel, squeeze it over the glass skin side down, and taste again. What changed?
6. Record everything on the worksheet and in your tasting notes.

### What you should see

Most people choose the 15 or 20 ml cup in the syrup ladder, but the answer depends on your lemons and your palate; that is the point (professional judgment). The salted tea tastes less bitter and somehow "smoother", but should not taste salty; if it does, you added too much. The room-temperature sample tastes noticeably sweeter and softer than the iced one, even though the recipe is the same, and a little diluted. The expressed peel makes the drink smell and taste more of lemon without changing its sourness much: that is aroma at work.

### What this practice cannot teach

Alcohol adds warmth, sweetness and body of its own and makes the bite of acid feel different, so a balance point found with water will shift when you make real cocktails; the method of changing one thing at a time stays the same.

- [ ] I tasted the five-step syrup ladder in both directions and found my balance point.
- [ ] I compared tea with and without saline and recorded the difference.
- [ ] I compared the same mix cold and at room temperature and can say which tasted sweeter.
- [ ] I tasted the effect of expressed lemon peel.
- [ ] My ratings and notes are on the worksheet and in my tasting notes.

## Common mistakes

- **Changing two things at once.** You cannot tell what helped. Fix: one variable per test, everything else fixed.
- **Judging balance warm.** A drink tasted from the tin before ice tastes sweeter than it will be cold. Fix: taste at serving temperature.
- **Adding sugar to fix bitterness.** It masks a little but makes the drink cloying. Fix: try 1–2 drops of saline first.
- **Too much salt.** If you can taste salt, it is too much. Fix: drops, not pinches.
- **Ignoring aroma.** A flat drink often lacks smell, not sugar. Fix: check the twist, the freshness of the juice and the glass.
- **Tasting from the guest's drink.** Fix: a straw or clean spoon from the tin, before pouring.

## Key takeaways

- Taste is sweet, sour, bitter, salty and umami; aroma and texture complete flavour, and retronasal smell matters most.
- Sweet and sour balance each other; salt suppresses bitterness; cold hides sweetness; dilution softens everything.
- Balanced means no element sticks out; the spec sets the target, your palate checks it.
- Test one variable at a time, at serving temperature, with notes.
