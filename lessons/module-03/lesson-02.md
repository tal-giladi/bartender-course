# 03.2 · Measuring and pouring accurately

In lesson 01.3 you learned to fill a jigger with water. Real ingredients are harder: syrups cling, liqueurs pour slowly, and some bars expect you to pour without a jigger at all. This lesson defines what "accurate" means in numbers, shows how to measure thick liquids and check them by weight, and teaches free pouring with a speed pourer and a count. In the hands-on part you calibrate your own count and test it on a scale.

## Why it matters

Your bar's house pour is 30 ml. You free-pour a little generously, about 35 ml every time. That is 5 ml, or 17%, extra spirit in each drink: the drink is stronger than the menu says, it tastes different from your colleague's, and over a busy night it is several bottles the bar paid for and did not sell. It also matters for responsible service: a guest counting drinks trusts that a drink is a drink. Accuracy is not about being fussy; it is how a recipe, a price and a promise to the guest stay true.

## Core ideas

### Accuracy, consistency and tolerance

- **Accuracy** is how close a pour is to the target.
- **Consistency** is how close your pours are to each other.
- **Tolerance** is how far off a pour may be and still count as right. This course uses **±5%** for every measured pour, the same figure used in the final technique assessment.

$$\text{error \%} = \frac{\text{measured} - \text{target}}{\text{target}} \times 100$$

A 30 ml target with 32 ml measured is +6.7%: out. 29 ml is −3.3%: in. At 30 ml, ±5% is 28.5–31.5 ml; on a scale with 1 g steps that is 29–31 g of water.

### Thick liquids cling

Sugar syrup, honey syrup and cream liqueurs are **viscous**: thick and slow-flowing. They stick to the inside of a jigger, so the same quick tip that empties a jigger of water leaves 1–2 ml of syrup behind (professional judgment; you measure it in the hands-on). Three habits help:

- Tip fully and hold the jigger upside down over the tin for a beat before you take it away.
- If a recipe has both thin and thick ingredients, measure the thin ones first so the jigger is wet; syrup slides out of a wet jigger more easily (professional judgment).
- Rinse the jigger in the dump sink or with water between a thick ingredient and the next drink.

### Checking by weight: density

A scale is the most accurate measuring tool you have, but it weighs grams, not ml. **Density** is the weight of 1 ml of a liquid. Water is 1.00 g/ml, so 30 ml weighs 30 g. Sugar syrup is heavier because sugar is dense:

| Liquid | Density (g/ml, about 20 °C) | 30 ml weighs | 22.5 ml weighs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | 1.00 | 30 g | 22.5 g |
| 1:1 syrup by weight (50% sugar) | 1.23 | 37 g | 27.7 g |
| 2:1 syrup by weight (67% sugar) | about 1.33 | 40 g | 30 g |

$$\text{grams} = \text{ml} \times \text{density} \qquad \text{ml} = \text{grams} \div \text{density}$$

Spirits are slightly lighter than water, so 30 ml of vodka weighs a little under 30 g. Lesson 03.4 explains where the syrup numbers come from.

### Free pouring

**Free pouring** means pouring from a bottle fitted with a **speed pourer** (פורר, *porer*: a metal or plastic spout that gives a steady stream) without a jigger, timing the pour with a silent count. Many bartenders count four beats for 1 oz, so one count is about 7.5 ml (VinePair, from a working bartender). Everyone's count is a little different, and the flow changes with:

- the pourer (metal or plastic, wide or narrow),
- the liquid (syrups and liqueurs flow slower than spirits),
- temperature (a spirit from the freezer pours slower),
- how full the bottle is and how fast you turn it over.

So a count is not a fixed number: it is a rhythm you **calibrate** (check and adjust against a measure) and re-check regularly. Bars decide whether you jigger or free-pour; many require jiggers, at least for expensive spirits and complex recipes. Follow the house rule.

### Where error hides

| Fault | Effect |
|---|---|
| Jigger not level | under-pour |
| Slow or partial tip | under-pour, worse with syrup |
| Liquid domed over the brim | over-pour |
| Free pour: no start count for the first air bubble | under-pour |
| Free pour: pausing after the last count before turning up | over-pour |
| Pouring while looking at the guest | random error, both ways |

> [!NOTE]
> The four-counts-per-ounce rhythm is common bartender practice, not a standard. Densities are physical values at about 20 °C (checked 2026-10-17); they change slightly with temperature.

## Step by step

### Measure syrup with a jigger

1. Hold the jigger level over the tin.
2. Pour until the syrup reaches the brim or line; syrup settles slowly, so wait half a second and top up if needed.
3. Tip fully and hold it inverted for a beat.
4. Scrape the rim once on the tin edge if a drop hangs.

### Free pour with a count

1. Hold the bottle by the neck, index finger resting on the base of the pourer so it cannot fall out.
2. Turn the bottle over quickly and fully, to at least 45° and ideally nearly vertical, so the stream starts at once.
3. Count from the moment the stream starts: "one, two, three, four" at your rhythm.
4. On the last count, turn the bottle up quickly with a small twist to cut the stream. Do not pause.
5. Check against a jigger or the scale often, not only when learning.

### Calibrate your count

1. Fill a bottle with water and fit a speed pourer.
2. Free-pour a "4 count" into a jigger held over a tin.
3. If the jigger overflows, speed up your count; if it is short, slow it down.
4. Repeat until three pours in a row fill the jigger to the brim, then test on the scale.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- A bottle (a clean spirit or wine bottle) filled with water, fitted with a speed pourer.
- A jigger, a tin or glass, a kitchen scale with 1 g steps, a towel.
- Sugar syrup (1:1 by weight, from lesson 03.1 or freshly made).
- The drill sheet: [labs/module-03/free-pour-drill.md](../../labs/module-03/free-pour-drill.md).
- Your practice log ([template](../../templates/practice-log.md)).
- Low-cost alternative: a speed pourer costs only a few shekels in kitchen or bar-supply shops (professional judgment). Without one, do the jigger and syrup parts only, and practise the free-pour movement with the bottle's own neck, which is less even.

### Time

50 minutes: 10 to calibrate, 20 for the free-pour test, 10 for the syrup test, 10 to clean up and log.

### Steps

1. Calibrate your 4 count with the jigger as in the steps above.
2. Tare the empty tin. Free-pour five 4-count pours, weighing and taring each time. Write each weight on the drill sheet.
3. Repeat with five 2-count pours (target 15 ml) and five 6-count pours (target 45 ml).
4. Mark each pour in or out of tolerance and work out the error % of your worst pour.
5. Syrup test: tare the tin, jigger 22.5 ml of 1:1 syrup five times, weighing each. Target 27.7 g; in tolerance (±5%) is 26.3–29.1 g, so 27–29 g on a 1 g scale.
6. Repeat the syrup test once with a deliberate slow tip and compare.
7. Optional: run your converted numbers through the recipe scaler's convert preset to check the oz values.
8. Log your count, the in-tolerance totals and one thing to change.

### What you should see

On a first session most learners get 2–3 of 5 free pours in tolerance at 30 ml, and fewer at 15 ml, where a fraction of a second is a big percentage (professional judgment). Consistent under-pours mean the bottle turns over too slowly or you start counting before the stream; consistent over-pours mean you pause before cutting. The syrup jigger usually reads 1–2 g under target with a normal tip and more with a slow tip: that is clinging, and it is why the inverted hold matters.

### What this practice cannot teach

Real spirits pour slightly faster or slower than water depending on the product and temperature, and a real shift adds noise and conversation, so keep checking your count against a jigger during real service.

### Completion checklist

- [ ] I calibrated my 4 count against a jigger.
- [ ] I free-poured and weighed five pours at each of 15, 30 and 45 ml and marked tolerance.
- [ ] At least 3 of 5 pours at 30 ml were within ±5%, or I logged what to fix and will repeat.
- [ ] I weighed five jigger pours of syrup and can explain the difference from water.
- [ ] The session is in my practice log.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting the count without checking it.** Your rhythm drifts with speed and mood. Fix: check against a jigger at the start of every practice or shift.
- **Slow bottle turn.** The first second is a trickle, so the pour is short. Fix: turn fast and fully; start the count on the stream.
- **Weighing syrup as if it were water.** 22.5 ml of 1:1 syrup is 27.7 g, not 22.5 g. Fix: grams = ml × density.
- **Leaving syrup in the jigger.** Fix: full tip, hold inverted for a beat.
- **Free-pouring when the bar requires jiggers.** Fix: ask and follow the house rule; accuracy is part of the job, not a style.

## Key takeaways

- Error % = (measured − target) ÷ target × 100; this course's tolerance is ±5%.
- Thick liquids cling: tip fully and hold the jigger inverted for a beat.
- Check by weight with density: water 1.00, 1:1 syrup 1.23, 2:1 syrup about 1.33 g/ml.
- A free-pour count (often four beats per 30 ml) is a personal rhythm: calibrate it and keep checking.
- Small over-pours add up to stronger drinks and lost bottles.
