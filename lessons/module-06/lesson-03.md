# 06.3 · French 75, Tom Collins and Paloma

All three of these drinks are long and fizzy, and all three are sours underneath: spirit, citrus and something sweet. What changes is the lengthener. The French 75 is topped with Champagne, the Tom Collins with soda water, and the Paloma gets its sweetness and its fizz from a grapefruit soda. So the skill this lesson trains is the top: adding a fizzy liquid to a base so the drink keeps its bubbles and does not foam over the glass. You will also learn why the Tom Collins has no IBA page of its own, what the John Collins is, and why the IBA Paloma has a pinch of salt in it.

## Why it matters

It is brunch, and six French 75s are on one ticket. If you pour the Champagne fast onto a shaken base, each flute foams over, you wait, top again, and lose half the bubbles and a minute per drink. Or a guest asks for a Tom Collins and your bar has no Old Tom gin. You need to know what to say: what you can make, and what to call it.

## Core ideas

### The top: keeping the fizz

You learned in [lesson 03.6](../module-03/lesson-06.md) that carbon dioxide (CO2) escapes faster when the liquid is warm, when it is poured hard, and when it hits rough surfaces or a lot of tiny bubbles and ice chips. A shaken base is full of fine air bubbles and ice chips, so sparkling wine poured onto it foams strongly. Three habits keep the fizz:

- **Everything cold.** Chilled glass, chilled sparkling wine or soda, base strained cold.
- **Pour down the side.** Tilt the glass or pour onto the bar spoon so the top slides in rather than plunges.
- **One lift, not a stir.** Put the spoon to the bottom and lift once ([lesson 04.2](../module-04/lesson-02.md)). The IBA says "stir gently"; one or two lifts are enough.

Difford's Guide adds a trick for the French 75: pour half the sparkling wine into the flute first, then the strained base, then the rest. The base mixes in as it falls and there is less foam, so no stir is needed.

### Champagne and other sparkling wines

**Champagne** is sparkling wine from the Champagne region of France; **Prosecco** (Italy) and **cava** (Spain) are other sparkling wines ([lesson 02.8](../module-02/lesson-08.md)). The IBA French 75 names Champagne. Most bars use a dry ("brut") sparkling wine they already pour by the glass; tell the guest if it is not Champagne, because the name on the menu creates an expectation (professional judgment). Opening sparkling wine safely is taught step by step in Module 7 ([lesson 07.4](../module-07/lesson-04.md)).

> [!WARNING]
> A sparkling-wine cork can fly out at high speed and injure an eye. Keep a thumb or a cloth on the cork, point the bottle away from people and never open it by shaking.

### Tom, John and the Collins family

The IBA has no "Tom Collins" page. Its **John Collins** is 45 ml gin, 30 ml lemon, 15 ml syrup and 60 ml soda, built in a highball full of ice, and the page adds a note: use **Old Tom gin** for a Tom Collins. Old Tom is an older, slightly sweeter and softer style of gin ([lesson 02.2](../module-02/lesson-02.md)). Difford's Guide quotes the drinks writer David Embury (1948): the Tom Collins was made with Old Tom gin and the John Collins with Holland gin (jenever), and the names got mixed up once London dry gin replaced both. Today many bars make a "Tom Collins" with London dry gin. More on naming in [lesson 06.6](lesson-06.md).

Two methods are common: the IBA builds the Collins in the glass and stirs gently; Difford's Guide shakes the gin, lemon and syrup, strains them over fresh ice and tops with soda. Shaking mixes and chills better; building is faster in a rush (professional judgment).

### The Paloma: salt and grapefruit

**Paloma** means "dove" in Spanish. The IBA spec is a highball: tequila, a squeeze of lime (5 ml), a pinch of salt and pink grapefruit soda. Salt in small amounts reduces the perception of bitterness and makes fruit taste brighter ([lesson 03.3](../module-03/lesson-03.md)); in a grapefruit drink you do not taste "salty", you taste less bitter grapefruit. Too much and it does taste salty, so keep it to a small pinch.

| | French 75 | Tom Collins | Paloma |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base | gin 30 ml | Old Tom gin 45 ml | tequila 50 ml |
| Citrus | lemon 15 ml | lemon 30 ml | lime 5 ml |
| Sweet | syrup 15 ml | syrup 15 ml | from the soda |
| Top | Champagne 60 ml | soda 60 ml | pink grapefruit soda 100 ml |
| Ice in glass | none (flute) | full highball | full highball |

> [!NOTE]
> Specs checked on iba-world.com and diffordsguide.com on 2026-10-17. Pink grapefruit sodas differ a lot in sweetness between brands; taste the one your bar stocks.

## Step by step

### French 75

| Ingredient | ml | oz |
|---|---|---|
| Gin | 30 | 1 |
| Fresh lemon juice | 15 | ½ |
| Sugar syrup (1:1) | 15 | ½ |
| Champagne (brut) | 60 | 2 |

- **Method:** shake gin, lemon and syrup with ice; strain into a chilled flute; top with Champagne; stir gently (one lift).
- **Glass and ice:** chilled Champagne flute, no ice.
- **Garnish:** none in the IBA spec; a lemon twist is common.
- **Profile and key technique:** dry, bright, festive: a gin sour lifted by wine bubbles. The key is the top: cold wine, poured down the side, so it does not foam over.
- **Spec source and variations:** [IBA — French 75](https://iba-world.com/iba-cocktail/french-75/). Variation: [Difford's Guide French 75](https://www.diffordsguide.com/cocktails/recipe/790/french-75) is 45 ml London dry gin, 15 ml lemon, 1½ bar spoons powdered sugar (or about 7.5 ml 2:1 syrup), 75 ml brut sparkling wine; sugar stirred into the lemon, gin added, shaken, fine strained into a chilled flute, topped; lemon twist. The same page notes that before the 1980s–90s the drink was usually served long over ice in a Collins glass.
- **Substitutions:** any dry (brut) sparkling wine, such as cava or a dry Prosecco (say so to the guest); cognac for gin is a known variation (the "French 125" on Difford's Guide).
- **Home-practice version:** 30 ml cold water + 15 ml lemon + 15 ml 1:1 syrup, shaken, strained into a flute and topped with 60 ml cold soda water.
- **Alcohol-free analogue:** an alcohol-free gin alternative (or juniper tea) with lemon and syrup as above, topped with alcohol-free sparkling wine or dry sparkling white grape juice. Close and guest-ready.
- **Common mistakes:** warm wine (big foam, flat drink); stirring hard; a sweet sparkling wine plus full syrup (too sweet); overfilling the flute.

### Tom Collins

| Ingredient | ml | oz |
|---|---|---|
| Old Tom gin | 45 | 1½ |
| Fresh lemon juice | 30 | 1 |
| Simple syrup (1:1) | 15 | ½ |
| Soda water | 60 | 2 |

- **Method:** build directly in a highball filled with ice; stir gently.
- **Glass and ice:** highball (Collins) glass, filled with ice cubes.
- **Garnish:** lemon slice and maraschino cherry.
- **Profile and key technique:** long, cold, lemony and lightly sweet, with a soft gin. The key is a full glass of ice and a gentle stir so the soda keeps its fizz.
- **Spec source and variations:** [IBA — John Collins](https://iba-world.com/iba-cocktail/john-collins/), whose note says to use Old Tom gin for a Tom Collins; there is no separate IBA Tom Collins page. Variation: [Difford's Guide Tom Collins](https://www.diffordsguide.com/cocktails/recipe/1972/tom-collins) is 52.5 ml Old Tom gin, 22.5 ml lemon, 15 ml 2:1 syrup, 52.5 ml soda; the first three shaken, strained into an ice-filled Collins glass, topped with soda and stirred briefly; orange wheel and cherry.
- **Substitutions:** London dry gin if there is no Old Tom (strictly a John Collins by the IBA's note; tell the guest); 2:1 syrup at about two-thirds the volume.
- **Home-practice version:** 45 ml cold water + 30 ml lemon + 15 ml 1:1 syrup, built over ice, topped with 60 ml soda: a real, drinkable lemonade soda.
- **Alcohol-free analogue:** the same with an alcohol-free gin alternative or a few drops of juniper or rosemary tea. Close; guest-ready.
- **Common mistakes:** half a glass of ice (warm and watery fast); stirring the fizz out; calling it a Fizz (a fizz has no ice).

### Paloma

| Ingredient | ml | oz |
|---|---|---|
| Tequila (100% agave) | 50 | 1⅔ |
| Fresh lime juice | 5 | ⅙ |
| Salt | a pinch | — |
| Pink grapefruit soda | 100 | 3⅓ |

- **Method:** pour the tequila into a highball, squeeze in the lime, add ice and the salt, fill with pink grapefruit soda, stir gently.
- **Glass and ice:** highball glass, ice cubes.
- **Garnish:** lime slice.
- **Profile and key technique:** refreshing, bittersweet grapefruit with agave and a hint of salt. The key is the small pinch of salt: it softens the bitterness without tasting salty.
- **Spec source and variations:** [IBA — Paloma](https://iba-world.com/iba-cocktail/paloma/). Variation: [Difford's Guide Paloma](https://www.diffordsguide.com/cocktails/recipe/1456/paloma) is 50 ml blanco tequila, 25 ml fresh pink grapefruit juice, 12.5 ml lime, 7.5 ml agave syrup, shaken and strained into an ice-filled Collins glass, topped with 50 ml pink grapefruit soda; optional salt rim; grapefruit wedge.
- **Substitutions:** any grapefruit soda; if none, 50 ml fresh grapefruit juice + 10 ml 1:1 syrup + 50 ml soda water. Mezcal for tequila is a known variation (smoky).
- **Home-practice version:** 50 ml cold water + 5 ml lime + a pinch of salt + 100 ml grapefruit soda, built.
- **Alcohol-free analogue:** fresh grapefruit juice and lime with a pinch of salt, topped with grapefruit soda or soda water. Close and popular as a guest drink.
- **Common mistakes:** a spoonful of salt instead of a pinch; flat soda from an open bottle; mixto tequila instead of 100% agave without telling the guest.

### Top a shaken base with sparkling wine

1. Chill the flute and the wine (or soda for practice).
2. Shake and strain the base. For several drinks, line up the flutes and strain each base in turn.
3. Tilt the flute about 45° and pour the wine slowly down the inside wall, or onto the back of a bar spoon held in the glass.
4. Stop at the fill line, about 1 cm under the rim; wait 2 seconds if it foams, then finish.
5. One lift with the bar spoon. Garnish and serve straight away.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- A shaker or jar, a jigger, a strainer, a bar spoon, two flutes (or narrow wine glasses), two highballs, ice.
- 3 lemons, 1 lime, 1:1 syrup, salt, 1 litre of cold soda water, a cold bottle of grapefruit soda (or a grapefruit, syrup and soda), optionally a bottle of alcohol-free sparkling wine.
- A phone timer, a ruler, your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: soda water stands in for all the sparkling tops; any tall glasses work.

### Time

50 minutes: 10 to set up, 30 for the drinks, 10 to taste and record.

### Steps

1. Recite the three specs, then check them.
2. **French 75 style, fast top.** Shake 30 ml water, 15 ml lemon, 15 ml syrup; strain into a flute; pour 60 ml cold soda straight down the middle quickly. Measure the foam height and time the drink until it is full and settled.
3. **French 75 style, slow top.** Repeat, but tilt the glass and pour down the side, then one lift. Measure and time again.
4. **Collins built vs shaken.** Make one Tom Collins style built in the glass (IBA method) and one with the base shaken and strained over fresh ice, then topped (Difford's method). Taste both after 1 minute and after 5 minutes.
5. **Paloma with and without salt.** Build two Paloma-style drinks (50 ml water, 5 ml lime, 100 ml grapefruit soda), one with a small pinch of salt. Taste them blind if someone can hand them to you.
6. Record foam, times and tasting notes.
7. Run the simulation for the Module 6 cards.

### What you should see

The fast top foams up near or over the rim and takes several seconds to settle; you may need to top again. The slow, tilted pour gives a lower foam and the drink is ready in one pour. With practice the slow top is not slower overall, because there is no second pour.

The shaken Collins is colder and better mixed at first; the built one may taste sweeter at the bottom until it is stirred. After 5 minutes they taste closer. The salted Paloma tastes less bitter and more "grapefruit" but not salty; most people pick it as the better drink without knowing why.

<details>
<summary>Reference result</summary>

Fast top: foam 3 cm, spilled once, 22 s to full. Slow top: foam 1 cm, 14 s to full. Collins: shaken "colder, even"; built "sweet at the bottom, fine after a lift". Paloma blind test: picked the salted one, "rounder, less bitter".

### What this practice cannot teach

Soda water foams less than real Champagne, which has more dissolved gas and some proteins that hold foam, so the real top needs even more care; practise with real sparkling wine at a bar or with an alcohol-free sparkling wine.

## Common mistakes

- **Warm sparkling wine or soda.** Big foam, flat drink. Fix: keep tops in the fridge and open them just before use.
- **Pouring the top down the middle.** Foams over. Fix: tilt, pour down the side or over a spoon.
- **Stirring a fizzy drink like a Martini.** The bubbles go. Fix: one or two lifts.
- **Calling a London dry gin Collins a "Tom Collins" without thinking.** Fix: know the IBA note; if the guest cares, explain what you are using.
- **Too much salt in the Paloma.** It tastes salty. Fix: a small pinch between two fingers.
- **A half-full glass of ice in a Collins.** Fix: fill the glass with ice before any liquid.

## Key takeaways

- French 75 (IBA): 30 ml gin, 15 ml lemon, 15 ml syrup, shaken, strained into a flute, topped with 60 ml Champagne.
- Tom Collins: the IBA John Collins (45 gin, 30 lemon, 15 syrup, 60 soda, built on ice) made with Old Tom gin.
- Paloma (IBA): 50 ml 100% agave tequila, 5 ml lime, a pinch of salt, 100 ml pink grapefruit soda, built on ice.
- Keep tops cold, pour down the side, lift once.
- A pinch of salt cuts bitterness without tasting salty.
