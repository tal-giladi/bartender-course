# 06.6 · Substitutions, adaptations and naming around the world

Every bar runs out of something, every shift brings a guest who cannot or will not drink alcohol, eggs or nuts, and every traveller orders a drink by the name they learned at home. This lesson gives you a method for all three. Substitutions work when you replace an ingredient with one that plays the same role in the drink and adjust for sweetness and strength. Adaptations work when you keep the balance of the original and tell the guest what changed. Naming problems disappear when you know the handful of names that mean different things in different places, and ask one short question when in doubt.

## Why it matters

The Cointreau is finished, a guest wants a Margarita, and another asks for a "Mai Tai without nuts". A tourist orders a "Martini" and means an Espresso Martini; a regular asks for a "Tom Collins" and your bar has only London dry gin. In each case there is an honest, fast answer, and a wrong one that sends a drink back or, with allergies, puts a guest at risk.

## Core ideas

### Substitute by role, then adjust

Every ingredient has a **role** ([lesson 05.1](../module-05/lesson-01.md)): base, modifier, sour, sweet, lengthener or accent. Replace like with like inside the same role, then check two things: is the substitute sweeter or less sweet, and stronger or weaker?

| Missing | Substitute | Adjust | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1:1 syrup | 2:1 syrup | use about ⅔ of the volume ([lesson 03.4](../module-03/lesson-04.md)) | 15 ml 1:1 ≈ 10 ml 2:1 |
| Superfine sugar | 1:1 syrup | 2 bar spoons sugar ≈ 15 ml 1:1 ([lesson 05.4](../module-05/lesson-04.md)) | syrup is faster and dissolves fully |
| Triple sec brand A | triple sec brand B or orange curaçao | taste; add 5 ml syrup if drier | same role: sweet + orange |
| Liqueur as sweetener | syrup + a flavour | less volume of syrup (it is sweeter), and the drink gets weaker | changes the drink; tell the guest |
| Lemon | lime (or the reverse) | usually 1:1 | similar sourness, different aroma (professional judgment) |
| Old Tom gin | London dry gin | consider 5 ml more syrup | Old Tom is slightly sweeter |
| Champagne | another dry (brut) sparkling wine | none | say so if the menu says Champagne |
| Egg white | aquafaba | 1:1 | vegan; tell the guest it is chickpea |
| Bourbon | rye whiskey | none | spicier, drier |
| Campari | another red bitter aperitif | taste | Aperol is much less bitter: not a like-for-like swap |

Two rules keep substitutions honest:

1. **If the name promises it, say so.** A "Sidecar" with Spanish brandy, a "French 75" with cava, a "Margarita" with mixto tequila: still good drinks, but tell the guest before they pay (professional judgment; menu honesty is covered in Module 10).
2. **If the role cannot be filled, change the drink, not the name.** A Mai Tai has no substitute for orgeat; a Penicillin without the smoky float is just a honey-ginger whisky sour. Offer another drink from the same family.

### Adapting a classic

**Alcohol-free.** Replace the spirit with something that brings body and flavour, not just water: a non-alcoholic spirit alternative, strong tea, a juice with tannin (grape, cranberry), or a little more of the modifier. Alcohol tastes slightly sweet and gives weight, so alcohol-free versions often need a touch less syrup or a little more body (professional judgment; taste and adjust by 5 ml). You have made many of these on the recipe cards in this module.

**Lower alcohol.** Three common ways: halve the base and keep the rest (a "half-strength" sour); turn a short drink into a long one (a sour lengthened with soda becomes a Collins, [lesson 06.1](lesson-01.md)); or use a lower-alcohol base, such as vermouth or an aperitif, in a spritz.

**Allergies and diet.** Know the hidden ones in this module: egg (Pisco Sour, foamed sours), almond (orgeat, Mai Tai), fish (Worcestershire, Bloody Mary), celery (celery salt), honey (Penicillin; not vegan), and sulphites, which wine-based ingredients such as vermouth and sparkling wine contain; Difford's Guide lists sulphites as an allergen for sweet vermouth. Leave the ingredient out only if the drink still works; otherwise offer another. Never guess. The legal side is in Module 9.

### Names that mean different things

| What the guest says | What they may mean | What to ask or do |
|---|---|---|
| "Tom Collins" | the IBA John Collins with Old Tom gin; most bars use London dry gin | make it; if asked, explain the gin. Difford's Guide quotes Embury (1948): Tom = Old Tom gin, John = Holland gin (jenever) |
| "Caipirinha with vodka" | a Caipiroska (IBA note); with rum, a Caipirissima | use the right name |
| "Buck's Fizz" | the IBA Mimosa: orange juice and Prosecco, "also known as Buck's Fizz" | make the Mimosa spec |
| "Gimlet" | gin and lime cordial (the classic); some bars use fresh lime and syrup | ask "with lime cordial, or fresh lime?" |
| "Martini" | a Dry Martini, a vodka Martini, or any drink in a V-shaped glass (Espresso Martini, Porn Star Martini) | ask "a classic Martini, gin or vodka, or the espresso one?" |
| "Straight up" | to some, served up (chilled, no ice); to others, neat (room temperature, no ice) | ask "chilled and strained, or neat?" (professional judgment) |
| "Margarita" / "Daiquiri" | the shaken classic or a frozen, blended version | ask "classic shaken, or frozen?" ([lesson 05.4](../module-05/lesson-04.md)) |

### Measures around the world

Recipes travel in different units:

- **US recipes** are written in fluid ounces. This course uses 1 oz = 30 ml, the common bar convention ([lesson 03.1](../module-03/lesson-01.md)).
- **In the UK**, the law sets the measure for gin, rum, vodka and whisky sold by the glass: 25 ml or 35 ml, or multiples, and not both on the same premises. Other spirits have no specified quantity ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/weights-measures-and-packaging-the-law/specified-quantities)). A UK spec with "50 ml gin" is a double 25.
- **IBA specs** are in ml, often in 15 ml steps, with some 7.5 and 22.5 ml amounts.
- **In Israel**, this course found no statutory standard spirit measure in this pass; follow the house spec (not verified; Module 9 covers Israeli alcohol law).

> [!NOTE]
> Checked on iba-world.com, diffordsguide.com and gov.uk on 2026-10-17. Brand recipes, allergen labels and the UK rules can change; read the current label and the current law.

## Step by step

### Make a substitution during service

1. Name the missing ingredient's role (base, modifier, sour, sweet, lengthener, accent).
2. Pick a replacement with the same role from what you have.
3. Ask: sweeter or drier? stronger or weaker? Adjust by the table above, in 5 ml steps.
4. Taste a drop with a clean straw.
5. If the drink's name promises the missing ingredient, tell the guest before serving: "We are out of Cointreau, I'm using another triple sec, it's a touch drier."
6. Write it on the shift notes so the next bartender and the order list know.

### Adapt for an alcohol-free, lower-alcohol or allergy request

1. Listen for the reason: no alcohol at all, less alcohol, or an ingredient to avoid. Ask if it is an allergy or a preference; an allergy means clean tools and no guessing.
2. Choose the method: replace (alcohol-free), reduce or lengthen (lower alcohol), remove or switch drinks (allergy).
3. Keep the balance: same sour-to-sweet relationship as the original, adjusted by taste.
4. Give it an honest name: "a Virgin Mary", "an alcohol-free French 75-style", "a half-strength Whiskey Sour". For an alcohol-free drink, check that every product in it is alcohol-free (bitters contain alcohol).

### Clear up an ambiguous name

1. Repeat the name back with the two possible meanings: "A Martini: a classic gin or vodka Martini, or the espresso one?"
2. Offer the one detail that decides it (lime cordial or fresh lime; frozen or shaken; chilled or neat).
3. Confirm, then make it. One question costs five seconds; a remade drink costs two minutes.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- The worksheet [labs/module-06/substitution-worksheet.md](../../labs/module-06/substitution-worksheet.md) and a pen.
- A shaker or jar, a jigger, a strainer, two tall glasses, two flutes, ice.
- 3 lemons, 1:1 and 2:1 syrup ([lesson 03.4](../module-03/lesson-04.md)), soda water, cold water, and a juniper, rosemary or black tea for the alcohol-free base.
- Your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: any strong tea works as the "body" ingredient.

### Time

45 minutes: 20 for the worksheet, 20 for the two builds, 5 to record.

### Steps

1. Answer the eight scenarios in the worksheet in writing, then check them against [substitution-worksheet-solution.md](../../labs/module-06/substitution-worksheet-solution.md).
2. **Syrup swap, blind.** Build two Collins-style drinks: 45 ml water, 30 ml lemon, soda to top; one with 15 ml 1:1 syrup, one with 10 ml 2:1. Ask someone to swap them around, then taste and guess which is which.
3. **Alcohol-free French 75 style, two bases.** Shake 15 ml lemon and 15 ml 1:1 syrup with (a) 30 ml cold water or (b) 30 ml cold, strong juniper, rosemary or black tea. Strain into flutes and top with 60 ml soda. Compare body and length of flavour.
4. Adjust version (b) once by taste (5 ml lemon or syrup) and write your final alcohol-free spec.
5. Record everything; run a round of the family drill.

### What you should see

On the worksheet, most learners get 6–7 of 8 on the first try; the usual misses are the nut-allergy Mai Tai (the safe answer is a different drink, not a smaller amount of orgeat) and the "straight up" question.

In the blind syrup test, most people cannot tell the two Collins drinks apart reliably, because ⅔ of the volume of 2:1 gives about the same sweetness. The water-based French 75 style tastes thin and short; the tea-based one has more body and a longer finish, which is why alcohol-free drinks need a "body" ingredient.

### What this practice cannot teach

At home you choose the substitute calmly; on shift you decide in seconds, with what is on the shelf and a guest waiting, and you learn your bar's house rules about what may be swapped without asking a manager.

### Completion checklist

- [ ] I completed the substitution worksheet and checked it.
- [ ] I swapped 1:1 for 2:1 syrup at ⅔ volume and tasted the result blind.
- [ ] I built an alcohol-free sparkling sour with a "body" ingredient and wrote my spec.
- [ ] I can ask the clarifying question for "Martini", "Gimlet" and "straight up".

## Common mistakes

- **Swapping by colour or bottle shape.** Aperol for Campari, sweet vermouth for dry. Fix: swap within the role and taste.
- **Keeping the name after the drink changed.** Fix: tell the guest, or rename it.
- **Reducing an allergen instead of removing it.** A little orgeat is still almond. Fix: remove it or offer another drink.
- **Water as the alcohol-free base.** Thin and short. Fix: tea, juice with tannin, or an alcohol-free spirit.
- **Assuming one meaning of "Martini" or "straight up".** Fix: one clarifying question.
- **Bitters in an "alcohol-free" drink without saying so.** Fix: leave them out or tell the guest.

## Key takeaways

- Substitute inside the same role, then adjust for sweetness and strength; 2:1 syrup is about ⅔ the volume of 1:1.
- If the name promises an ingredient you do not have, tell the guest; if a role cannot be filled, offer another drink.
- Alcohol-free versions need a body ingredient; lower-alcohol versions halve, lengthen or use a lower-ABV base.
- Several names mean different things in different places: ask one short question.
- UK law sets 25 ml or 35 ml measures for gin, rum, vodka and whisky; US recipes are in oz (course: 1 oz = 30 ml).
