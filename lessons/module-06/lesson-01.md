# 06.1 · Cocktail families: the patterns behind the recipes

In Module 5 you learned twelve drinks one by one. This module teaches more than twenty more, and that only works if you stop seeing them as separate recipes. Most cocktails belong to a small number of families, and each family has a pattern: which kinds of ingredients, in roughly which amounts, made with which method, served in which glass. Once you know the pattern, a new drink is just the family plus a few differences. This lesson gives you the ten families this course uses, shows how to sort a drink you have never seen, measures the sour pattern from real IBA specs, and ends with one non-alcoholic base served three different ways.

## Why it matters

A guest asks for "something like a Whiskey Sour, but with gin". Another wants "a long, fizzy drink that isn't too sweet". A new menu arrives with fourteen drinks and you have one shift to learn them. In each case the bartender who thinks in families has an answer in seconds: a gin sour, a Collins, "these four are sours, these three are highballs, only the differences need learning". The bartender who memorised isolated recipes has to guess.

## Core ideas

### What a family is

A **cocktail family** is a group of drinks that share the same structure: the same kinds of ingredients in similar proportions, the same method and usually the same type of glass. Difford's Guide, a large drinks reference, compares the cocktail world to families with shared "genetics", and lists dozens of them ([Difford's Guide — Cocktail categories/families](https://www.diffordsguide.com/encyclopedia/1989/cocktails/cocktail-categories/families)). Different books group drinks slightly differently. This course uses ten families, the same ones the course's recipe library and simulations use, because ten is enough to cover every drink you will learn and few enough to remember.

You already met four simple templates in [lesson 05.1](../module-05/lesson-01.md) (spirit-forward stirred, sour, highball, sparkling or coffee). The families below split those templates more finely.

### The ten families

| Family | Pattern | Usual method | Usual glass and ice | Drinks you know or will learn |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sour | base spirit + citrus + sweetener (syrup or liqueur), no fizz | shake | chilled glass, no ice; or rocks glass on ice | Daiquiri, Margarita, Whiskey Sour, Cosmopolitan, White Lady, Sidecar, Pisco Sour, Gimlet, Caipirinha |
| Collins and fizz | a sour lengthened with soda water | shake then top (fizz), or build (Collins) | tall glass; Collins on ice, fizz without ice | Tom Collins, Gin Fizz |
| Highball | spirit + a larger amount of fizzy mixer, often a little citrus | build | highball on ice | Mojito, Moscow Mule, Paloma, Cuba Libre |
| Old fashioned | spirit + a little sugar + bitters (+ water) | build and stir | rocks glass, large cube or cubes | Old Fashioned |
| Martini and Manhattan (spirit-forward stirred) | base spirit + aromatised wine or bitter aperitif, no citrus | stir | chilled glass, no ice; or rocks on a large cube | Dry Martini, Manhattan, Negroni, Boulevardier |
| Spritz and sparkling | sparkling wine as the lengthener | build (or shake, then top) | wine glass on ice, or flute | Aperol Spritz, French 75, Hugo Spritz |
| Tiki and punch | rum (often several), several juices and syrups, spice or nut | shake | rocks or tiki glass, crushed ice | Mai Tai |
| Savory | salt, spice, tomato or other savory ingredients | stir or roll | rocks or highball | Bloody Mary |
| Coffee | coffee with spirit and liqueur | shake | chilled glass, no ice | Espresso Martini |
| Other | anything that does not fit the above | varies | varies | (none yet in this course) |

Three new words in that table:

- **Lengthener**: a large, usually non-alcoholic mixer that makes the drink long, such as soda, tonic, ginger beer or sparkling wine.
- **Aromatised wine**: wine flavoured with herbs and spices and slightly fortified, such as vermouth ([lesson 02.7](../module-02/lesson-07.md)).
- **Tiki**: a style of rum drink invented in American "Polynesian" restaurants from the 1930s: several rums, lime, syrups such as **orgeat** (an almond syrup), crushed ice and big garnishes. You meet it in [lesson 06.4](lesson-04.md).

### How to sort a drink you have never seen

Ask four questions in this order. The first "yes" usually gives the family.

1. **Is there coffee?** → coffee.
2. **Is there tomato, salt as a main flavour, or spice sauces?** → savory.
3. **Is there citrus?**
   - With several rums, orgeat or several juices, served on crushed ice → tiki and punch.
   - Topped with sparkling wine → spritz and sparkling.
   - Topped with soda water → Collins and fizz.
   - Topped with another fizzy mixer (cola, ginger beer, grapefruit soda), or a small citrus squeeze in a long fizzy drink → highball.
   - No top at all → sour.
4. **No citrus?**
   - Sparkling wine is the long part → spritz and sparkling.
   - A fizzy mixer is the long part → highball.
   - Spirit + sugar + bitters only → old fashioned.
   - Spirit + vermouth or bitter aperitif → martini and Manhattan.

The method follows the same logic you learned in [lesson 04.1](../module-04/lesson-01.md): citrus, egg, cream or coffee → shake; only spirits and wines → stir; a fizzy mixer → build, fizz last.

> [!TIP]
> Families are a memory tool, not a law. A Mojito is built like a highball but tastes like a long sour; the Caipirinha is a sour that is built in the glass. When a drink sits between two families, learn it as "family A, with this difference".

### The sour pattern, measured

The sour is the biggest family, so it is worth measuring. Here are IBA sours you know or will learn (all ml, from the IBA pages checked 2026-10-17):

| Drink | Base | Citrus | Sweetener |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daiquiri | rum 60 | lime 20 | 2 bar spoons sugar (≈ 15 ml 1:1 syrup) |
| Margarita | tequila 50 | lime 15 | triple sec 20 |
| Whiskey Sour | bourbon 45 | lemon 25 | 1:1 syrup 20 |
| White Lady | gin 40 | lemon 20 | triple sec 30 |
| Sidecar | cognac 50 | lemon 20 | triple sec 20 |
| Pisco Sour | pisco 60 | lemon 30 | syrup 20 |
| Gin Basil Smash | gin 60 | lemon 22.5 | syrup 22.5 |
| Tommy's Margarita | tequila 60 | lime 30 | agave nectar 30 |

What the numbers say:

- The base is 40–60 ml, usually about twice the citrus.
- The citrus is 15–30 ml.
- When the sweetener is a syrup, it is usually a little less than or equal to the citrus. When it is a **liqueur** (a sweetened spirit such as triple sec), there is often as much liqueur as citrus, or more, because a liqueur is less sweet than syrup and also adds alcohol and flavour.

A common starting ratio that many bartenders use for a new sour is 60 ml base : 22.5 ml citrus : 22.5 ml 1:1 syrup, then adjusted by taste (professional judgment; it matches the IBA Gin Basil Smash exactly). Treat it as a check, not a rule: if a new sour spec has 45 ml of lemon and 10 ml of syrup, something is unusual and worth a second look.

### One base, three serves

The sour, the Collins and the fizz share the same three ingredients and differ only in how they are served:

| | Sour | Collins | Fizz |
|---|---|---|---|
| Example (IBA) | Whiskey Sour 45 / 25 / 20 | John Collins: gin 45, lemon 30, syrup 15, soda 60 | Gin Fizz: gin 45, lemon 30, syrup 10, splash of soda |
| Method | shake, strain | build in the glass, stir gently | shake, strain, top with a splash of soda |
| Ice in the glass | none (or on the rocks) | yes, glass filled with ice | none (IBA: "serve without ice") |
| Glass | cocktail or rocks | highball / Collins | thin tall tumbler |

The IBA builds the John Collins directly in the glass; Difford's Guide shakes the Collins base and tops it with soda over ice ([Difford's Guide — Collins cocktails](https://www.diffordsguide.com/encyclopedia/490/cocktails/collins-cocktails)). Both are common. The fizz is the one most often confused: it has no ice in the glass and less soda, so it is shorter and colder at first, and it should be drunk quickly.

> [!NOTE]
> Specs and family descriptions checked on iba-world.com and diffordsguide.com on 2026-10-17. The IBA updates its list from time to time (the current list is grouped as "The Unforgettables", "Contemporary Classics" and "New Era Drinks"); the families themselves do not go out of date.

## Step by step

### Sort an unfamiliar spec

1. Read the whole ingredient list once, without amounts.
2. Run the four questions above (coffee, savory, citrus, then the long part). Write the family next to the drink.
3. Check the method against the family. If the spec says "stir" but there is citrus, re-read it: either it is an exception (the Bloody Mary) or you misread it.
4. Check the amounts against the family pattern. For a sour: base 40–60 ml, citrus 15–30 ml, sweetener close to the citrus.
5. Write the difference from the family in one line, for example "White Lady = sour with gin and triple sec as the sweetener".
6. Learn only that line plus the amounts. The family gives you the rest.

### Turn a family into a new drink on request

1. Ask what the guest liked: base spirit, long or short, sweet or sharp.
2. Pick the family that matches "long or short" (sour or Collins; stirred or highball).
3. Swap the base spirit inside the family pattern and keep the proportions.
4. Taste a drop on a straw (a clean straw each time, never back into the drink) and adjust sweet or sour by 5 ml at most.
5. Tell the guest what it is: "a gin sour, our Whiskey Sour spec with gin". Do not give it a classic's name if it is not that classic.

## Hands-on

### What you need

- The printable sort cards in [labs/module-06/family-sort-cards.md](../../labs/module-06/family-sort-cards.md) and a pen (or a notes app).
- A shaker or sealed jar, a jigger, a strainer, a bar spoon, one stemmed glass and one tall glass, ice.
- 3 lemons, 1:1 syrup ([lesson 03.4](../module-03/lesson-04.md)), cold strong black tea or cold water, soda water.
- A phone timer, a kitchen thermometer if you have one, your [practice log](../../templates/practice-log.md).
- Low-cost alternative: a jar with a tea strainer; any tall glass; a bottle of soda water you open fresh.

### Time

50 minutes: 20 for the card sort, 25 for the three serves, 5 to record.

### Steps

1. **Card sort (20 minutes).** The lab file lists 24 IBA drinks by ingredients only. For each, write the family and the method. Time yourself. Then check against [family-sort-cards-solution.md](../../labs/module-06/family-sort-cards-solution.md) and count correct families.
2. Juice the lemons and strain the juice.
3. **The base.** Make three identical bases of 45 ml cold tea (it stands in for the spirit), 25 ml lemon and 20 ml 1:1 syrup.
4. **Sour.** Shake base 1 hard with ice for 12 seconds and strain into the chilled stemmed glass. No ice in the glass.
5. **Collins.** Fill the tall glass with ice. Pour base 2 straight in, add 60 ml soda, give one gentle lift with the spoon.
6. **Fizz.** Shake base 3 hard with ice for 12 seconds, strain into a clean tall glass with no ice, add about 30 ml soda.
7. Taste each one (it is alcohol-free) and, if you can, measure the temperature right away and again after 5 minutes. Note taste, fizz and temperature in the log.
8. Optional: run the simulation and sort a round of drinks by family.

[Simulation: Recipe drill — cocktail families](../../simulations/recipe-drill/index.html?preset=families)

### What you should see

On the card sort, most first attempts get 18–21 of 24 right. The usual misses are drinks between families: the Americano (a long, low-alcohol aperitif with a splash of soda), the Mojito and the Caipirinha (sours in structure, built in the glass), and the French 75 (a sour topped with sparkling wine). The solution lists accepted alternatives for these.

In the three serves, the sour tastes the most intense and the sharpest. The Collins tastes lighter and stays cold longest, because the glass is full of ice. The fizz starts nearly as cold as the sour, has a fine, lively fizz from the soda meeting a shaken base, and warms within a few minutes because there is no ice in the glass.

### What this practice cannot teach

Tea and lemon show how the serve changes texture and temperature, but not how the alcohol in a real spirit changes sweetness and body in each serve; that needs small, optional tastings of real drinks.

## Common mistakes

- **Learning every drink from zero.** Slow and easy to forget. Fix: write the family first on every new card, then only the differences.
- **Sorting by base spirit.** "Gin drinks" is not a family: a Martini and a Tom Collins have nothing else in common. Fix: sort by structure and method.
- **Calling every long sour a Collins.** A fizz has no ice and less soda. Fix: ask "ice in the glass?" before you name it.
- **Treating the sour range as a rule.** Some sours sit outside it (the IBA Last Word is four equal parts of 22.5 ml). Fix: use the range to spot typos, then follow the spec.
- **Inventing a drink and giving it a classic's name.** The guest may order it again somewhere else and get something different. Fix: name the family and the change.

## Key takeaways

- A family is a shared structure: ingredient types, proportions, method and glass.
- The course uses ten families: sour, Collins and fizz, highball, old fashioned, martini and Manhattan, spritz and sparkling, tiki and punch, savory, coffee, other.
- Sort a new drink with four questions: coffee? savory? citrus (and what tops it)? or no citrus (and what is the long part)?
- IBA sours: base 40–60 ml, citrus 15–30 ml, sweetener close to the citrus (more when it is a liqueur).
- Sour, Collins and fizz share a base and differ in serve: no top, soda over ice, a splash of soda with no ice.
