# 06.8 · Build a twelve-cocktail menu

This is the module's practical piece. You will put together a menu of twelve cocktails for a bar you describe yourself, using drinks from Modules 5 and 6. A good menu is not twelve favourites: it gives guests a real choice of styles and strengths, it can be made fast from one station with a short prep list, it has honest alcohol-free options, and every drink has a spec someone else could follow. You will record everything in a spreadsheet, check it with a rubric, and leave the cost and price columns empty for now; in Module 10 you will cost and price this same menu.

## Why it matters

Bar managers often ask candidates, or new staff, "What would you put on our menu?" A list of drinks shows taste. A menu with families, methods, prep, allergens and alcohol-free options shows that you understand how a bar actually works on a busy night, and that is what gets people trusted with the menu later.

## Core ideas

### What makes a balanced menu

| Check | Why | Course target (professional judgment) |
|---|---|---|
| Families | guests choose by style: short and sour, long and fizzy, stirred and strong | at least 6 of the 10 families ([lesson 06.1](lesson-01.md)) |
| Methods | too many shaken, foamed or muddled drinks slow the bar in a rush | at least 3 methods; no more than half the menu shaken |
| Strength | not everyone wants a stirred drink that is almost all spirit | at least 3 lighter drinks (long or low-alcohol) |
| Base spirits | variety, and less dependence on one bottle | at least 4 different bases |
| Shared ingredients | fewer prep items, less waste | most fresh items used in 2 or more drinks |
| Glassware | each new glass type is more storage and washing | 4 glass types or fewer |
| Alcohol-free | guests who do not drink deserve a real choice ([lesson 06.6](lesson-06.md)) | at least 2, beyond the 12 |
| Allergens | the bartender must know them before the guest asks | every drink has an allergen note |
| Local story | a reason for this bar, in this place | at least 1 local product or serve, with a source ([lesson 06.7](lesson-07.md)) |

### Cross-utilisation: one prep, many drinks

**Cross-utilisation** means choosing drinks that share ingredients. Fresh lemon juice serves the White Lady, Tom Collins, French 75, Penicillin and Bloody Mary; lime serves the Daiquiri, Paloma, Mai Tai and Cuba Libre; mint serves the Mai Tai garnish and the Hugo Spritz. Every additional fresh item (basil, ginger, grapefruit) is another thing to prep, store and waste if it does not sell. That does not mean never use one: use it on purpose, and ideally twice.

### Spec source and the house spec

Each drink on your menu needs a written spec. Use the IBA spec from the lesson card, or a named variation, and write which one in the spreadsheet. If you change a spec (for example 50 ml instead of 60 ml base, to fit your glass or price), that is your **house spec**: write it down and give it the same name only if the drink is still recognisably that drink ([lesson 06.6](lesson-06.md)).

### Costing placeholders

The spreadsheet has two empty columns: cost per serving (ILS) and menu price (ILS). Leave them empty. In Module 10 you will learn to calculate cost per serving from bottle prices, pour cost, margin and VAT-inclusive prices, and fill them in with the costing calculator for this same menu. Writing exact ml now is what makes that possible later.

## Step by step

### Build the menu

1. **Describe the bar** in three lines: type (cocktail bar, pub, restaurant bar, hotel lobby), city, typical guest and busiest night.
2. **Pick 12 drinks** from the cards in [lessons 05.2–05.7](../module-05/lesson-02.md) and [06.2–06.7](lesson-02.md). Start with one per family you want, then fill.
3. **Fill one row per drink** in the spreadsheet: family, method, glass, ice, garnish, base spirit, spec source and lesson, strength (light, medium, strong), allergens, shared ingredients, alcohol-free version.
4. **Check the balance** against the table above. Swap drinks that break it (for example a third egg-white sour, or a fifth glass type).
5. **Add two or more alcohol-free drinks** with their specs (from the cards' alcohol-free analogues or your own from [lesson 06.6](lesson-06.md)).
6. **Write the prep list**: every fresh item, how much for 20 of each drink (use the scaling from [lesson 03.1](../module-03/lesson-01.md)), and which drinks use it.
7. **Order the menu** for guests: group by style (for example "Fresh and sour", "Long and fizzy", "Stirred and strong", "No alcohol"), lightest first within each group (professional judgment).
8. **Score it** with the rubric in the menu brief. Fix anything under the target.

### Test-run part of the menu

1. Choose four drinks with four different methods.
2. Make them as home-practice versions in one timed sequence, as if they were one ticket ([lesson 05.8](../module-05/lesson-08.md)).
3. Note the slowest drink and why; decide whether the menu needs a change (prep it ahead, simplify the garnish, or swap the drink).

### Time

90 minutes: 10 for the bar description, 40 for the twelve rows and the alcohol-free drinks, 15 for the prep list and menu order, 20 for the four-drink test run, 5 to score.

### Steps

1. Write the bar description at the top of the menu brief.
2. Fill the 12 rows of `menu-builder.csv`. Leave `cost_per_serving_ils` and `menu_price_ils` empty.
3. Add your alcohol-free drinks as rows 13 and 14 (and more if you like), each with a full recipe card.
4. Count families, methods, strengths, bases and glass types. Fix anything below the targets.
5. Write the prep list and the guest-facing menu order in the brief.
6. Test-run four drinks with four methods as one ticket; record the total time and the slowest drink.
7. Score the menu with the rubric. Compare it with the example in [twelve-cocktail-menu-solution.md](../../labs/module-06/twelve-cocktail-menu-solution.md) only after you have scored your own.
8. Run a recall round on your twelve drinks in the simulation.

### What you should see

A first draft usually has too many sours and too many shaken drinks, because sours are the biggest family and the most fun to make. After the balance check, a typical menu has 3–4 sours, 2–3 long drinks, 1–2 stirred, 1–2 sparkling, and one each of tiki, savory or coffee. The prep list shrinks when you swap a drink that needs a single-use ingredient. In the test run, the shaken and muddled drinks are the slowest; a four-drink ticket made from home-practice versions typically takes 3–5 minutes at this stage (professional judgment).

### What this practice cannot teach

A menu is only proven by guests: what they order, what comes back, what runs out. You learn that from a bar's sales and from working the menu during a real rush.

## Common mistakes

- **Twelve favourites, six of them sours.** Fix: count families before you finish.
- **Too many shaken, foamed or muddled drinks.** The bar slows down. Fix: no more than half shaken; at most one egg-white drink.
- **A single-use garnish or ingredient.** Waste and extra prep. Fix: use it twice or swap the drink.
- **No spec source.** Nobody can make it the same way tomorrow. Fix: IBA, a named variation, or a written house spec.
- **"Mocktails" as an afterthought.** Fix: build alcohol-free drinks with the same care and a body ingredient.
- **Allergens unknown.** Fix: an allergen note in every row.

## Key takeaways

- A balanced menu spreads across families, methods, strengths and bases.
- Shared ingredients keep prep short and waste low.
- Every drink needs a written spec with its source, and an allergen note.
- At least two real alcohol-free drinks belong on every menu.
- Exact ml now make costing and pricing possible in Module 10.
