# 04.7 · Garnishes: preparation and presentation

A garnish is the last thing you add and the first thing the guest notices. Some garnishes change the drink: the oils sprayed from a lemon twist over a Martini are part of its flavour, and a salt rim changes every sip of a Margarita. Others signal what the drink is or simply make it look finished. In lesson 01.4 you learned to cut wheels, wedges and peels safely. This lesson turns those cuts into service garnishes: expressing a twist correctly, rimming a glass, preparing mint, placing everything the same way every time, and keeping garnish safe to eat. In the hands-on part you prepare a garnish tray against the clock and practise expressing oils.

## Why it matters

Two bartenders make the same Negroni. One drops in a pale, thick half-moon of orange cut an hour earlier, touched with bare fingers and placed wherever it lands. The other cuts a fresh, thin orange peel, holds it skin side down over the glass, pinches it so a fine mist of oil hits the surface, and lays it neatly on the same side as on the menu photo. The second drink smells brighter before the guest even sips it, and it looks like a bar that cares. Garnish also costs money and time: a tray cut badly before service runs out at the worst moment, or goes in the bin at closing. Consistent, hygienic garnish is part of the spec, not decoration.

## Core ideas

### What a garnish does

A **garnish** (קישוט, *kishut*) is anything added to the finished drink. It can do one or more of four jobs:

| Job | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Aroma** | Expressed citrus twist, mint sprig, grated nutmeg, bitters dashed on foam | The nose does much of the tasting (lesson [03.3](../module-03/lesson-03.md)); the first smell sets expectations |
| **Flavour** | Salt or sugar rim, olive, citrus wedge to squeeze | Changes every sip; the guest should be able to avoid it if they wish |
| **Look and identity** | Three coffee beans on an Espresso Martini (IBA), a cherry in a Manhattan (IBA) | Tells guests and colleagues which drink it is |
| **Information** | A lime wedge on a highball tells a waiter which glass is the gin and tonic (house practice, professional judgment) | Only if the whole team uses the same code |

The IBA spec names the garnish for most drinks (for example the Margarita: "half salt rim", optional; the Negroni: half orange slice). Follow the spec or the house standard; do not invent a garnish on a busy night.

### The twist and expressing oils

A **twist** or **zest** (קליפה, *klipa*, "peel") is a strip of citrus skin. Its oil glands hold the strongest aroma in the fruit. **Expressing** means squeezing the peel so the oil sprays out over the drink. Difford's Guide describes the method:

- Use a firm, clean, unwaxed fruit (lemon, orange, grapefruit or lime) and cut a strip about 12 mm wide (this course accepts 1.5–2 cm for a wider orange peel) with a knife or Y-peeler, with only a little pith.
- Hold it **coloured side down**, over the drink, about 5–10 cm above the surface.
- Pinch it between thumbs and forefingers and twist the ends in opposite directions so the oils spray onto the surface. In good light you see a fine mist and tiny oil droplets on the drink.
- Then **drop it in, mount it** on the rim through a small cut, or **discard** it, as the spec or house style says.

Some bartenders rub the peel around the rim; Difford's notes that others think this only makes the rim taste bitter. Follow the house style.

> [!NOTE]
> Some bars flame an orange twist (squeezing the oils through a lit match). This course does not teach it: it is a fire trick, and fire behind a bar full of spirits causes serious burns. Garnish guidance from Difford's Guide and the IBA pages was checked 2026-10-17.

### Wheels, wedges, half-slices and rims

The cuts come from lesson [01.4](../module-01/lesson-04.md). For service, the standards are:

- **Wheel:** 4–5 mm thick, a slit from centre to edge if it sits on the rim.
- **Half-slice (half-moon):** a wheel cut in half; often dropped into the drink (IBA Negroni, Americano).
- **Wedge:** one-eighth of a lime, seeds removed, a slit across the flesh if it sits on the rim. Guests squeeze wedges, so they are a flavour garnish.
- **Salt or sugar rim:** usually a **half rim** on the **outside** of the glass only, so the guest can choose and no salt falls into the drink. Run a lime wedge round the outside edge of half the glass, then roll that edge in salt on a small plate. Tap off the excess.

### Herbs and other garnishes

- **Mint sprig:** pick a bushy top with fresh leaves. Clap it once between your palms just before placing it: this bruises the surface and releases aroma (professional judgment). Place it next to the straw, so the guest smells it with every sip.
- **Cherries and olives:** taken from their jar with a pick or tongs, never fingers. Drain them so the liquid does not drip into the drink.
- **Grated spice:** a few passes of nutmeg or cinnamon on a fine grater over foam or cream.
- **Dashes on foam:** the IBA Pisco Sour finishes with a few drops of bitters on its egg-white foam, as an aromatic garnish.

### Consistency and placement

Every drink of the same name should look the same: the same cut, size and position. Pick a fixed spot (for example, twist at "3 o'clock" from where the guest holds the glass, wedge on the rim at the far side) and use it every time. Garnish should never block drinking, hide a chip in the glass, or hang so far outside the glass that it touches the bar.

### Garnish is food

Garnish goes into or onto something the guest puts in their mouth, so it follows food-safety rules:

- Wash your hands and the fruit before prep (lesson [01.7](../module-01/lesson-07.md)).
- Food codes, including the U.S. FDA Food Code, prohibit **bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat food**: place garnish with tongs or picks, or with gloves if the bar uses them.
- Keep cut garnish **covered and chilled**. Difford's Guide stores pre-cut zests refrigerated in a sealed box with moist paper towel and notes they last only a little over a day; this course's working rule is to cut citrus garnish for the shift and discard what is left at closing unless the bar's written policy says otherwise (professional judgment; Israeli Ministry of Health requirements are covered in Module 9).
- Never reuse a garnish that came back on a used glass.

## Step by step

### Cut and express a twist to order

1. Take a washed lemon or orange in your non-dominant hand, fingers on the sides, away from the blade's path.
2. With a Y-peeler, draw a strip 1.5–2 cm wide and 5–6 cm long from top to bottom, pressing lightly.
3. Check the back: a thin, even layer of white. Trim thick pith with the paring knife on the board, blade moving away from your fingers.
4. Optional: trim the edges straight for a neat rectangle.
5. Hold the peel coloured side down, 5–10 cm above the finished drink.
6. Pinch and twist the ends in opposite directions. Look for the oil mist on the surface.
7. Drop it in, mount it or discard it, as the spec says. Place it in the same position every time.

### Half salt rim

1. Pour a thin layer of fine salt onto a small plate.
2. Hold the empty glass upside down. Run a lime wedge round the outside edge of half the rim only.
3. Roll that outer edge through the salt, keeping the glass tilted so salt does not get inside.
4. Tap the glass gently to remove loose salt. Rim the glass before you make the drink.

### Set a garnish tray for service

1. Wash your hands, then the fruit.
2. Set the board on a damp cloth (lesson 01.4).
3. Cut in this order: wheels, half-slices, wedges, then peels (peels last, because they dry fastest).
4. Pick mint tops and stand them in a cup of cold water.
5. Put each garnish in its own covered compartment, with tongs or picks, and label the tray with the time.
6. Keep the tray cold (on ice or in the fridge) and covered between uses.

## Hands-on

> [!CAUTION]
> This practice uses a paring knife and a peeler. Board on a damp cloth, claw grip, cut away from your fingers, never catch a falling knife (lesson 01.4). Wash your hands before you start and use tongs or picks to place every garnish.

### What you need

- 2 lemons, 2 oranges, 3 limes, a small bunch of mint, fine salt, a few cocktail cherries or olives if you have them.
- Paring knife, Y-peeler, cutting board, damp cloth, a small plate, tongs or cocktail picks, a container with a lid (or a muffin tin covered with cling film) for the tray.
- Six glasses of cold water to express twists over (a dark glass or a dark plate under the glass makes the oil easier to see), a rocks glass for the salt rim.
- A ruler, a phone stopwatch, a phone to film, the drill log ([labs/module-04/technique-drill-log.csv](../../labs/module-04/technique-drill-log.csv)) and the garnish checklist ([labs/module-04/garnish-tray-checklist.md](../../labs/module-04/garnish-tray-checklist.md)).
- Low-cost alternative: any vegetable peeler; toothpicks instead of cocktail picks.

### Time

50 minutes: 5 to wash and set up, 25 for the tray and twists, 10 for the rim, filming and placement, 10 to clean up and log.

### Steps

1. Wash hands and fruit. Set the board on the damp cloth.
2. **Tray against the clock.** Start the stopwatch and cut: 6 lemon wheels, 4 orange half-slices, 8 lime wedges, 4 mint sprigs (in water). Stop the clock. Target: under 6 minutes.
3. **Ten twists.** For each twist: start the stopwatch, cut an orange or lemon peel, trim if needed, express it over a glass of water, place it on the rim or drop it in, stop the clock. Target: 20 seconds or less, width 1.5–2 cm (measure with the ruler).
4. For each twist, look at the water surface against the dark background: can you see the oil sheen? Smell the glass: is the citrus clear?
5. Make one half salt rim on the rocks glass. Check that no salt is inside the glass.
6. Film twists 9 and 10: peel held coloured side down? Above the glass? Placed in the same spot?
7. Go through the garnish checklist and tick each item.
8. Cover the tray, label it with the time and refrigerate it (use it for practice within the day). Enter the twists in the drill log (movement "garnish") and log the tray time and one fix.

### What you should see

A good twist leaves a visible film of tiny oil droplets on the water and a strong citrus smell; if you see nothing, the peel was too thin (no oil glands), too old, or held too far away. First twists usually take 30–40 seconds including the cut, falling to about 20 by the tenth (professional judgment). Wedges and wheels should look alike when lined up; if they do not, slow down and use longer, smoother strokes. A clean half rim has salt only on the outside and only on one half.

### What this practice cannot teach

A real drink's aroma changes with its ingredients, and a real service tray must last a whole shift in a warm, busy bar; both you will learn on the job, so keep the safe habits from this session.

- [ ] I cut a full garnish tray and timed it.
- [ ] I cut and expressed ten twists, timing each and checking the oil on the water.

## Common mistakes

- **Expressing with the coloured side up.** The oil sprays away from the drink. Fix: coloured side down, over the glass.
- **Thick, pithy twists.** Bitter and they do not bend to release oil. Fix: light pressure with the peeler; trim the pith.
- **Garnish by hand.** It breaks food-safety rules and guests notice. Fix: tongs or picks every time.
- **Salt inside the glass.** Every sip becomes salty and the drink is unbalanced. Fix: rim the outside edge of half the glass only.
- **Random placement.** Drinks of the same name look different. Fix: one fixed position per garnish, the same on every drink.
- **Cutting the whole tray hours early, uncovered.** Peels dry out and lose their oil. Fix: cut for the shift, peels last, covered and cold.

## Key takeaways

- A garnish adds aroma, flavour, look or information; follow the spec or house standard.
- Express twists coloured side down over the drink, then drop, mount or discard as specified.
- Rim only the outside edge, usually half the glass.
- Same cut, same size, same position on every drink of the same name.
- Garnish is food: washed hands and fruit, tongs or picks, covered and chilled, discarded at closing unless the bar's policy says otherwise.
