# 04.3 · Stirring

Stirring is how bartenders make spirit-forward drinks such as the Martini, the Manhattan and many Negronis: cold, silky and perfectly clear. It looks like the easiest movement behind the bar, but a good stir is a trained skill. The spoon has to glide around the inside of the glass without splashing, chipping the ice or shaking the drink full of bubbles, and it has to do this for the same time on every drink. This lesson teaches the grip, the movement, the timing and the strain, and in the hands-on part you stir ten timed reps with water and check how consistent you are on the scale and on film.

## Why it matters

A guest at the bar orders a Manhattan and watches you make it. You stir for eight seconds, the spoon rattling the ice, then pour. The drink is warm and harsh, with little shards of ice floating on top, and the guest leaves half of it. The next bartender stirs the same drink for 25 quiet seconds and strains it cleanly into a frozen coupe; it is cold, smooth and clear. Same recipe, same bottle. In bars that serve many stirred drinks, the stir is also something guests notice: it is done in front of them, and a smooth, calm stir looks professional (professional judgment).

## Core ideas

### What stirring does, and does not do

**Stirring** (ערבוב, *irbuv*) moves the drink and the ice around together in a mixing glass, so the drink chills and picks up meltwater without being beaten. Compared with shaking (lesson 04.1):

- **No aeration.** No air is whipped in, so the drink stays clear and has a heavier, silkier feel. Arnold looked at stirred and shaken drinks under a microscope: the stirred ones showed nothing, the shaken ones were full of tiny bubbles.
- **Less dilution, less cold.** Stirring is slow. Arnold found it can take over two minutes of stirring to do what 15 seconds of shaking does, so stirred drinks are always served warmer and stronger than shaken ones. That suits drinks made only of spirits, vermouths, liqueurs and bitters: they stay rich and smooth.

That is why you stir drinks with **no citrus, no egg, no cream and no juice**, and almost always serve them either "up" (in a chilled stemmed glass without ice) or on a large fresh cube.

### Time and dilution

Because a stirred drink never reaches the full chill a shake gives, the time you stir controls its dilution. Here is the course's educational model for a typical spirit-forward drink: 60 ml of a 40% spirit and 30 ml of a 16% vermouth, 90 ml at about 32% ABV.

| Stir time | Water added (model) | Volume in the glass | ABV after |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 s | about 25% | about 112 ml | about 25.5% |
| 20 s | about 37% | about 123 ml | about 23% |
| 30 s | about 44% | about 130 ml | about 22% |
| 45 s | about 48% | about 133 ml | about 21.5% |
| 60 s | about 50% | about 135 ml | about 21% |

Two things stand out. First, **most of the change happens in the first 20–30 seconds**: by 30 seconds the drink has about 44% of the roughly 51% it would reach if you stirred for minutes, and each extra 15 seconds adds only a few percent. Second, a 10-second stir leaves the drink noticeably warmer and stronger. This course uses **20–30 seconds** as the standard on home-freezer cubes (it is the standard in the technique rubric). Difford's Guide stirs for 30–45 seconds, about 50 turns, and Arnold suggests about 45 seconds; bars with very cold, hard ice often stir longer because their ice melts more slowly. Whatever time your bar uses, the point is to use **the same time on every drink**.

> Dilution figures are from this course's educational model (`dilution-lab`, based on Arnold's measurements), checked 2026-10-17. Your real numbers depend on your ice and glass; lesson 03.5 showed how to measure them.

### The tools

- **Mixing glass**: a thick glass or steel vessel of about 500–700 ml (lesson [01.3](../module-01/lesson-03.md)). The shaker's large tin works too and chills faster, because steel takes less heat from the drink than thick glass.
- **Bar spoon** (כף בר, *kaf bar*): a long spoon with a twisted shaft that spins smoothly in your fingers. A 30–40 cm spoon suits a tall mixing glass.
- **Julep strainer**: a perforated spoon-shaped strainer that sits inside the mouth of the mixing glass. Difford's notes it catches small ice fragments less well than a Hawthorne but is more pleasant to use with a mixing glass. A **Hawthorne** works too.
- **Chilled serving glass**: a stirred drink has no ice in the glass to keep it cold, so the glass must be cold first. Keep coupes in the freezer or fridge, or fill the glass with ice and water while you stir and empty it before straining.

### The grip and the movement

Difford's Guide describes the classic grip: hold the shaft between your thumb and first two fingers, with the stem running between your middle and ring fingers, like holding a pen loosely near its top third. The **back of the spoon's bowl faces the glass wall** and stays against it. Your **fingers move the spoon, not your wrist or arm**: the index and middle fingers pull it towards you and the ring finger pushes it away, so the bowl glides round the inside of the glass and the ice turns as one block with the liquid.

Signs that the stir is right:

- **Quiet.** A good stir makes almost no noise. Rattling and clinking mean the spoon is hitting the ice, which chips it into tiny pieces that melt fast and over-dilute.
- **The ice turns as a block.** The cubes rotate together with the liquid instead of jumping around.
- **No splashing,** and the liquid level stays flat, without a deep whirlpool.
- **The outside of the mixing glass frosts** or beads with water as the drink chills.

### Straining a stirred drink

Fit the julep strainer into the mouth of the mixing glass, resting on the ice, and hold it in place with your index finger while your other fingers wrap round the glass. Pour in one smooth movement from close to the serving glass, finishing with a small upward flick so the last drop does not run down the outside. The ice in the mixing glass is never served: if the drink goes on the rocks, it goes onto **fresh** ice in the serving glass (Difford's Guide makes the same point).

## Step by step

### Stir a drink served up

1. Chill the serving glass: from the freezer, or filled with ice and water while you work.
2. Measure the ingredients into the mixing glass, cheapest first (bitters, then vermouth, then spirit), so a mistake wastes less.
3. Fill the mixing glass about two-thirds with fresh, solid cubes. Difford's notes that ingredients can sit on the ice for up to a minute without much effect, so you do not need to rush between steps 3 and 4.
4. Slide the spoon down the inside of the glass, back of the bowl against the wall, until it almost touches the bottom.
5. Hold the mixing glass at its base with your free hand to steady it (your hand on the base warms the drink less than a hand around the middle).
6. Stir with your fingers for 20–30 seconds by the stopwatch, smoothly and quietly. Count turns if you prefer: note how many turns your 25 seconds is and use that count from now on.
7. Lift the spoon out along the wall, without dragging ice up.
8. Empty the ice water from the serving glass.
9. Fit the julep strainer, strain the drink in one smooth pour, finish with a small flick.
10. Garnish (lesson 04.7), serve, then dump the used ice and rinse the mixing glass.

### Stir a drink served on the rocks

Do steps 2–7 above, then fill the serving glass with fresh ice (or put in one large cube), and strain over it. Never pour the mixing-glass ice into the drink.

### Find your stir count

1. Put 90 ml water and ice in the mixing glass.
2. Start the stopwatch and count each full turn of the spoon.
3. Stop at 25 seconds. Repeat three times and average. Most people make 1.5–2.5 turns per second, about 40–60 turns in 25 seconds (professional judgment).

## Hands-on

> [!CAUTION]
> A thin glass can crack when you stir hard against it, especially when it is very cold. Use a thick mixing glass, a steel tin or a sturdy jar, and stop at once if you hear a crack.

### What you need

- A mixing glass (or a steel tin, a sturdy pint glass or a large jar), a bar spoon (or a long teaspoon), a julep or Hawthorne strainer (or a tea strainer).
- A stemmed glass to strain into (a small wine glass is fine), and about 1.5 kg of ice cubes from the freezer.
- 1 l cold water, a jigger, a kitchen scale with 1 g steps, a phone stopwatch, a phone to film.
- The drill log: [labs/module-04/technique-drill-log.csv](../../labs/module-04/technique-drill-log.csv) and your practice log ([template](../../templates/practice-log.md)).
- Low-cost alternative: a long chopstick works for the movement if you have no bar spoon, but it slips; a bar spoon costs only a few shekels (professional judgment).

### Time

45 minutes: 5 to set up, 25 for ten stirs, 15 to watch the film, clean up and log.

### Steps

1. Find your stir count as above.
2. **Rep 1–10.** For each rep: tare the empty mixing glass, add 90 ml water by jigger and write the weight; add ice; stir 25 seconds by the stopwatch (or your count); strain into the tared serving glass; write the strained weight.
3. Work out the weight gain for each rep: (strained − water) ÷ water × 100. Some liquid stays on the ice, so the gain you measure is a little lower than the real dilution; what matters is that every rep is measured the same way.
4. Look at the ten gains. Your **spread** is the highest minus the lowest. Target: 5 percentage points or less.
5. Film reps 9 and 10 from the side at bar-top height, with the sound on.
6. Watch the film. Is the spoon's back on the glass? Is the wrist still? Does the ice rattle? Is the strain smooth with no drips down the glass?
7. Run the stir preset of the dilution simulation: predict the dilution of a 90 ml, 32% ABV drink at 10, 20 and 30 seconds, then compare with the model.
8. Enter your reps in the drill log (movement "stir"), and log your spread and one fix in your practice log.

### What you should see

With water, the gain is much smaller than for a real drink, because water cannot cool below 0 °C (lesson 03.5 explains why): the course model predicts about 18% at 25 seconds, and you will usually weigh a few points less, often 12–16%, because some water stays on the ice. Beginners' first few stirs vary a lot, often 6–10 percentage points between reps, and tighten to within 3–5 points by the last reps as the movement becomes steady (professional judgment). A noisy stir usually gives the highest gains: chipped ice melts faster. In the film, a good stir looks almost lazy: the spoon glides, the ice turns in one block, and nothing above the wrist moves.

### What this practice cannot teach

Water shows your consistency but not whether a real Manhattan or Martini is cold and diluted enough to taste right; that judgment needs real ingredients (optional, small tastings) in Module 5.

## Common mistakes

- **Stirring with the wrist or arm.** The spoon hits the ice and the drink sloshes. Fix: still wrist, fingers only, back of the spoon against the glass.
- **Too short a stir.** The drink is warm and harsh. Fix: time it; use your count until 20–30 seconds feels natural.
- **Rattling the ice.** Chipped ice over-dilutes and leaves shards in the drink. Fix: slower, smoother; let the ice turn as one block.
- **A warm serving glass.** A stirred drink served up warms in a minute. Fix: chill the glass before you start.
- **Pouring the mixing-glass ice into the drink.** It is broken, wet and over-dilutes. Fix: strain onto fresh ice.
- **Shaking a stirred drink "to be quick".** It turns cloudy and thinner. Fix: stir unless the guest asks for it shaken.

## Key takeaways

- Stir drinks made only of spirits, wines, liqueurs, bitters and syrup: stirring chills and dilutes without adding air.
- Grip the spoon in the fingers, back of the bowl on the glass, wrist still; a good stir is quiet.
- In the course model a 32% drink gains about 37% water at 20 s and about 44% at 30 s; each extra 15 s after that adds only a few percent.
- Use the same stir time every time; this course's standard is 20–30 seconds on home-freezer ice.
- Chill the serving glass, strain with a julep or Hawthorne, and never serve the mixing-glass ice.
