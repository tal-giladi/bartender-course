# 04.4 · Shaking, dry shakes and egg white

Shaking is the movement people picture when they think of a bartender, and it is the method for every drink with citrus, egg, cream or coffee. A good shake is short, hard and safe: the tins are sealed so nothing leaks, the ice travels the full length of the shaker, and the drink is strained out ten to fifteen seconds later, cold, diluted and lively. This lesson teaches the Boston shaker from loading to opening, explains what the shake does in numbers, and covers the special shakes for foamy drinks made with egg white or its plant-based stand-in, aquafaba. In the hands-on part you shake ten timed reps with water and run a foam test.

## Why it matters

It is your second week at a cocktail bar and you are shaking a Whiskey Sour in front of a table of guests. The tins were not sealed properly; halfway through the shake they come apart and the drink sprays across the bar and onto a guest's shirt. On the next order you over-correct, slam the tins together, and cannot open them; you bang the shaker on the counter while the guest waits. Both problems come from the same missing habit: a correct seal and a correct break. Shaking well is also tiring, and bartenders who shake from the arm instead of the whole body get sore shoulders by the end of a long shift (professional judgment). A clean, safe, repeatable shake is one of the first things a bar manager watches at a trial shift.

## Core ideas

### What shaking does

**Shaking** (ניעור, *ni'ur*) throws the drink and the ice from one end of a sealed shaker to the other. It does three things fast:

- **Chills and dilutes.** Dave Arnold found that shaking brings a drink to a near-steady state in about 15 seconds; Difford's Guide gives about 12 seconds to reach roughly −5 to −8 °C. Both found that the type of ice and the style of shake make little difference once you shake long enough.
- **Mixes thoroughly.** Citrus, syrup, cream and egg combine with the spirit into one even liquid.
- **Aerates.** Tiny bubbles give shaken drinks a light, frothy texture. Arnold's tasters could tell shaken from stirred drinks blind; the shaken texture lasted at least 5 minutes but was gone by about 8. That is one reason shaken drinks should be served at once.

In the course's educational model, a sour that starts at about 25% ABV gains this much water:

| Shake time | Water added (model) | 95 ml of sour becomes |
|---|---|---|
| 5 s | about 47% | about 140 ml |
| 10 s | about 61% | about 153 ml |
| 12 s | about 63% | about 155 ml |
| 15 s | about 65% | about 157 ml |

The curve flattens after 10–12 seconds: a 30-second shake is not twice as cold or twice as diluted as a 15-second one. A 5-second shake, on the other hand, leaves the drink warm and strong. The course standard is **10–15 seconds of hard shaking**.

### The Boston shaker

A **Boston shaker** is two parts: a large tin and a smaller tin (or a mixing glass) that fits inside it at an angle (lesson [01.3](../module-01/lesson-03.md)). Tin-on-tin is the usual choice in busy bars: it is light, it does not break, and steel chills faster. Difford's Guide advises against glass Boston halves because glass absorbs more heat and can break. A **cobbler shaker** (tin, built-in strainer cap and lid) is common at home; its cap can stick when cold, so open it with a twist, never with a knife.

**Sealing.** Put the small tin into the large one at a slight angle and give the top one firm tap with the heel of your hand. The cold contracts the air inside and the seal tightens as you shake. Check it by giving the small tin a gentle pull before you start: it should not move.

**Breaking the seal.** Difford's Guide recommends pushing the small tin sideways with your thumb at the point where the rims meet, and advises against banging the shaker on the bar. A common alternative is a sharp tap with the heel of your hand on the large tin, just where the two tins meet (professional judgment). If it will not open, warm the joint for a few seconds with your hand and try again.

### How to hold and move

- **Two hands, one on each tin.** One hand caps the small tin, the other holds the base of the large tin. Never shake with one hand on a Boston shaker.
- **Small tin towards you.** Hold the shaker with the small tin closer to your body and the large tin pointing away, roughly over your shoulder, and shake away from guests, so that if the seal fails, the drink lands behind the bar (professional judgment).
- **The ice must travel.** Move the shaker in a long straight line, back and forth, so the ice slams from one end to the other. You should hear a hard, clean rattle. A short, soft movement just swirls the ice.
- **Use your body, not just your arms.** Keep your back straight, shoulders relaxed, and let the movement come from the shoulders and hips. This saves your shoulders over a long shift.

Difford's Guide notes that the famous Japanese "hard shake" makes no measurable difference to temperature or dilution. Style is personal; time and a full ice load are what count.

### Ice in the shaker

Fill the **large** tin about two-thirds with fresh, solid cubes, then pour the drink over the ice, or bring the ice tin over the small tin and seal (Difford's Guide prefers filling the large tin, which avoids splashing and holds more ice). Do not shake with crushed ice unless the spec says so: it dilutes far more and less predictably. A **whip shake** is a short shake with a few pieces of crushed ice or one small cube, used for drinks that will be poured over crushed ice anyway; you will meet it in Module 6.

### Dry shake and reverse dry shake

Egg white (or aquafaba, see below) gives a drink a thick, white **foam** (קצף, *ketzef*) on top. Proteins in the white unfold when beaten and trap air; they foam better when they are not ice-cold. That is why bartenders use special shakes:

| Shake | Steps | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Wet shake only** | Shake everything with ice, strain | A thin foam; the IBA Whiskey Sour simply says to shake a little harder when using egg white |
| **Dry shake** | Shake everything **without** ice first, then add ice and shake again, strain | A thicker foam; Difford's dates the technique to at least 1951 |
| **Reverse dry shake** | Shake **with** ice first, strain the liquid back into the small tin, discard the ice, then shake again **without** ice, fine strain | The thickest foam; Difford's prefers it, and notes it works only with a two-piece shaker |

Always **fine strain** an egg drink (lesson 04.5): the fine mesh catches bits of egg and the stringy white cord (the chalaza) and keeps the foam smooth.

> [!WARNING]
> Raw eggs can carry Salmonella bacteria, even when the shell is clean and uncracked (U.S. FDA). Symptoms include diarrhoea, fever and vomiting, and the illness can be serious for young children, older adults, pregnant women and people with weakened immune systems. In this course, practise and serve egg-white drinks only with **pasteurised egg white** (sold in cartons, heated enough to kill bacteria) or **aquafaba**. If a bar uses fresh eggs, follow its food-safety procedure exactly: refrigerated eggs, no cracked shells, separate each egg fresh for each drink, never pool eggs in a container, wash your hands after handling shells. Egg is a major allergen: always mention it to the guest (Module 9).

### Egg white and its alternatives

- **Fresh egg white.** The IBA Pisco Sour uses one whole raw egg white; the IBA Whiskey Sour and Clover Club use "a few drops". A large egg white is about 30 ml; many bars use 15–25 ml per drink (professional judgment).
- **Pasteurised egg white** from a carton behaves almost the same and is the safe default. The CDC describes pasteurised eggs as free of pathogens and ready to eat, and advises restaurants to use them and not to pool raw eggs.
- **Aquafaba** is the liquid from a can of chickpeas. It foams like egg white and is vegan. Camper English's tests at Alcademics found that the liquid from canned chickpeas gave the best amount and quality of foam. A common starting point is the same volume as the egg white it replaces (professional judgment). Chickpeas are a legume: mention aquafaba to guests with legume allergies.

## Step by step

### Shake a drink served up

1. Chill the serving glass (freezer, or ice and water while you work).
2. Measure the ingredients into the **small** tin, cheapest first (citrus and syrup before spirit), so a mistake wastes less.
3. Fill the **large** tin about two-thirds with fresh, solid cubes.
4. Pour the small tin's contents over the ice in the large tin (the safe way), or bring the ice tin over the small tin in one movement.
5. Put the small tin in at a slight angle, tap it once firmly with the heel of your hand, and check the seal with a gentle pull.
6. Hold one hand on each tin, small tin towards you, and shake hard in a long line for 10–15 seconds, away from guests.
7. Hold the shaker upright with the large tin at the bottom. Break the seal by pushing the small tin sideways with your thumb at the joint (or one sharp tap with the heel of your hand where the rims meet).
8. Put the Hawthorne strainer on the large tin, hold a fine strainer over the glass, and pour (lesson 04.5).
9. Garnish, serve at once, then rinse both tins.

### Reverse dry shake for an egg-white or aquafaba drink

1. Measure all ingredients, including the pasteurised egg white or aquafaba, into the small tin.
2. Add ice to the large tin, combine, seal and shake hard for 10–12 seconds.
3. Open, hold the Hawthorne on the large tin and strain the liquid back into the empty **small** tin. Discard the ice.
4. Seal the two tins again (without ice) and shake hard for about 10 seconds. The seal is weaker without ice; hold both tins firmly.
5. Open and fine strain into the chilled glass. Let the foam settle for a few seconds before you garnish.

For a classic dry shake, reverse the order: shake without ice first (about 10 seconds), open, add ice, shake again 10–12 seconds, then fine strain.

## Hands-on

> [!WARNING]
> Use aquafaba or pasteurised egg white only, never raw shell eggs, for this practice. Keep both refrigerated and use them within the time on the pack (aquafaba: keep it covered in the fridge and use it within a day or two; professional judgment). Shake away from people and breakable objects, and if a tin is dented or the seal will not hold, do not use it.

### What you need

- A Boston shaker (two tins) or a cobbler shaker, a Hawthorne strainer, a fine strainer (a tea strainer works), a jigger.
- 2 kg of ice cubes, cold water, a kitchen scale with 1 g steps, a phone stopwatch, a phone to film, a ruler.
- For the foam test (three drinks): 135 ml cold black tea, 75 ml lemon juice, 60 ml 1:1 sugar syrup (lesson 03.4), and 60 ml aquafaba from a can of chickpeas or pasteurised liquid egg white.
- Three identical glasses, ideally small wine glasses or coupes.
- The drill log: [labs/module-04/technique-drill-log.csv](../../labs/module-04/technique-drill-log.csv) and your practice log ([template](../../templates/practice-log.md)).
- Low-cost alternative: a clean jar with a screw lid works as a shaker for the foam test; it cannot teach sealing and breaking a Boston shaker.

### Time

60 minutes: 5 to set up, 25 for ten water shakes, 20 for the foam test, 10 to clean up and log.

### Steps

1. **Water shakes, reps 1–10.** For each rep: tare the large tin, add 90 ml water by jigger and write the weight; add ice, seal, and shake 12 seconds by the stopwatch; break the seal; strain into a tared glass and write the weight. Note any leak, any failed seal and how many seconds it took to open.
2. Work out the weight gain of each rep and your spread (highest minus lowest). Target: no leaks, every tin opened in under 3 seconds, spread of 5 percentage points or less.
3. Film reps 9 and 10 from the side. Check: two hands, small tin towards you, long straight movement, ice hitting both ends, shoulders relaxed.
4. **Foam test.** Make three identical alcohol-free sours: 45 ml tea, 25 ml lemon, 20 ml syrup, 20 ml aquafaba or pasteurised egg white.
   - Drink A: wet shake only, 12 seconds, fine strain.
   - Drink B: dry shake 10 seconds, then with ice 12 seconds, fine strain.
   - Drink C: reverse dry shake (with ice 12 seconds, strain back, without ice 10 seconds), fine strain.
5. One minute after straining, measure the foam layer in each glass with the ruler, in mm. Measure again at 5 minutes.
6. Optional: taste a small sip of each and note the texture.
7. Run the shake preset of the dilution simulation: predict the dilution of a 25% ABV sour at 5, 10 and 15 seconds, then compare.
8. Enter the water reps in the drill log (movement "shake") and log foam heights and one fix in your practice log.

### What you should see

Plain water shaken 12 seconds gains about 19% in the course model, and you will usually weigh a few points less (often 15–18%) because some water stays on the ice. A leaking seal usually shows as a wet hand in the first second: stop, dry the tins, re-seal. In the foam test, the dry-shaken and reverse-dry-shaken drinks should have a clearly thicker, finer foam than the wet-shaken one, often twice as thick or more, and the reverse dry shake is usually the tightest and longest-lasting (professional judgment; Difford's reports the same order). Aquafaba foam is often a little looser than egg-white foam and can smell faintly of chickpea until it settles.

### What this practice cannot teach

Water and an alcohol-free sour show your movement, seal and foam technique, but not how shaking changes the balance of a real cocktail, and nothing replaces shaking forty drinks in a row on a busy night; build stamina gradually and keep good posture.

## Common mistakes

- **A weak seal.** The tins come apart mid-shake. Fix: angle, one firm tap, check with a gentle pull before shaking.
- **Banging the shaker on the bar to open it.** It dents tins and frightens guests. Fix: thumb push or one tap at the joint where the rims meet.
- **Shaking too short or too gently.** The drink is warm, strong and flat. Fix: 10–15 seconds, hard, ice hitting both ends.
- **Shaking carbonated ingredients.** Pressure builds and the tins can burst open. Fix: build or top with soda after straining.
- **Raw shell eggs at home or without a safety procedure.** Risk of Salmonella. Fix: pasteurised egg white or aquafaba; fine strain every egg drink.
- **Letting the drink sit.** The aeration fades within minutes and the foam collapses. Fix: strain and serve straight away.

## Key takeaways

- Shake drinks with citrus, egg, cream or coffee: it chills, dilutes, mixes and aerates in 10–15 seconds.
- In the course model a 25% ABV sour gains about 61% water at 10 s and about 65% at 15 s; longer shaking adds little.
- Seal with an angle and one tap; open with a thumb push at the joint; shake with two hands, away from guests.
- Dry shake (no ice, then ice) or reverse dry shake (ice, then no ice) gives a thicker foam; fine strain egg drinks.
- Use pasteurised egg white or aquafaba; raw eggs can carry Salmonella, and egg and chickpea are allergens to declare.
